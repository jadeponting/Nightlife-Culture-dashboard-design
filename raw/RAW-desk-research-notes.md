# The interactive evidence instrument — foundational research design v0.1

**Nightlife Culture · 16 August 2026 · for Carly**

A proposed chapter set. Not a product, not an artefact decision, not a recommendation about
whether the NCVI should be an index. Those are research outputs. This is the research.

---

## What this is, and how it was derived

The Decision Book's chapters are not topics. Each is **one bolded question someone is forced to
answer**, and the sequence is the decision stack — Ch 1 is rungs 1–2, Part 2 is the evidence
layers, Part 5 is the practice. The chapter set was derived from *the order in which decisions
resolve*, not from a table of contents.

So the equivalent first move here is not choosing an artefact. It is establishing **which decisions
a person building an interactive evidence instrument is forced to make, in what order they resolve,
and what evidence exists to settle each**.

Eighteen questions came out of the research. That number is a coincidence, not a target.

### The resolution stack

When two considerations compete, the higher one wins. This is the analogue of the Decision Book's
seven-rung stack, derived from the same logic and from what the research says is unrecoverable if
got wrong.

```
1. exposure and consent          who is harmed by being counted
2. referential integrity         can the claim be traced to a thing and a source
3. inferential integrity         does the method support the claim being made
4. the decision it enters        what the number has to be true enough for
5. legibility                    can the reader read it correctly
6. standing                      will anyone else reconcile to it
7. register                      how it feels, what it looks like
```

Two things to notice. **Rung 1 is not rung 3.** A number can be methodologically impeccable and
still expose someone; Form 696 was methodologically fine. And **rung 7 is last and is not
optional** — the Decorative tier is genuinely free, and the research says the expressive layer is
where the persuasive weight legitimately sits, provided nothing load-bearing lives only there.

### Tier convention, carried over

**Fixed** — uncertainty; a right answer exists; pass or fail; measurable.
**Adaptable** — ambiguity; no single correct answer; defensible or not.
**Decorative** — free, provided nothing load-bearing lives there.

The research is unusually clear about which is which, and the split is not where instinct puts it.
Uncertainty display is Fixed and measured. Narrative genre is Adaptable and unevaluated. Ornament
is Decorative and, on the evidence, evidence-positive.

# Part 0 · The spine

## Ch 1 — Whose decision does this have to survive, and what does that decision need to be true?

Not "who is the user." An evidence instrument's Ch 1 question is **which decision the number
enters**, because that determines everything downstream and it cannot be recovered later.

The Green Book Review 2025 (one year on, 30 June 2026) states that "a single summary metric of
social value (e.g. a BCR) is not enough to make a balanced judgement," instructs appraisers not to
use "arbitrary 'BCR thresholds'," and confirms a proposal with a BCR below one may still be value
for money. So the doctrinal answer to "must it be monetised" is **no**. The institutional answer is
**but** — unmonetised benefits sit in a narrative annex; monetised benefits sit in the ratio the
spending review reads. DCMS built the entire Culture and Heritage Capital programme on the premise
that this asymmetry is fatal.

Munzner's nested model supplies the reason this is rung 4 and not rung 6: four levels — domain
problem characterisation, data/operation abstraction, visual encoding, algorithm — each with its
own threat and its own validation method, and **"an upstream error inevitably cascades to all
downstream levels."** Level 2, abstraction, has *no immediate validation available*, only downstream
field study. A beautiful encoding of the wrong abstraction is unrescuable.

**Fixed:** an instrument built for one decision does not serve another (the Ch 1 mode rule, one
level up). **Adaptable:** which decision to serve.

**What the research already settles.** CHC's Evidence Bank — a live government spreadsheet, last
updated 7 January 2026 — covers museums, galleries, theatres, historic churches, cinema and
digital collections. There is no nightclub, no grassroots venue, no scene. **That is a hole in a
government database, not a rhetorical gap.**

**Still open.** Whether to build the number that survives in the annex, the number that survives in
the ratio, or both, tagged so the reader knows which is which.

## Ch 2 — Who is exposed by being counted, and what would tell you before it happens?

Rung 1, and it is placed here because the sector has its own documented case and nobody uses it.

**Form 696.** The Metropolitan Police required promoters across 21 London boroughs to submit names,
stage names, **private addresses** and phone numbers of promoters, DJs and artists, plus music
style and target audience — originally including which ethnic groups were likely to attend. It ran
2008–2017 and was withdrawn on 10 November 2017 after Sadiq Khan's review. It was criticised as
racial discrimination by UK Music, the British Academy of Composers and Songwriters, and the Music
Producers Guild. Nobody intended harm. A legibility instrument built for safety became an
instrument of genre-targeted policing.

Three more mechanisms, each with a case:

- **Aggregation reveals what no individual disclosed.** Strava's global heatmap, published November
  2017, exposed US bases in Syria and Afghanistan and a Royal Navy nuclear base by January 2018 —
  from voluntary, individually innocuous traces. Later work exposed leaders' security details and
  French submariners. Ask what 5,000 pins reveal that 50 does not.
- **Participatory affective maps are brigadeable.** Queering the Map — now 500,000+ anonymous posts
  in 23 languages, and the closest existing analogue to a vibe map — was taken offline in February
  2018 after a pro-Trump pin flood. Moderation was built *after* the attack.
- **Informality is selectively tolerated.** Aramayona and Guarneros-Meza (*Urban Studies*, 2024)
  show Southern European cities promoting "vibrant" nightlife for tourism while criminalising
  precarious, racialised migrant workers in the same nocturnal economy. Some participants are
  currently protected by *not* being counted.

**And one finding that cuts the other way, which is why this chapter has to be researched rather
than assumed.** The strongest available evidence does *not* support "mapping cultural assets causes
displacement." Grodach, Foster & Murdoch (*Urban Studies* 2018; 30 largest US metros, 2000–2013)
find commercial arts associated with increased likelihood of gentrification, fine arts with none,
and **neither associated with displacement** — their causal reading is that "gentrified places are
attracting the arts." Kitsos, Nathan & Gutierrez-Posada (Creative PEC, 2023; UK Output Areas
2001–2011) find creative firms follow gentrification with effects roughly a tenth the size of
school quality, and state plainly "we cannot observe displacement in our data."

So the honest formulation is not *the map causes displacement*. It is: **the map supplies the
vocabulary and the coordinates that a place-marketing programme will use** — and displacement is
poorly measured in both studies. Saying that puts you ahead of most of the field.

**Fixed:** disclosure enforced at query time (Ch 11); no schema field that identifies a person's
sexuality, immigration status, or presence at a place and time. **Adaptable:** what to publish at
all.

**Refusal test.** If a licensing officer, a police intelligence unit or an immigration enforcement
team obtained the dataset tomorrow, what could they do that they cannot do now? If that question
has not been asked with the *whole* publication in front of you, it has not been asked.

# Part 1 · Referential integrity

## Ch 3 — What is the unit, and what survives when it changes?

Every precedent picks one unit and inherits its blindness. Creative Footprint picks the **venue** —
so a scene living in warehouses, house parties and pop-ups is invisible, and it says so, excluding
venues that host only private events. REKOM's Night Index picks the **consumer night out** — so it
measures spend, frequency and duration and cannot see culture at all. UK Music and NTIA pick the
**industry** — a venue closing is a jobs number. CHC picks the **asset**. Impacts 08 picked the
**city over time**.

Straw's 1991 definition is the sharpest available and it has a direct build consequence: a scene is
"that cultural space in which a range of musical practices coexist, interacting with each other
within a variety of processes of differentiation" — distinguished from a *community*, whose
composition is stable and rooted in geographically specific heritage. A scene is defined by
differentiation and alliance-drawing, **not by membership**. So *"I'm part of this scene"* is not a
membership claim the way "I live here" is, and a self-identification affordance risks smuggling in
a community model where the object is a scene.

Bowker & Star name the mechanism that makes this unrecoverable: **torque**, "a twisting of time
lines that pull at each other." A venue that closes and reopens under a new name with new operators
in the same building has one UPRN, potentially three place records, and an unresolvable question
about whether the cultural entity persisted. Grassroots venues do this constantly. Whatever is
decided must be decided **before the first identifier is minted**, because it is not retrofittable.

**Fixed:** the unit is declared before minting. **Adaptable:** which unit.

## Ch 4 — Does the identifier have to be adopted by others, and what do you owe if it is?

The load-bearing decision. Internal IDs are a join key whose only requirement is stability. Public
IDs are a commitment: you owe redirects, a resolution service, and a policy for when two venues
turn out to be one venue.

MusicBrainz shows the shape of that commitment. An MBID is a 36-character UUID "permanently
assigned," and critically, **"when an entity is merged into another, its MBIDs redirect to the
other entity."** Merge-with-redirect is the hardest promise in the scheme, because in a sector with
no prior authority you will discover duplicates constantly — which is precisely why your own rule
is *never auto-merge, surface evidence, wait for confirmation*. That rule is already correct; what
it lacks is the public redirect obligation.

**Ticket Atlas already has the right architecture.** `docs/ARCHITECTURE.md`: `venue_id` and
`event_id` are "assigned by us, sequentially, the first time a record is created… They never change
and are never derived from a platform's URL, slug, or numeric ID." Platform identity is isolated in
`venue_crosswalk.csv`. That is the correct design and it is already built.

**What made other schemes get adopted, concretely.**

| Scheme | The move that worked |
|---|---|
| **ROR** | Launched as an explicit "minimum viable registry"; **deliberately excludes university departments** to keep it stable and usable; inherited GRID's installed base rather than competing with it. Crossref's own retirement announcement: ROR depositions grew **356% annually vs 12% for Funder IDs**. Two registries did not co-exist. |
| **ORCID** | Got the demand side to *require* it — 112 journals and publishers signed the November 2015 open letter. The trick is commitment two: **collect the iD via the API, never let authors type it.** Typed identifiers carry typos and impersonation; API-collected ones carry proof. |
| **UPRN** | Rejected as a government standard in 2015 **on licensing grounds**; selected in June 2019 conditional on the data being opened; open under OGL from July 2020. The licence was the blocker, not the technology. |
| **Wikidata** | Became the hub nobody has to switch to — 2,000+ knowledge bases linked, so "both VIAF and ISNI are just one hub away." Properties already exist for Songkick venue ID (P3977) and MusicBrainz place ID (P1004). |
| **MusicBrainz** | Distributed *authority*, not just labour: three unanimous yes votes or auto-accept after 14 days; voting requires 11 accepted edits and two weeks' membership. Governance conducted in public. |

**The opening.** MusicBrainz has had a **place** entity since 2013 — venues and studios, with
coordinates and hierarchical containment. Beyond that, ISRC/ISWC/ISNI cover recordings, works and
names; DDEX covers recorded-music metadata; schema.org/MusicVenue is a web vocabulary. *There
appears to be no live-music venue identifier standard.* Treat that as a strong hypothesis needing a
targeted check with DDEX and the ticketing trade bodies — proving a negative needs a phone call —
but if it holds, it is the single largest structural opening in the whole strand.

## Ch 5 — Is the definitions atlas a dictionary or a crosswalk?

Star & Griesemer's boundary objects argue hard for the crosswalk: objects "plastic enough to adapt
to local needs and the constraints of the several parties employing them, yet robust enough to
maintain a common identity across sites," and the headline finding — **"consensus is not necessary
for cooperation nor for the successful conduct of work."**

Your 51 definitions from 32 bodies is already a boundary object of the **repository** type —
ordered, standardised, letting different worlds "access materials for their own purposes without
direct negotiation of differences." Turning it into a dictionary destroys that property. Making the
crosswalk explicit — *this body's "venue" equals that body's "venue" minus these three cases* —
preserves it and makes the incompatibility itself the product.

The operational lesson is sharper still. Grinnell's collecting protocols worked as a lingua franca
between amateurs and professionals because they focused on **"how" rather than "why"**, which
"displaced potential theoretical conflicts through procedural consensus." Trade bodies will not
agree on what nightlife *is*. They can agree on how a headcount is taken. **Standardise method, not
meaning.**

The sector already has a working demonstration. MVT's Grassroots Music Venue definition — six
criteria met in "some or all," where criterion one is the **"Elephant Test": "Musicians and
audiences in the town/borough/city think that is the Grassroots Music Venue"** — was established in
2015 and is now used by Arts Council England and DCMS. It is a definition that deliberately
declines to be an algorithm, and it got adopted by government. Vagueness was not a bug.

**And the residual category is a political decision disguised as a schema decision.** Bowker & Star
found designers should *distribute* residuals rather than concentrate them: "not elsewhere
classified" appears throughout the ICD "but never as a top-level category," keeping ambiguity as
local as possible. A single top-level "Other" is where the queer venues, the unlicensed spaces, the
community centres running one night a month and the whole informal economy go — and once there they
are invisible to policy.

## Ch 6 — Where did this number come from, and can a machine answer that?

The sector's actual disease, in your words: numbers propagate between reports without their
derivation, so they cannot be checked, and repetition reads as corroboration. Your comparative
workbook's evidence-dependency sheet is already the diagnosis. This chapter is the cure.

Prose methodology notes are a document. **W3C PROV-O** (Recommendation, 30 April 2013) is a graph:
three starting-point classes — Entity, Activity, Agent — and nine properties (`wasGeneratedBy`,
`wasDerivedFrom`, `wasAttributedTo`, `used`, `wasAssociatedWith`, `wasInformedBy`,
`actedOnBehalfOf`, `startedAtTime`, `endedAtTime`). Only the graph version makes *"which report did
this number come from, and what did it come from before that"* machine-answerable. Machine-
answerable is what stops laundering.

**Nanopublications** raise the stakes usefully: head graph, assertion graph, provenance graph,
publication-info graph, with trusty URIs for immutability. The atomic citable unit becomes **one
claim with its derivation attached**, not a dataset and not a report.

**RDA Dynamic Data Citation** solves the "how do you cite a living database" problem in 14 steps:
version the data and never delete; run a query store; normalise and stably sort queries; checksum
the result set; timestamp the query; and — the key move — **assign the persistent identifier to the
query, not to a copy of the data**. Re-executing the timestamped query against the timestamped
source returns exactly the data as originally selected. Reported implementation effort across
adopters: *a few person-months spread over six months to a year*.

**The finding that will actually decide whether this works.** Edwards et al., *Science Friction*:
scientists routinely bypass formal metadata systems — one workshop built an Excel spreadsheet
instead of using the web portal because "our Excel table serves us a little better." Their
diagnosis applies exactly to trade bodies: researchers' "main interest… is in using data, not in
describing them for the benefit of invisible, unknown future users, to whom they are not
accountable and from whom they receive little if any benefit." Their metaphor — metadata aims for
the precision of "a Swiss watch" but real practice is "a car engine, running fast and hot, bathed
constantly in motor oil." Their conclusion is a both/and: **"metadata products can be powerful
resources, but very often — perhaps even usually — they work only when metadata processes are also
available."**

Edwards' three frictions — data, computational, and **metadata friction**, "the difficulty of
recovering contextual knowledge about old records" — name what the reference layer's business
actually is: absorbing friction that trade bodies currently push onto each other. **If contributing
to the register is more work than not contributing, adoption fails regardless of quality.**

# Part 2 · Inferential integrity

## Ch 7 — What does your aggregation rule commit you to, and can you state it in real units?

The first and least escapable decision, and the one that answers your own question — *where would
scoring create false precision or harmful incentives?*

The OECD/JRC Handbook is explicit: in linear aggregation "weights express trade-offs between
indicators. A deficit in one dimension can thus be offset (compensated) by a surplus in another,"
at a rate that stays **constant across the whole range**. Geometric aggregation gives partial
non-compensability, and "compensability is lower for the composite indicators with low values" — it
punishes imbalance hardest at the bottom. Non-compensatory multi-criteria aggregation removes
offsetting entirely, and the Handbook says it is the right choice "if the analyst decides that an
increase in economic performance cannot compensate for a loss in social cohesion."

The JRC's worked illustration is the fastest way to see it: **(21,1,1,1) and (6,6,6,6) are identical
under additive aggregation and different under geometric.**

**The nightlife translation.** Under additive aggregation, a city with a booming corporate-owned
late-night drinking economy and a collapsed grassroots, queer and underground scene scores the same
as a city with a modest but broad ecology. Choosing additive aggregation *is* the claim that those
are substitutable.

Ravallion's HDI critique shows what this looks like at scale. The 2010 HDI valued an extra year of
life expectancy at **$0.51 in Zimbabwe, $5.51 in Liberia, and roughly $9,000 in the richest
countries** — a ratio over 17,000 to one — and a poor country "experiencing falling life expectancy
due to a collapse in its health-care system could still see its HDI improve with even a small rate
of economic growth." That was the *consequence of moving to geometric aggregation*. So the lesson is
not that geometric is safer. It is that **every aggregation rule embeds an implicit exchange rate,
and you are responsible for reading it out loud.**

**The refusal test, and it is the best one in the whole research.** Compute your instrument's
implicit exchange rate at several points on the scale. *How many additional venues does your index
say compensate for the loss of one forty-year-old grassroots institution?* If you cannot state the
number, you do not understand your own instrument. If you can state it and it is indefensible, you
have your answer about whether to score at all.

## Ch 8 — Do your weights mean what you say they mean?

Two distinct problems, routinely conflated.

**The theoretical problem.** Munda & Nardo: in a linear aggregation weights are mathematically
trade-off ratios and therefore scale-dependent, so "the interpretation of weights as a measurement
of the (psychological) concept of importance is completely inappropriate," and "the concept of
symmetrical importance is incompatible with a linear aggregation rule." The near-universal
practice — convene a stakeholder panel, ask how *important* each dimension is, plug the numbers into
a weighted sum — **is internally inconsistent.** Importance coefficients belong in a
non-compensatory voting framework; trade-off ratios belong in an additive one.

**The empirical problem.** Paruolo, Saisana & Saltelli measure *actual* influence with the
first-order sensitivity index Sᵢ. 2009 HDI: nominal weights 11–33%, all four indicators roughly
equally important — "the weights are little more than cosmetic." THES rankings: teaching quality
**20% nominal weight, 21% of variance**; peer review **40% nominal, 81% of variance**. Sustainable
Society Index: two components each nominally 28.6%, explaining 9% and 67%.

The mechanism is correlation structure and unequal variance. If three of your nightlife indicators
are all downstream of population density, they will dominate regardless of the announced weights.
This is the precise content of *"the index tells you more about the weights than the world"* — and
the sharpening is that often it tells you neither, because it tells you about **the correlation
structure of your data, which nobody negotiated.**

**Fixed:** publish Sᵢ alongside every nominal weight. If the gap is large, the consultation was
theatre. **Fixed:** budget allocation degrades beyond 10–12 items — respondents suffer "serious
cognitive stress" — which is a hard cap on how many dimensions a panel can legitimately weight.

**The incumbent fails this.** Creative Footprint Rotterdam 2024 publishes Space **8.58**, Community
& Content **5.90**, Framework Conditions **5.41**, and a headline of **6.10/10**. The mean of its
own three parts is **6.63**. No weights are disclosed in the 2018 methodology or the 2024 report.
A published composite that is not reproducible from its published components is the exact failure
mode your comparative workbook exists to detect — and it is the incumbent's.

## Ch 9 — Can the instrument be audited, and at what N does the audit become impossible?

The JRC runs a published statistical audit protocol with numeric thresholds. An index passes or
fails them. From the audit of the 2025 Global Innovation Index:

**Structural coherence.** Indicator-to-sub-dimension correlation **≥ 0.3**. Sub-dimension-to-
dimension **≥ 0.70**. Cronbach's alpha **≥ 0.70** per sub-index. PCA: **one component with
eigenvalue > 1.0** per dimension — more than one means your "dimension" is two things. Outlier
screen: absolute skewness **> 2.25** *and* kurtosis **> 3.5** flags for treatment. Data coverage
floors (GII 66%; the EU Cultural and Creative Cities Monitor accepted 45%).

**Robustness — the step almost nobody does.** Monte Carlo uncertainty analysis; GII uses **5,000
runs**, perturbing weights and varying imputation and aggregation method. Then publish median rank
and a **90% confidence interval on rank for every unit**. The operative threshold: *an interval
narrower than 10 positions is treated as reliable.* GII 2025 reports 85 of 139 economies meeting
it; seven have intervals 21–33 positions wide, meaning their published rank is meaningless. The EU
Monitor's own statement: "for about 80% of the cities… the ranks are the result of the underlying
data and not of the modelling choices" — which means **for one city in five, the rank is an
artefact.**

**And here is the decisive finding for your constraint.** This apparatus is designed for N in the
hundreds. At two places, rank confidence intervals necessarily overlap; correlation-based coherence
tests cannot run; sensitivity analysis has nothing to be sensitive across. **A scored index at N=2
is unauditable in principle.** That is not a preference about form. It is a property of the audit
standard, and it is a strong argument for a diagnostic, a ledger or a registry at this stage — with
the index deferred until N supports it.

## Ch 10 — What comparative claims does your sample actually license?

`GAP-13`, worked against the methods literature. 18–24 conversations across 2 places is 9–12 per
place. A suppression threshold of three survives one frequency count and nothing else.

**QCA is at or below its floor.** Its own self-description addresses datasets "too small for linear
regression but large enough for cross-case analysis." The binding constraint is combinatorial: k
binary conditions generate 2ᵏ configurations, and N cases populate at most N of them. Thiem shows
standard QCA variants "systematically supplement empirical data with artificial data" to fill the
gaps and can thereby "commit causal fallacies of severe magnitude." Add Krogslund et al. (results
"highly sensitive to minor parametric and model-susceptibility changes"), Hug (measurement error
breaks the deterministic assumptions) and Braumoeller ("even very convincing QCA findings may be
the result of chance"). **fsQCA does not rescue this** — it relaxes dichotomisation and relocates
the arbitrariness to calibration thresholds.

**Process tracing is the method that fits.** Collier's four tests give a defensible non-numeric
evidentiary grammar within each case: *straw-in-the-wind* (neither necessary nor sufficient);
*hoop* (necessary, so failing eliminates the hypothesis); *smoking gun* (sufficient, so passing
confirms); *doubly decisive*. Graded, auditable causal claims from a handful of cases — which a
score cannot produce.

**Lijphart's framing is the honest one.** "Many variables, small N" has two solutions: increase N
and go statistical, or **decrease variables** by selecting highly comparable cases. With two places
only the second is available. The design implication is direct and uncomfortable: **the number of
dimensions must be small and theoretically forced, not comprehensive. A nine-dimension framework
applied to two places is not a comparison; it is nine anecdotes.**

## Ch 11 — What can be published at every filter combination, not just the default view?

Disclosure enforced at query time, on every combination — and there is a direct architectural
precedent in ONS's Census 2021 flexible table builder.

The actual rule set, transplantable: **marginal minimum** (row and column totals not too small);
**marginal dominance** (at least 20 respondents outside the most common category); **sparsity**
(≥40% of cells non-zero, and ≥50% of non-zero cells greater than 1); **variable limits** (maximum 4
variables at OA/LSOA, 5 at MSOA and above); and **classification completeness** — users "cannot
request subsets of categories," which is what prevents disclosure by differencing. Plus **cell key
perturbation**: a random record key per record, summed to a cell key, looked up in a perturbation
table and applied *consistently*, so the same cell perturbs identically across every dataset it
appears in. Around 14% of cell counts were perturbed in Census 2021.

**And an accepted compromise you can inherit if you declare it.** ONS assesses "each request…
using the same disclosure rules, and each request is assessed independently" — first come, first
served, with **no tracking of cumulative disclosure risk across a user's query history**. If the UK's
national statistical institute accepts that trade-off for the census, you can defensibly accept it
too. But an unstated version of the same choice reads as ignorance rather than judgement.

**Differential privacy is contested, and you may follow ONS rather than the academic consensus if
you cite the paper.** UKSA's EAP215 (December 2024) rejects DP for population and migration
outputs on three grounds: a privacy budget cannot be managed when "numerous researchers will access
the data across several years"; noise on zero and small cells produced "negative cell values"
biasing rare categories; and retrospective attribute linkage makes ex-ante risk definition
impossible. ONS uses Laplace noise in cell key perturbation and states plainly that it "does not
claim that our disclosure methods will be differentially private."

**Refusal test.** Filter deliberately to a rare category in the smallest geography and see whether
you reach a single venue or operator. Then run two near-identical queries and check the
perturbation is keyed to the cell, not re-drawn per request. If you have not tried to filter your
way to an individual, you have tested the demo.

# Part 3 · The felt layer

## Ch 12 — How first-class is the vibe, and what makes it evidence rather than decoration?

**Straight answer: vibe is researchable, but only if you split it into three things, because the
literature treats them separately and only two are measurable.**

**Vibe as produced condition — fully researchable.** Böhme locates atmosphere as "the common reality
of the perceiver and the perceived," and his decisive move is showing atmospheres are *made* — by
"design, stage sets, advertising, musical atmospheres, cosmetics, interior design." That labour is
observable. Michels turns it into an empirical programme with three tractable layers: tracing the
material modulation, exploring sensual capacities, and accounting for (in)stability — with a named
toolkit of ethnography, formal and informal interviews, autoethnographic field notes, audio-visual
recording, and sensory apprenticeship. **This is also the layer where the resident DJ, the lighting
tech and the door person become visible as skilled labour** — which is your "lifetime value of a
resident DJ" question, made answerable.

**Vibe as perceived quality — measurable, and there is an ISO standard.** ISO 12913-1:2014 defines
soundscape as "acoustic environment as perceived or experienced and/or understood by people, in
context" — perception is constitutive, not incidental. ISO/TS 12913-3 supplies an eight-attribute
circumplex — *pleasant, **vibrant**, eventful, chaotic, annoying, monotonous, uneventful, calm* —
rated 1–5 at 45° intervals, from which Pleasantness and Eventfulness are computed by cosine and
sine. **"Vibrant" is literally a standardised, internationally agreed perceptual attribute of a
place.** It recommends medians, interquartile ranges and Spearman correlations, and cautions about
distributional assumptions at small n.

**Vibe as singular excess — representable, not measurable.** Anderson defines affective atmospheres
as "singular affective qualities that emanate from but exceed the assembling of bodies," holds them
in three deliberate ambiguities, and proposes no method. Stewart practises rather than prescribes.
Garcia-Mispireta's *Together, Somehow* gives the best nightlife demonstration — "stranger-intimacy"
on minimal techno and house floors in Chicago, Paris and Berlin — and is explicit that the
resulting sense of community is "vague, fluid, and may hide exclusions and injustices."

**The design consequence: let the first two carry the evidential weight and the third carry the
persuasive weight, and do not let a reviewer collapse them.** That is Ch 7 of the Decision Book
arriving in a new domain — name the purchase, ship the precise version alongside.

**Methods that work at your N, with a published precedent at exactly your scale.** Reed, Paddon,
Wilkinson & MacLeod (*Sexualities*, 2025) ran participatory collaborative mapping with **12
self-identified lesbian participants across three discussion events in Southampton**, plus five
expert interviews with venue owners and organisers, annotating maps under prompts about safety,
activity and community. It produced a *safety paradox* — suburbs unsafe for public intimacy, city-
centre anonymity a refuge, but safety also requiring being *known* to trusted operators — and an
explicit self-critique that a predominantly white, employed sample risked "re-embedding the
racialised exclusions which so many queer spaces still uphold."

Build it as four stacked components: **go-alongs not sit-downs** (Kusenbach's five domains —
environmental perception, spatial practices, biographies, social architecture, social realms; walk
the route to and from a night, don't interview about it in a café); **sensory ethnography as the
frame** (Pink for emplacement, Michels for reportability); **the ISO circumplex at each site**
(this is the piece that makes the affective layer first-class rather than decorative — a
standardised measurement of felt quality, not a vibe check); and **Q methodology for "who owns your
city's sound"** (produces factors, not averages; 24 participants across two places sits below the
median 40–60 but well within the reported 4–5 minimum, and Q explicitly does not generalise to a
population).

**Integration, and the single decision that separates evidence from advocacy.** Fetters, Curry &
Creswell name three possible outcomes of integrating qualitative and quantitative strands:
*confirmation*, *expansion*, and **discordance** — and treat discordance as a finding, not a
failure. **Pre-commit to reporting discordance.**

**The foil.** Silver, Clark & Rothfield's *Scenescapes* is the only serious numerical
operationalisation of "scene" — three dimensions (Legitimacy, Theatricality, Authenticity) × five
sub-dimensions, 700+ amenity types coded 1–5, ~40,000 ZIP codes — and **no participant was ever
consulted.** The gap between that and a go-along is precisely your value proposition.

# Part 4 · Legibility

## Ch 13 — Does the interaction carry meaning, and what evidence would settle it?

The sharpest question in the brief, and there is a real answer, and it is uncomfortable.

Boy, Détienne & Fekete ran three between-subjects web field experiments (2,975 + 1,178 + 160
sessions) adding narrative introductions to exploratory visualisations. Result: **null to
negative.** "Augmenting exploratory visualizations with introductory 'stories' does not seem to
increase user-engagement in exploration." Users in the *no-story* condition explored roughly twice
as long — **108.8s vs 54s** — and performed more semantic operations. Only 35.4% followed the
intended linear sequence.

The practice-lore version — Archie Tse's "if you make a tooltip or rollover, assume no one will
ever see it," and the widely-repeated 10–15% click figure — is **lore, not evidence.** It traces to
a 2016 Malofiej talk reported second-hand. It points the same direction as Boy et al., which is why
it has stuck, but say so when citing it.

**What does work is narrow, and the narrowness is the useful part.** Prediction-before-reveal
reduced recall error by ~4 percentage points (n=378) — **visual modality only, unfamiliar data
only**. Staged animated transitions improved object tracking and value estimation (n=24, small) —
but *over*-staging increased error, and axis rescaling degraded performance regardless.

**The decision this settles:** default to authored, static-first delivery of every load-bearing
claim; spend interaction budget only where it buys a documented mechanism. Anything else must
justify itself against a known null.

**Two frameworks as coverage audits rather than findings.** Heer & Shneiderman's twelve interaction
types in three groups — Data & View Specification, View Manipulation, and **Process & Provenance**
(record, annotate, share, guide) — is explicitly unvalidated, but the third group is exactly where
evidence instruments under government scrutiny fail, because that is where citation, permalinking
and reproducibility live. Segel & Heer's seven genres and the Martini Glass / Interactive Slideshow
/ Drill-Down patterns are a menu with **no user study behind them**; the authors flag the absence of
"reader-centric research." Martini Glass is the structurally obvious choice here — authored
argument, then reader-driven exploration — but that is a design bet, not evidence.

**And on reader-reweighting.** Hullman & Diakopoulos name the mechanism: **procedural rhetoric** —
anchoring, default views, fixed comparisons, spatial ordering, filtering. A default weighting *is*
an argument; letting readers reweight does not remove the argument, it relocates it to the default.
The OECD Better Life Index is the precedent — 11 petals, user-assigned importance reorders the
ranking, six million visitors, tens of thousands of shared indexes — and the strategic finding is
that the weightings became "an important back channel" of data back to the OECD about public
priorities. **Reader weighting turns the instrument into a collection device**, which is a
defensible answer to "why interactive at all."

## Ch 14 — How do you show uncertainty without making the number look worthless?

The most empirically settled area in the research, and therefore the chapter that proves the
instrument is serious.

- Kale, Kay et al. (n=408, incentive-compatible payoff): **quantile dotplots with 50 dots achieved
  97% of optimal expected payoff** (95% CI [95%, 98%]), ~5pp better than a no-uncertainty control,
  with within-subject SD of 3pp against 7pp for control. Complementary CDFs nearly as good.
  **Textual interval statements were unstable** — the 85% interval performed poorly with little
  learning.
- Hullman, Kay et al. (n=288): HOPs dramatically beat error bars and violin plots for
  **multi-variable** judgements (MAE 0.09–0.13 vs 0.35–0.63) and were **worse** for single-variable
  mean estimation on high-variance data (9.6 vs 2.1). A measured trade-off, not a winner.
- Padilla, Kay & Hullman: hard visual boundaries — error bars, cone of uncertainty — induce
  **deterministic construal error**, where viewers categorise inside/outside, and this "persists
  even with extensive instructions." And explicitly: **"there is no one-size-fits-all uncertainty
  visualization approach."**
- Correll, Moritz & Heer's Value-Suppressing Uncertainty Palettes: **only 24 participants per
  experiment**; the result is a distribution shift in *risk behaviour* (KS D=0.5, p=0.03), not an
  accuracy gain — and the authors flag that suppressing uncertain values is wrong when the black
  swans are the point.

**Settled:** frequency-framed, discrete, outcome-sampled representations over intervals.
**Not settled:** which one, for which task. That requires your own test.

**And the reader-side finding that should change the interface.** Ge, Cui & Kay's CALVI instrument
measures critical visualisation literacy. Baseline performance: **mean correctness 0.39 on
misleading items against 0.80 on normal items.** Hardest to detect: overplotting, missing data, and
**missing normalisation**. Easiest: misleading annotations and bad ordering. So per-capita and
denominator handling is where your audience will *not* catch your error and a hostile reviewer
*will*. **Make normalisation visible in the interface, not just the methodology note.**

Your design system already has the instinct — *"A range is a legitimate headline; uncertainty can
itself be the finding"* and *"Never round to imply precision that the evidence does not support."*
The research supplies the encodings.

## Ch 15 — Who can use it, and is that a build step or a bespoke cost?

Chartability (Elavsky, Bennett & Moritz, EuroVis 2022) is the framework: 50 heuristics under seven
principles — POUR plus **Compromising, Assistive, Flexible** — with 10 flagged Critical, validated
across eight audit environments, adopted by Microsoft, Highcharts, Jupyter, FiveThirtyEight and
Vega-Lite. Honest caveat from the authors: novices need **2–8 hours per full audit** and automated
testing cannot replace manual evaluation.

Zong et al. give the design model — **structure / navigation / description**, with navigation split
structural, spatial and targeted — evaluated with 13 blind and visually impaired screen-reader
users, finding hierarchical structures reduce cognitive load against flat tables. It ships as
**Olli**, open source, working from Vega, Vega-Lite and Observable Plot specs. **That turns the
accessibility decision from a bespoke cost into a build step**, which is the whole argument. Umwelt
extends this to authoring across visual, textual and sonified representations without requiring a
visual chart first — but was evaluated with only five expert screen-reader users, so treat it as
promising rather than proven.

**The regulatory finding is a trap and an opportunity.** The Public Sector Bodies Accessibility
Regulations 2018 almost certainly do **not** bind you — they cover public sector bodies, and
charities only where mostly publicly funded or providing essential public services. Your duty is
the Equality Act's reasonable adjustments. So meeting **WCAG 2.2 AA is a distribution decision, not
a legal one**: a government user cannot embed or link a non-compliant product without inheriting a
problem. That is a much stronger argument than compliance, and it is the one to make internally.

# Part 5 · Standing

## Ch 16 — What does your own standard already assert, and what would an external one add?

*Reframed on Carly's review, 16 August 2026. The first draft asked which external badge to acquire.
That was the wrong question, and it was wrong because it ignored an artefact she already has.*

**You already have an evidence standard, and it has an architecture.** Style Guide v1.4 §06 states
the precedence rule explicitly: the full standard — "provenance codes, minimum source metadata, the
indicator comparability gate, classification versioning, ethics and the recommendation strength
scale" — lives in *Comparative Place-Based Research Methodology v1.0*, and **"where the two
documents touch, the methodology governs."** Two documents, one canonical, precedence declared.
That is the same move the Decision Book makes with its stack, in a different domain.

**And in the places that matter, your standard is stricter than the external one.** Three rules from
§06 and §13 are refusals, not aspirations:

> "A comparison without this note is not publishable."
> "No published figure rests on fewer than three contributing units."
> "Decide disclosure-safe granularity before designing to a granularity, not after."

The Code of Practice refuses nothing. Voluntary application is self-declared, with OSR stating "we
do not have a formal role in regulating the voluntary application of the pillars." So the honest
statement is: **adopting the Code does not raise your floor. It makes your floor legible to people
who have not read your style guide and never will.** That is a smaller purchase than "credibility,"
and a much more useful one, because it tells you exactly when it is worth buying.

**Three models coexist in this niche, and it is a choice rather than a ladder.**

| Route | Who | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Accreditation** | Crown bodies and those acting for them | Legally foreclosed to you — Statistics and Registration Service Act 2007 |
| **Voluntary Code application** | 41 organisations on the public register, including **Carnegie UK**, Social Metrics Commission, The Institute for Global Prosperity (UCL), The Health Foundation, Greater London Authority Group, Femicide Census | Open, self-declared, complaint-triggered review |
| **Self-authored standard plus reputation** | **Centre for Thriving Places** | Publishes the Thriving Places Index. **Not on the register.** |

**Centre for Thriving Places is the case that reframes this, and it is the closest analogue you
have.** A Bristol charity publishing a place-based composite index at local-authority level,
self-funded, with a published methodology and indicator list — and it has taken *neither* route
onto the register. Its own account: the TPI is kept "independent to ensure it reflects the best
available evidence and data… beyond the reaches of political or commercial pressures," and "it has
a strong reputation for rigour and accessibility and we have fought hard to keep both those by
largely self-funding this, our flagship product."

Its nearest peer — Carnegie UK, another wellbeing-framework publisher — took the register route.
**Both work.** Which means the register is not a threshold you must cross to be citable. It is one
of at least two ways to be citable, and the trade-off is legible.

**So the question this chapter has to answer is not which rung.** It is: *what does your own
standard structurally fail to self-supply?* The research says two things, and only two:

- **Independence.** The AQuA Book's RIGOUR requires assurance by someone who is not the analyst. A
  standard cannot certify its own independence, however well written. This is the one gap no amount
  of internal rigour closes.
- **Legibility to a stranger under time pressure.** A civil servant citing you has not read Style
  Guide v1.4 and will not read it. The register is a shortcut that saves them the reading — which
  matters precisely in the moment where they decide whether to check your number or cite it.

**And the cost is real.** A self-declared statement is a hostage: OSR says "if someone raises a
concern with us, we will contact the organisation and review its statement and procedures." Write
it to survive an adversarial reading by a trade body that dislikes your findings, because that is
who will raise the concern. Femicide Census — a small independent project on a contested dataset
built from FOI requests and media sources — is the size precedent: its statement of compliance runs
roughly 1,200 words. That is the artefact, not a programme.

**What follows still stands, because it is about the work rather than the badge.** Everything below
raises the floor whichever route you take.

| Rung | What | Cost | Solo-achievable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Published methodology + Background Quality Report against the five ESS dimensions | 2–4 weeks | Yes. This is the floor; below it nothing else counts |
| 2 | **Minimum RAP** | 3–6 weeks if built in; months to retrofit | Yes |
| 3 | **Voluntary application of Code 3.0** | 1–2 weeks writing, on top of 1–2 | Yes — **highest leverage per unit of credibility, and the only externally visible badge** |
| 4 | QAAD-style assurance of the scraped data (level A2) | 3–8 weeks | Yes, and honest completion will expose gaps you cannot fix, which is the point |
| 5 | Query-time disclosure control | 4–12 weeks engineering | Yes — the genuine cost centre |
| 6 | Full Duck Book higher QA + AQuA Book | Months | **No.** RIGOUR's "Independent" requires a reviewer who is not you |
| 7 | Accredited official statistics | — | Legally unavailable |

**Minimum RAP's seven requirements**, verbatim in effect: minimise manual steps ("copy and paste,
point and click or drag and drop"); build in open-source software, "preferably python or R"; peer
review; audit trail via version control, "preferably Git"; "be open to anyone" on a code-sharing
platform; established QA practices; well-commented code with documentation "embedded and version
controlled within the product." **Any copy-paste in the pipeline fails.**

**And the standard government now applies to itself.** The Magenta Book 2026 transparency annex
says government evaluation "should be performed using code written in an open-source language (such
as R, Python and so on) where possible," with a 12-week registration rule. If you are closed-source
you are below the bar government has set for itself. Note also: you **cannot** register on the
Evaluation Registry — it is restricted to government departments, ALBs and What Works Centres. Your
route is OSF Registries or a Registered Report.

**Your data type is not disqualifying.** ONS moved web-scraped and scanner data into production
consumer price statistics — rail fares and second-hand cars, with impact analysis published January
2025. But it took roughly a decade of published research articles and impact analyses. The
implication is precise: **publish the transformation research, not just the outputs.** And the ESS
web content retrieval guidelines set the scraping conditions — identify via user-agent with contact
and purpose, respect robots.txt, rate-limit, **seek an API or data-sharing agreement before
scraping**, and use data solely for statistical purposes.

**Cheapest high-value action, and it is not desk research.** Email `regulation@statistics.gov.uk`,
describe the instrument, ask about voluntary application under Code 3.0. OSR runs a community of
practice, has publicly argued for non-government adoption, and has form with exactly this kind of
organisation.

## Ch 17 — What makes others adopt it, and what does success cost you?

Strip the theory and the adoption mechanism is a small set of repeatable moves, visible across
MusicBrainz, ORCID, DataCite, ROR and UPRN:

**Solve an acute, visible failure. Launch deliberately minimal. Make the data un-encloseable. Get
one high-leverage demand-side actor to *require* it. Inherit an installed base rather than compete
with one. Charge for services, not data. Govern in public.**

MusicBrainz exists *because* of enclosure — Robert Kaye had typed roughly 200 CDs into CDDB, and
when Gracenote privatised it, "I didn't get a check." The licence split (factual data public
domain; live feeds under CC BY-NC-SA) is designed to prevent recurrence, and institutional users —
BBC, Last.fm from 2007 — pay for timely convenient access while the data stays free. POSI states
the rule flatly: **"Revenue generated from services, not data."** A sector whose venues have been
repeatedly asset-stripped will read a licence as a promise about power, not a legal formality.

**FAIR gives you the technical shape of your own instinct.** You said: *"open source at the point of
the user… the data layer is protected, but the knowledge layer is shared."* FAIR's principle **A2 —
"metadata are accessible, even when the data are no longer available"** — is exactly that, and it
means rich public metadata plus clear access rules gives "a high degree of FAIRness even in the
absence of FAIR publication of the data itself." Publish the index and the provenance; gate the
values. That is not a compromise, it is a recognised pattern.

**And there is a live proof of it in your own sector, in your own city.** Centre for Thriving
Places' *Statement about data and pricing transparency* (February 2025) describes exactly the split
POSI prescribes and you described to Skiddle. The public layer: months of expert data-team work
"made freely available to all via the website in the form of **headline scores and interactive
maps**." The funded layer: "the framework and the data behind it," sustained by "a tiered
subscription model" plus "a series of costed support offers to meet the needs and resources of
different actors."

Two details are worth taking whole. First, they published the *reason* — "this work, when done
well, is not cheap to deliver" — rather than quietly gating. Second, and this is the sharpest line
in the document, the subscription exists partly to ensure "that investment comes from sources that
**will not seek to adapt its purpose or reach**." That is a governance argument wearing a pricing
statement's clothes, and it is the answer to the question a nightlife funder will ask about
independence. It is also the counterpart to the MusicBrainz enclosure story: one organisation
protects the layer by licence, the other by choosing its buyers. Both are promises about power.

**CARE decides who can revoke.** Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics —
written because open-data principles "primarily focus on characteristics of data that will
facilitate increased data sharing" while overlooking rights. The formulation to adopt is the
authors' own: **"Be FAIR and CARE."** The concrete question: can a *scene*, not just an individual,
revoke its representation — at what granularity, and how fast?

**Two uncomfortable findings about success.**

First, **successful infrastructure becomes invisible.** Star's properties include transparency — it
"invisibly supports" tasks and "becomes visible upon breakdown." Bowker & Star add that
classifications naturalise until taken-for-grantedness is "a sine qua non of membership in a
community of practice." If the business model depends on visibility, it is fighting its own
success. The durable assets are not the schema: they are **the curation function** (someone must
arbitrate merges and splits forever), **the installed base** (infrastructure "wrestles with the
inertia of the installed base"), and **services on top**.

Second, **you cannot have comparability across time and accuracy in the present.** Bowker & Star on
the ICD, revised roughly every decade since the 1890s: "maintaining continuity for historical
comparison requires accepting outdated categories, while accuracy demands constant updating." A
trade body wanting a ten-year trend and a venue wanting to be described correctly today are asking
for different systems. Pick, and say which. The GLA's Cultural Infrastructure Map already
demonstrates the cost of not picking — it states its 2019 and 2023 music venue datasets are "not
directly comparable" because the definitions changed.

**And publish the living will.** POSI requires organisations to communicate long-term stewardship
commitments and define succession criteria. Nobody adopts an identifier scheme that dies with its
founder. That is an adoption mechanism, not paperwork.

## Ch 18 — Advocacy or evidence: what happens when the number is inconvenient?

The closing chapter, because it is the one that makes the rest binding.

Belfiore's *On bullshit in cultural policy practice and research* documents former Culture Secretary
Chris Smith's 2003 admission that he deployed contested impact statistics as **"ammunition"** to
secure Treasury funding regardless of their validity. Merli's demolition of Matarasso's *Use or
Ornament?* is the technical version: no baseline, no control group, ~75% questionnaire
non-response, leading questions with social desirability bias, and fifty hypotheses phrased so that
participation *"can"* produce effects — which she compares to a mineral water label reading "it can
be diuretic."

An organisation aiming to be **the source of truth that trade bodies reconcile to** is structurally
exposed here, because trade bodies are advocacy organisations. There is exactly one mechanism that
distinguishes you from the thing Belfiore is describing: **a pre-registered methodology and a
published correction policy, in place before you publish a number.**

Porter sharpens the diagnostic. Quantification is a "technology of distance," adopted where personal
trust and professional authority are absent; numbers reflect the **weakness** rather than the
strength of an expert community, and "a decision made by the numbers… has at least the appearance
of being fair and impersonal." His closing observation — that elite physicists rely on personal
trust, not metrics — is the question to sit with. *Does the sector want an index because it has a
measurement problem, or because it lacks standing and is reaching for borrowed authority?* Those
call for different products. Saltelli's version: composite indicators proliferate for **advocacy**
rather than analytical reasons, and the failure mode is advocacy wearing the costume of
measurement.

**And the door is open.** DCMS's own Evidence Review of the UK City of Culture programme (Warwick
Business School, February 2025) found Derry/Londonderry pursued 35 outcomes, Hull 27, Coventry 15;
only Coventry completed a social cost-benefit analysis; and media value estimates for comparable
activity ranged from **£19m to £450m**. Its conclusion is the single most useful sentence in the
place-based literature for you: there is an argument for **"evaluating less but doing so with more
rigour and robustness."** Government has already conceded in print that the sector's evaluation is
incomparable. An externally supplied standard is welcome, not resented.

# What the research has already decided

Stated separately, because these are findings and not options.

1. **The free-events assumption is dead.** 239 zero-price offers across 193 events in Ticket Atlas;
   98 entirely free; the Gallimaufry case is decisive. Caveat honestly: it is almost all Headfirst,
   and the other platforms have no tier data scraped, so their silence is a gap not evidence.
2. **A scored index at N=2 is unauditable in principle.** The JRC robustness apparatus needs N in
   the hundreds. This is not a preference about form.
3. **QCA does not work at 9–12 cases per place.** Process tracing does.
4. **Interaction is null-to-negative by default.** Static-first for every load-bearing claim.
5. **Uncertainty display is settled enough to be Fixed.** Quantile dotplots and CCDFs over
   intervals and text.
6. **"Vibrant" is an ISO-standardised perceptual attribute of a place.** The affective layer can be
   first-class without being decorative.
7. **You already have an evidence standard, and it is stricter than the external one.** Style Guide
   v1.4 §06 declares its own precedence rule and contains actual refusals. An external standard buys
   *legibility to strangers* and *independence*, not a higher floor.
8. **Accreditation is legally closed. The register is a choice, not a threshold.** Centre for
   Thriving Places publishes the TPI and is not on it; Carnegie UK, its nearest peer, is. Both work.
9. **There appears to be no live-music venue identifier standard.** Needs one phone call to confirm.
10. **The incumbent's arithmetic does not reconcile.** Creative Footprint Rotterdam: 8.58 / 5.90 /
    5.41 → published 6.10, mean 6.63, weights undisclosed.
11. **Arts largely follow gentrification rather than causing it**, and displacement is unobservable
    in the two strongest studies. The honest risk is vocabulary and coordinates, not causation.
12. **ADE × VibeLab launch a Global Nighttime Culture Index in October 2026** — six cities, six
    categories, "an interactive public dashboard paired with an annual report."

# What this research design does not do

It does not choose the artefact. It does not choose the robustness standard. It does not say what
"source of truth" means operationally. Those were asked as questions and correctly refused: they
are what the research is for.

It also does not yet contain a single rule. Rules come from real artefacts breaking. Under the
Decision Book's own stop rule, none of the above is a rule until something built against it fails.
