# Chapter 11 — The first screen and the path through it

*Dashboard Design · Part 3: The reading experience*

---

*Reading this chapter.* This is one chapter of *Dashboard Design*, Nightlife Culture's research book on how evidence becomes a display a reader can trust, read and act on. The studio ("the house" in some chapters) is Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio; the brief is its founder Carly Heath's commission for the book, and her eight ways a dashboard fails, the failure register, run through it. The Vibe Map, the studio's prototype for showing a place's nightlife culture, appears only as an illustration, never as a specification. "Version 1" is the book's archived first draft, whose positions this version re-tests against the sources. Every rule carries a kind (where it comes from: Standard, Evidence, Reading, Practice or Nightlife Culture) and a tier (how firm it is: Fixed, Adaptable or Decorative); Chapter 0 explains both, and the three senses of Fixed: a requirement that can be checked, a sequence whose order is the hard line, and a statement of the record. A dagger (†) marks a source read at the authors' abstract or summary only; "secondary" a quotation reached through a named reproduction; "(read once)" a quotation returned on a single reading and not confirmed by a second. Chapter 0 holds the glossary of the words the chapters share; each chapter lists its own coinages before its sources.

---

## The question

**What does a dashboard say before anyone touches it, and to whom; how are the maker's authored account and the reader's own exploration placed in one display so that neither is a toll on the other; what must an interaction buy to justify existing, and what does each cost; how does a state a reader reaches become something they can cite a year later; and how does a display read from the data layer and respond to it — the records beneath the numbers, the three nothings, a felt distribution, a map that does not stigmatise — so that the face carries what the chapters behind it require without becoming a methodology page?** The book does not design the Vibe Map's first screen. It sets out what is known about first screens, paths and states, what each form costs, and the tests that tell a surface that works from one that performs.

---

## The 60-second version

> Two public bodies that publish dashboard guidance in the UK begin from the same place: "Only use a dashboard if you have clear justification for doing so", because dashboards "put the burden of interpreting and understanding data onto the user", "can quickly become obsolete", "require a lot of maintenance" and "are difficult to make accessible and responsive for different devices". The research field's own survey of 83 dashboards says the community "has rarely given dashboards their due consideration"; its pattern catalogue of 144 says "Our patterns are purely descriptive" and that "It was beyond the scope of this work to determine what makes for a 'good' dashboard"; and a census of 25,620 published dashboards found most "far simpler than prior research accounts for", three in four interactive, with a median of eight blocks of which a fifth were text. So the first screen is designed on evidence from elsewhere: on what people read first — in the one study to ask, "Titles, headers, and primary overview components were consistently visited first", and most reading flows "began with either the title (42/64, 65.6%)"; on how much they read — "Data show that users only read around 28% of content on a page"; and on what they read it on — "Over half of users view the ONS website on a mobile device", smartphones carried 77 per cent of UK adults' time online in 2025, and on the ONS's phones "80% of users on a phone or tablet do not scroll past the first quarter of a release".
>
> So the canonical truths. The first screen is the product and it is a sentence, to a named reader, in words that lead, that works before any interaction; a dashboard is a form to be justified and the burden of understanding is the maker's. Every first screen is a landing pattern and an endorsement, chosen, named and designed as the anchor it will be; nothing is asked of the reader before something is given; and the small screen is the first screen. The authored account and the open door stand side by side with neither a toll on the other; every written line links to the view that shows it; and text on the face is not clutter and steers what is taken away — a title that contradicts its chart is remembered over it, and a caption that points at a weak feature loses to the chart's strong one — so the sentence says what the view shows. An interaction is paid for: it names its purchase and its cost, the static reading works first, nothing crucial sits behind hover, click, drag or motion, and dense marks that must be small get an equivalent route. A state a reader can reach is a state they can cite: a stable address that encodes the selection, a dated snapshot of data and text, and a generated citation — because links rot, content drifts and the national archive usually cannot hold an interactive page. A view is a function of a data version, a specification and a selection, and the display says so: the records' kinds on the face, the records one step away, a cell not shown marked and explained, and a source line that can be followed and not only seen. A map is the hardest first screen: rates not counts, declared breaks, matched legends, and a form that keeps small areas legible, because dark is read as more and small is missed by a third. Beneath every view sits a *face sheet*, one per view, and its first line is the view's sentence.
>
> The six landing patterns version 1 named — message-first, input-first, message-then-input, select-first, exemplar-first, map-first — have never been tested against one another; what exists is single-pattern evidence. Readers adopt a search box when it is beside content, not instead of it (66.7–80.6 per cent in four of five cases), and "the data people looked for while using search was often deeply personal". Asking readers to predict and then explain before they see helps recall by about four points per hundred on moderately familiar data, by one to two on unfamiliar data, and not at all on familiar; predicting alone buys less, and on unfamiliar data nothing. Asking them to configure is work few do: the one index built on reader weighting records tens of thousands of submitted indexes against millions of visitors. And the ONS's advice stands as the nearest rule: start "from a view that either shows the main message without needing any interaction, or helps users navigate the chart".
>
> The authored account and the open door. The field's vocabulary for sequencing — seven genres; the "Martini Glass", which "begins with an author-driven approach" and then "opens up to a reader-driven stage" — comes from a study of 58 pieces whose authors wrote that "Further research is needed to characterize how readers perceive narratives presented via visualizations", and whose most common structure may open with no text at all, "an interesting default view or annotations". The most-cited evidence that an authored lead-in does not make people explore more could not be re-read at source for this chapter and is carried with a flag. What could be read says: with 180 readers, a slideshow layout beat a vertical one for comprehension, and linking each sentence to its chart "significantly increased user engagement"; with about 200, readers recalled a title's message more often than the chart's even when the two disagreed, credibility fell as they diverged, and "72-87%" still called the information neutral; with 302, "heavily annotated charts were not penalized", readers preferred the most annotated, and annotations carrying statistics or outside context steered what readers took away, while "Perceptual content relating to patterns or trends should be positioned in the title"; and with 240, scroller against stepper made no difference to engagement and comprehension was flat throughout. The oldest principle in the explorable-explanations tradition is the one the evidence supports: "The reader is not forced to interact in order to learn."
>
> What an interaction must earn. The best-known taxonomy's authors say "Validating and evolving this taxonomy is a community project"; the cost framework names seven costs from 32 studies' reported problems; and the measured cases go one way. Across 2,452 readers analysed in two experiments, "adding interaction to a static Bayesian reasoning visualization does not improve participants' accuracy", "In some cases, it can significantly detract from it", and only 43–44 per cent of those offered a filter used it. Among 3,371 panel members, a required interaction cut completion from 79.3 to 65.5 per cent and the correct choice from 73.8 to 61.6 per cent in the condition where one option was clearly better. Across three studies of guided introductions totalling 596 people, "there are no significant differences between the answer correctness of the questions with and without onboarding", with one unfamiliar chart form the exception. GOV.UK records "evidence that some users avoid clicking the link to show more details, as they think it will take them away from the page". And hover is governed by a standard whose stated reasons are magnification, time and keyboard — not touch; the touch argument rests on practitioners ("They are not normally available on touchscreens") and on a sister criterion's remark that "people using a touchscreen device may not use a physical keyboard". Targets are "at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels", with a named exception for "an interactive data visualization where targets are necessarily dense" and a request for "equivalent functionality through alternative means".
>
> Citable states. "URIs don't change: people change them." Half the links in US Supreme Court opinions (49.9 per cent) "no longer had the cited material"; "one out of five" scientific articles suffers reference rot; for "over 75% of references the content has drifted away from what it was when referenced"; and the UK Government Web Archive says in terms that "Interactive content usually cannot be archived", naming interactive maps. The standards answer in five words from the data-citation principles — "timeslice, version and/or granular portion" — and in fourteen recommendations from the Research Data Alliance: version the data, timestamp every operation, store the query, give it a persistent identifier, checksum the result, resolve to a landing page. The working model is Our World in Data's three layers: the selection in the URL, a dated snapshot that "will never change or update", and a citation that names the snapshot and the retrieval date — with one gap, that the snapshot drops the reader's selection, which the chapter's rule closes by putting the selection's address and the snapshot's date in the same citation.
>
> How a display reads from the data. A grammar makes the data "independent from the other components"; a view is compiled from "(data, transforms, mark-type, encodings)" and "visual encodings are automatically re-evaluated as selections change" — so a state is a data version plus a specification plus a selection, and is reproducible from the three. On the face, metadata made charts "more thorough" but not more "accurate, clear, and complete"; revealing a source changed 17 of 42 people's rankings and left 25 unmoved; a source line "suggests that there may be no need for the user to check"; and beauty raises trust "fully mediated by people's inferences of the general competence of the graph maker". Leaving missing values out was the most biased way to show them; every uncertainty display lowered confidence; explaining a gap raised confidence and risk-taking. On maps, "Choropleth maps should display data as standardised rates, not absolute values"; quantile breaks beat natural breaks and "matched legends across series … increased map-comparison accuracy by approximately 28 percent"; and in a 260-person study "small areas were missed by approximately 30–40% of map users".

## 1 · What a dashboard is, and what its first screen is

Three bodies of writing describe a dashboard, and this chapter begins by setting them beside the book's own definition, because the gap between them is where the first screen is decided.

### The practitioner's single screen, and the field's two genres

The sentence most practitioners inherit is from 2004: "A dashboard is a visual display of the most important information needed to achieve one or more objectives; consolidated and arranged on a single screen so the information can be monitored at a glance." Its author added the test — "The means is a single-screen display; the purpose is to efficiently keep in touch with the information needed to do something" — and later distinguished displays "used for monitoring what's going on (dashboards)" from those "used to analyze data". The research field's survey of 83 built dashboards found the word had outgrown the sentence. It distinguishes "the visual genre of dashboards (a visual data representation structured as a tiled layout of simple charts and/or large numbers …) and the functional genre (an interactive display that enables real-time monitoring of dynamically updating data)" and will "consider a display a dashboard if it matches either the visual genre or the functional genre, or both"; it records that "The dashboard concept has evolved from single-view reporting screens to include interactive interfaces with multiple views and purposes, including communication, learning, and motivation"; and it admits both the field's neglect — "Despite their prevalence, the visualization research community has rarely given dashboards their due consideration" — and its own method's limit: "We did not directly consult dashboard users or designers". The pattern catalogue that followed, built on 144 dashboards, names 42 patterns in eight groups — screen fit or overflow, single page or hierarchy, open or stratified layout, data source and update information among the "meta information" — and says what they are: "Our patterns are purely descriptive in that they capture existing solutions and can serve as speculation tools in the design process"; "It was beyond the scope of this work to determine what makes for a 'good' dashboard, and this will depend on many subjective and domain-specific factors"; and, as the one structural law it offers, "Reducing one of the parameters (screenspace, abstraction, number of pages, interaction) requires an increase in one of multiple of the other parameters". Its workshop tested the vocabulary with 23 participants and no readers.

What ordinary dashboards look like is now counted. A census of "25,620 dashboards on Tableau Public" — the top tenth by views — found "19,304 of the 25,620 dashboards in our corpus (75%) were interactive", "The number of blocks per dashboard ranged from 2 to 267 (median: 8, mode: 4)", "53,267 text blocks (21%)", and the typical dashboard "far simpler than prior research accounts for". A separate study of 190 public dashboards found a "Title (present in 92% of dashboards in our corpus)" and "Interaction Guidance (58%)", with its experts split on whether to state conclusions at all — "the last thing I want to do is to feed conclusions to the user" — and dry about instructions: "They're really useful, but they're only useful once, right?"

### The official publishers' form to be justified

The UK's two public bodies that publish guidance on dashboards treat the form as something that needs a reason. The ONS: "A dashboard is a visual tool that shows different views of data to provide an overview of a specific topic"; "Only use a dashboard if you have clear justification for doing so"; "Dashboards can be problematic, as they:" "are not well-suited to highlighting important findings; they put the burden of interpreting and understanding data onto the user"; "are not well-suited to data that need a large amount of explanation to be understood"; "can quickly become obsolete, as findings and user needs change"; "require a lot of maintenance if they need regular updating"; "are difficult to make accessible and responsive for different devices". Its conditions: "you are communicating high-level indicators; the more specific the issues and insights are, the less likely it is that a dashboard will be useful", and "the data are updated frequently, and users are likely to revisit the dashboard". Its two tips: "put the most important insights at the top or in the most prominent position", and — dropping the practitioner's single screen — "you do not have to fit everything onto one screen; if the most important information is visible, the user can scroll up and down". "In most cases it is better to use charts that provide users with specific insights". The Analysis Function's guidance, published 6 February 2026, uses nearly the same sentence — dashboards "put the burden of understanding data and important messages onto the users", "are not suitable for data that requires a large amount of explanation", "can quickly become obsolete if findings and user needs change", and "Dashboards often require more resource than initially identified due to their complexity" — and adds the conditions "there is an identified user need", "users need flexibility of data combinations and the ability to interact with data", and "you have sufficient resource to support the dashboard"; its lifecycle runs Discovery, Alpha, Beta, Live, Retirement, and its testing page asks for "an inverted pyramid structure, with your most important headline content up front". "Dashboards are not good at explaining what's happening in the data and users may have to discover insights for themselves."

### What dashboard users do with one

The two studies that asked dashboard users are small and organisational, and worth reading for one finding each. Twenty interviews with analysts and dashboard users found that "By far the most common tool switch was the 'data dump': extracting data out of a dashboard for ad hoc analysis in a spreadsheet"; one participant: "it's really a jumping off point for questions"; and the authors note the people their method cannot reach — "individuals who experience such significant barriers that they forego dashboard use are absent from our study". A survey with "177 complete responses" from organisations, of which 164 were classified as "122 decision makers … and 42 data analysts", with ten people interviewed and nine retained on "a difficult decision they made in the past", found "Only one participant reported the use of dashboards during the decision making meeting" and that "Our findings stress the need to expand visualization design beyond data analysis into tools for information management"; the authors add that qualitative findings "should not be interpreted as conclusive or cases for drawing causal inferences".

### The book's definition, set against the three

The book's working definition — a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader — is narrower than the field's genre and closer to the official publishers' conditions than to the practitioner's screen. It names the reader (the Analysis Function's "identified user need"); it names the decision (the ONS's "specific insights"); and it says nothing about a single screen, a tiled layout or real-time monitoring, which are forms and not purposes. What it inherits from the three is the burden: a dashboard is a form that puts the work of understanding on the reader unless the maker takes it back, and the first screen is where the maker either takes it back or does not. That is the chapter's subject, and its first finding is that the field has described many first screens and measured almost none of them, so what follows is built from the perception evidence Chapter 3 settled, from the single-pattern and single-mechanism studies that exist, and from the public design systems' stated practice — each marked for what it is.

## 2 · Why people believe what they believe

Eight beliefs sit under most first-screen decisions. Chapter 3 dealt with "overview first", with the fifty-millisecond glance and with "a picture speaks for itself"; these are the eight that remain.

### Belief one: a dashboard needs no words

The intuition is that charts are the point and text is apology. The evidence runs the other way at every scale. In the census of published dashboards a fifth of all blocks were text, and in the 190-dashboard study nine in ten carried a title; in the one reading-order study, "Titles, headers, and primary overview components were consistently visited first"; when a title contradicts its chart, readers recall the title's message more often than the chart's — and most often neither; and with 302 readers, "heavily annotated charts were not penalized" and the most annotated were preferred. The ONS's own semi-automated area pages exist because "we need to pull out the narrative and the story to help guide our users through the numbers". The belief persists because text is the part of a dashboard that cannot be generated by a chart library.

### Belief two: it must fit on one screen

The intuition is the practitioner's definition. The ONS's guidance now says "you do not have to fit everything onto one screen; if the most important information is visible, the user can scroll up and down"; GOV.UK's and the ONS's content rules put the most important information first and let the rest follow; and the pattern catalogue names "Overflow" and "Multiple page" beside "Screenfit" as ordinary choices with a stated trade-off. What is true in the belief is narrower: what a reader sees without acting is what most readers see, so the sentence and the one thing it rests on are above the fold, and the rest is a path. The belief persists because "at a glance" is a good phrase.

### Belief three: the authored account must come first

The intuition is that a reader needs to be told why before they are shown what. The sequencing literature's own authors say they studied makers, not readers; the structure they found most common may open with no text at all, "an interesting default view or annotations"; and the one field test of an authored lead-in before exploration — carried here with a flag because it could not be re-read — says in its abstract that augmenting visualisations with introductory "stories" "does not seem to increase user-engagement in exploration" (abstract †). What helps is not the account first but the account *linked*: a sentence tied to its chart raised engagement with 180 readers, and a slideshow layout beat a vertical one for comprehension. The belief persists because an argument feels incomplete without a preamble, and because the preamble is the part the maker enjoyed writing.

### Belief four: interactive is better

The intuition is that a reader who can act understands more. In the largest direct test, "adding interaction to a static Bayesian reasoning visualization does not improve participants' accuracy on a Bayesian reasoning task" and "In some cases, it can significantly detract from it"; in a 3,371-person panel a required interaction lowered both completion and the correct choice; across 596 readers an interactive guide bought no correctness except for one unfamiliar form; and the field's own review says "There is limited empirical evaluation of the effectiveness of interactive articles" because "large publishers are unwilling to share internal metrics". The authors of the first study put the conclusion plainly: "interaction is not as unambiguously good as we often believe", and "a well designed static visualization can be as, if not more, effective than an interactive one". The belief persists because interaction is what distinguishes a dashboard from a report, and because the people who build dashboards use the controls.

### Belief five: put the detail in a tooltip

The intuition is that hover is free space. The standard that governs content on hover lists the reasons it fails — users "may not have intended to trigger the interaction", "may not know new content has appeared", magnification "generally follows the mouse pointer", and "Users with disabilities may require more time" — and the practitioners add the plain one: tooltips "are not normally available on touchscreens". None of the three UK public design systems has a tooltip component. The ONS exempts "charts where the only interactive element is a tooltip" from its definition of an interactive chart, and then builds its own chart components so that tooltips are reachable by keyboard and "Download options displayed below the chart". A tooltip is a place for what nobody needs; what someone needs is on the face. The belief persists because the chart library's default puts the value in a tooltip and nowhere else.

### Belief six: a link is a citation

The intuition is that a URL to a view is as good as a reference. Half the links in US Supreme Court opinions no longer lead to what was cited; three out of four scholarly web references lead to content that has drifted; the national web archive "usually cannot" capture interactive content; and the one public dashboard found to have changed its citation practice did so in 2025 so that "citation instructions now point to archived versions of charts, to ensure that any data points you are referring to in the cited chart remain stable and unchanged". A link is an address; a citation needs a snapshot. The belief persists because the link works on the day it is pasted.

### Belief seven: a map shows everyone fairly

The intuition is that geography is neutral. A choropleth of counts is "distorted by the size of the area"; the breaks the ONS defaults to were, in the one controlled test, in a lower group than quantiles; "small areas were missed by approximately 30–40% of map users"; and asked to say which of two maps without legends had the larger values, 93 per cent of readers in that study "selected that map that showed an overall darker appearance" when the strict answer was that they could not know. Chapter 10 added that red "spells danger" and that large empty areas "tend to dominate visually". A map is the first screen that most needs a twin, a legend and a declared method. The belief persists because a map is the one chart everyone can read at once.

### Belief eight: a guided tour will fix "what am I looking at"

The intuition is that onboarding solves the schema problem Chapter 3 named. The largest test — three studies, 596 people, bar charts to parallel coordinates — found no difference in answer correctness with or without the guide, readers who "may not take enough time with the onboarding concept itself to carefully read through the text", and a comment that "for highly familiar visualization types no onboarding is needed"; a dashboard-tour system was tried with six end users and no baseline. The public dashboards' own text studies say instructions are "only useful once". What fixes "what am I looking at" is a first screen that says, and a form the reader already has a schema for. The belief persists because a tour is something the maker can build.

## 3 · What a first screen says first

### Where reading starts

The one study to ask readers where they begin on a dashboard — "a mixed-methods study with 18 dashboard authors and 16 end-users", eight dashboards, participants ordering components in a flow tool while thinking aloud — found that "Most flows began with either the title (42/64, 65.6%)" or with a logo-then-title sequence, that "Titles, headers, and primary overview components were consistently visited first", and that "author flows tended to be cleaner and more coherent" than readers'. One participant: "if the text is too large, I will tend to skip over it". Its limits are its authors': "our study relies on self-reported and externally articulated reading sequences rather than direct behavioral measures such as eye tracking", and the sample leans to business users meeting a dashboard once. Read beside Chapter 3's eye-tracking finding that people look at the title first and longest, and the public dashboards' 92 per cent title rate, it says that the title is the first screen's first object, and that the place a reader starts is the place the maker's sentence goes.

### How much is read, and on what

The public design systems write their structure rules from one figure and one device. The ONS: "Data show that users only read around 28% of content on a page" — no source or date is given on the page; GOV.UK's content guidance says most people "only read 20 to 28% of text on a webpage", linking to a usability article that no longer resolves. Hence "We use the inverted pyramid model, which recommends ordering content by its importance", "The most important information in your content should be placed at the start of each sentence, paragraph or section", "Put the most important information first", "The quicker you get to the point, the greater the chance your users will see the information you want them to". The limits are numeric: chart titles "front-loaded, in the active voice, in sentence case, as concise as possible, 15 words or fewer" and describing "the main trend you want the chart to show to the user"; headings "preferably fewer than 60 characters" and "frontloaded with the most important information"; GOV.UK titles "65 characters or less (including spaces)"; summaries 160 characters; a release's main points "up to six bullet points, with one trend or point per bullet", the first "the one trend or figure that you want users to focus on or take away", each "fewer than 25 words … or what you can say in one breath". The ten-word chart-title rule version 1 quoted is no longer on the ONS's pages; fifteen is.

The device: "Over half of users view the ONS website on a mobile device" (the ONS's page), "Two-thirds of users of the ONS website will view content on a mobile device" (the Analysis Function's, linking to the ONS's; the two official figures disagree and neither is dated), and "80% of users on a phone or tablet do not scroll past the first quarter of a release" (undated, unsourced on the page). The measured external figures: 53.56 per cent of UK page views in September 2026 came from phones (a practitioner count), and "Smartphones remained the primary device for accessing the internet for UK online adults, accounting for 77% of all time spent online on the average day" in Ofcom's 2025 report. What phones do to a data display is counted in section 6; what they do to the first screen is simpler: there is no hover, there is less width than a chart wants, and four in five phone readers of the ONS's releases do not pass the first quarter. The sentence goes at the top because on the device most readers use there is nowhere else.

### The six landing patterns, against the evidence

Version 1 audited ten live instruments and named six shapes a first screen takes: *message-first* (an argument, data deferred); *input-first* (blank until you say where you are); *message-then-input* (the stakes, then the box); *select-first* (a panel awaiting a dropdown); *exemplar-first* (one concrete case carries the whole); *map-first* (everything plotted, filter to focus). The chapter asked whether any study compares them. None does. There is no head-to-head test of landing patterns for data tools, no study of empty states, none of map-first landing, and none of exemplar-first openings beyond the anchoring evidence Chapter 3 carries. What exists bears on single patterns, and it is reported pattern by pattern.

*Message-first.* The reading-order study above, and the two design systems' structure rules, say that readers begin with the title and that the most important sentence goes first; the ONS's advice on interactive charts says to start "from a view that either shows the main message without needing any interaction, or helps users navigate the chart". A message-first screen is the one the evidence describes, provided the message is a sentence and not a brochure.

*Input-first and search.* With 830 people and five visualisations, adding a search box to a display that already showed everything was adopted by 66.7 to 80.6 per cent in four cases and 32.5 per cent in one; "In general, a majority of people used text-based search when present in a visualization"; "the data people looked for while using search was often deeply personal"; and "participants spent significantly more time with data items when search was active". That is evidence for a search box beside content. It is not evidence for a blank screen that waits for one, which no study tested. The ONS's own area tool, as it stood on 6 October 2026, is still input-first: a heading, "Find, compare and visualise statistics about places in the United Kingdom", a "Find an area" search, a browse route by country and indicator, and no chart, map or figure before input.

*Input-first as prediction.* Asking a reader to guess before they see is an input-first screen with a measured benefit, under conditions. With 378 people, 42 per condition, "participants who are prompted to reflect on their prior knowledge by predicting and self-explaining data outperform a control group in recall and comprehension"; on the main dataset — voting data the authors chose because it "was neither clearly familiar nor unfamiliar to participants" — the predict-and-explain condition "had the lowest absolute error relative to the None-Vis condition by -4.08 … 4.08% out of 100%" per recalled value; predicting alone helped less (−2.30); on a low-familiarity dataset explaining alone lowered errors by 1.78 and predicting-and-explaining by 1.27, while predicting alone "had no effect compared to the None condition"; and on a highly familiar one there were "no differences between any conditions", because "participants were already relatively accurate in estimating the values, such that the initial 'gap' is too small". Version 1's "4 percentage points" is not the paper's figure; 4.08 is, and the only "percentage points" on the page is a different comparison — visual conditions made "3.76 percentage points (36%) more errors on average per recalled value" than text. A guess before the reveal earns its place when it is followed by an explanation, when the data is not already familiar, and when the guess is skippable; alone, and on unfamiliar data, it bought nothing in the one test.

*Select-first and configure-first.* The one public index built on the reader's own weighting shows, on arrival, "Create your Better Life Index" and "Rate the topics according to their importance to you"; whether its countries are drawn before any rating could not be established. Its uptake can be — and it is evidence on how many readers configure when invited, not a test of a screen that shows nothing until they do: its designer reports "over six million visitors and tens of thousands of indexes shared"; the OECD reports "more than 60 000 of these indexes from some 180 countries and territories" on one page and "close to 130 000 users since 2011" in a working paper's abstract (the two figures could not be reconciled). On either, the share of visitors who configured is of the order of one or two in a hundred — the chapter's arithmetic across two sources, not a published figure. No study of preference elicitation before content was reached; the practitioner statement is the login wall's: demanding registration before content "has high interaction cost and defies the reciprocity principle". The rule version 1 drew from its audit — no configuration before meaning — stands as a reading with that evidence beneath it, and as the ONS's standard for charts.

*Exemplar-first.* No study of an exemplar opening for a data tool was reached. Chapter 3's anchoring evidence — the first-shown view shaped a fifth of the session and the final estimates — is the reason the pattern is chosen as an endorsement and said so.

*Map-first.* No study. What is known is from section 8 and Chapter 8: a map is the costliest first screen to load (over two-fifths of the script budget for the renderer) and the one on which small areas are missed by a third of readers and dark is read as more. A map that opens a dashboard opens on those terms.

A live case shows a landing being changed on evidence. The UKHSA data dashboard rewrote its first screen twice with public change notes: on 15 October 2024, "The homepage's layout is now grouped according to theme. You can now instantly see data for any outbreaks and respiratory viruses"; on 2 July 2026, "The landing page now features a Popular Topics section and a complete list of Health Topics that can be filtered and searched". On 6 October 2026 its first screen showed a sentence, summary cards with figures and "Up to [date]" stamps, and a beta banner. Read for its structure only, it is message-first with figures, a stamp beside each, and a path — the shape sections 3 and 8 describe.

### The path: one page, then tabs

The chapter's title promises a path, and the evidence on paths is thin and consistent. The pattern catalogue names the structures — "Single Page, Hierarchical, Parallel, Open, Semantic" — and the screen-space choices — "Screenfit, Overflow, Detail-on-demand, Parameterized, Multiple page" — with its one law that reducing one of "screenspace, abstraction, number of pages, interaction" raises another; the ONS says a dashboard need not fit one screen because "the user can scroll up and down"; the Analysis Function asks for "your most important headline content up front and detailed information in other tabs", to "Avoid internal and horizontal scrolling" and to "limit the number of clicks a user needs to get to the data they want"; and GOV.UK's own tabs component carries the warning that "Tabs hide content from users and not everyone will notice them or understand how they work", that "the first section is more relevant than the others for most users", and that the component "has not yet been tried in research with users". The study of sequence in narrative visualisation that would say how readers prefer to move between views could not be read. What the sources agree on is a shape: one page that scrolls, the sentence and its figure at the top, the rest below in order of importance, and tabs or further pages only for what most readers do not need — with the first tab the one that matters, because the others may not be noticed. Where a reader arrives by a cited address at a state deep in the path, that state is a first screen too, and its face sheet's first line applies.

### No configuration before meaning, restated

Three sentences from three sources say the same rule. The ONS, on charts: start "from a view that … shows the main message without needing any interaction". GOV.UK's start-page pattern, on services: a start point should "give the user just enough information to help them understand what the service does and whether it will meet their need" before any button. Chapter 1, from version 1's audit: none of ten instruments asked the reader to weight or configure anything at arrival (several asked for a place or a selection, which is a different thing). The reader is given something before anything is asked, and what they are given is the sentence.

## 4 · The authored account and the open door

The brief's Chapter 11 asks how "story and exploration" sit in one display. This section reports what the narrative-visualisation literature claims for itself, what its evidence actually shows, and what the house's own shape — a poster beside an open door — has beneath it.

### The vocabulary, and what its authors said about it

The taxonomy every later paper inherits is from 2010. "In total we analyzed 58 visualizations using the case study method"; the examples "were taken from online journalism (71%), business (20%), and visualization research (9%)"; seven genres — "magazine style, annotated chart, partitioned poster, flow chart, comic strip, slide show, and film/video/animation"; an axis from author-driven — "a strict linear path through the visualization, relies heavily on messaging, and includes no interactivity" — to reader-driven; and three common structures. "The Martini Glass visualization structure begins with an author-driven approach, initially using questions, observations, or written articles to introduce the visualization"; "Occasionally no text is used at all, as the visualization instead relies on an interesting default view or annotations"; "Once the author's intended narrative is complete, the visualization opens up to a reader-driven stage where the user is free to interactively explore the data"; "This structure is the most common across the interactive visualizations we examined". The interactive slideshow "incorporates interaction mid-narrative within the confines of each slide"; the drill-down "presents a general theme and then allows the user to choose among particular instances". And the authors' own statement of what the paper is: "our analysis has focused primarily on the design decisions made by visualization creators. A promising direction for future research is to focus squarely on readers' experiences when viewing and interacting with narrative visualizations"; "Further research is needed to characterize how readers perceive narratives presented via visualizations"; "we must acknowledge that we are not experts in the study of narrative". It is a design space, which is to say a menu.

The second vocabulary is the one for how a display argues. "We use the term rhetoric to refer to the set of processes by which intended meanings are represented in the visualization via a designer's choices and then shaped by individual end-user characteristics, contextual factors involving societal or cultural codes, and the end-user's interaction"; "We distinguish between four editorial layers that can be used to convey meaning, including the data, visual representation, textual annotations, and interactivity"; from "fifty-one professionally-produced narrative visualizations". Its categories include "information access rhetoric functioning to limit the amount of information presented, provenance rhetoric functioning to provide background information, mapping rhetoric … and procedural rhetoric functioning to constrain interaction over time", the last defined, after Bogost, as "the expression of meanings through rule-based representations and interactive functions", and within it the anchoring sentence Chapter 3 carries: "Default views provide an initial point of interpretation anchored to the default visual configuration". The sentence version 1 attributed to the paper — that defaults are a form of procedural rhetoric — is not in it; the category and the default-view sentence are, and the reading that joins them is the book's. The paper's own status claim is modest: "Our work sets the stage for such studies by providing a taxonomy". What the two vocabularies give a dashboard is a way to say what it is doing — which genre, which structure, which layer carries the argument — and no evidence that any choice is better than another; where section 4 later leans on the taxonomy, it leans on it as a description of what is common, not as a finding that it works.

### The null that could not be re-read

Version 1 rested its sequencing rule on three field experiments at a news site, and cited two figures for them (a session count and a share who followed the authored order) which this chapter withdraws, because the page they came from could not be reached. For this chapter the paper's full text could not be read, and neither figure appears in the abstract index that could be. What can be said at source is only what the abstract supports in approximate form: three web-based field experiments at a news and opinion outlet, testing whether introductory narrative techniques increase engagement with exploratory visualisations, finding, "In contrast to our hypotheses", that "augmenting visualizations with introductory 'stories' does not seem to increase user-engagement in exploration" (abstract †, via an index record; the abstract names "a popular news and opinion outlet, and a gallery website"). What other papers say of it, at source: a 2019 analytics paper calls it "a case study examining the effect of the presence of narrative text on users' engagement with visualizations in the wild"; a 2017 study reports that "Boy et al. showed that readers engage with all sections on a page but that this may not occur linearly"; and a 2023 systematic review of 184 publications notes that "some studies have found no impact on engagement when combining storytelling visualizations with exploratory data analysis". The chapter therefore carries the finding's direction from the abstract and the citing papers, withdraws version 1's numbers, and does not let a rule rest on it alone. Note what the abstract says and does not: that an introduction "does not seem to increase" exploration, not that it costs it; the house's stronger claim — that a stem the reader must drink through *costs* exploration — rests on the required-interaction evidence of section 6, not on this paper.

### What was measured, and what it showed

Four studies that could be read carry the section's weight, and each is reported with its limits.

*Linking and layout.* "Through a crowdsourced study with 180 participants", two layouts ("vertical and slideshow") and two linking conditions — "linking refers to a bidirectional interaction mode that explicitly highlights the explanatory visualization element when selecting narrative text and vice versa" — measured engagement, comprehension and recall (abstract †). "We found that participants performed significantly better in comprehension tasks with the slideshow layout"; "Participant recall was better with the slideshow layout under conditions with linking versus no linking" — an interaction, not a main effect of linking; "We also found that linking significantly increased user engagement"; and "linking and the slideshow layout were preferred by the participants". Per-cell numbers and effect sizes were not reachable.

*Titles against charts.* With 104 survey responses matched to US demographics, "the slant of the title influenced the perceived main message of a visualization", "with viewers deriving opposing messages from the same visualization", yet "viewing visualizations with slanted titles did not result in a statistically significant attitude change overall", and about four in five "reported the information as being neutral" whichever slant they saw. With 100 per condition, when title and chart disagreed, "people's recall of the visualization's message more frequently aligned with the titles than the visualization" — "133 out of 398" recalled messages matched the title, 68 the chart, and about half matched neither, naming only the topic — "visualizations were persistently perceived as impartial by the majority", "72-87%" calling the information neutral, and credibility fell as the two diverged: "Data, visualization, and title were all seen as less credible when there was a bigger visualization-title misalignment". And the limit of the title's power: with 2,168 readers and 43 line charts, "when the caption describes a low-prominence chart feature, readers rely on the chart and report a higher-prominence feature as the takeaway", while a caption that names what the chart already shows is "doubly emphasized" and becomes the takeaway. A sentence steers the reading when the picture agrees; a title that contradicts the picture is remembered over it, though most readers remember neither; a caption that points at a weak feature loses to the picture's strong one; and readers call all of it neutral.

*Text on the face.* "302 participants ranked univariate line charts containing varying amounts of text" — no text; title and one annotation; many annotations; a paragraph alone — and "heavily annotated charts were not penalized"; "participants preferred the charts with the largest number of textual annotations over charts with fewer annotations or text alone"; 14 per cent "ranked the all-text option as their first choice". What the text did: annotations that named the encodings "had almost no effect"; those carrying statistics or outside context "had the greatest influence on the participants' takeaways", a reader given outside context in an annotation being "6.1x as likely" to report it. The authors' guidelines: "Rather than aiming for maximally minimalist design, annotate charts with relevant text"; "Higher quantities of annotations are not penalized for the 'clutter' they may cause"; "Perceptual content relating to patterns or trends should be positioned in the title", statistics and context "by a relevant point"; "Consider a text-only variant that can stand alone". Their limits: tidy synthetic charts with "abrupt peaks and trends, which is not often the case in practice", and "Future work should explore how readers integrate text with other chart types, including dashboards". A smaller study points the same way: with 24 people and a ten-second exposure, "Decluttering designs led to higher ratings on professionalism" but not on relevance, while "adding focus to the design led to higher ratings on aesthetics and clarity, and better memory for the highlighted pattern" — one highlight and one sentence did what stripping did not, though some called the focused charts "pushy". The ONS's own annotation rule pulls the other way on length and on the phone: "Keep annotations concise. Limit them to around 50 characters (10 to 12 words) and a single sentence"; "Annotations may not display on mobile devices in certain chart templates"; "Make sure any essential information you include in annotations is also included in the main text or footnotes" (all read once). The tension is real and the chapter records it: the evidence says more text on the face is wanted and works; the template says it may vanish on the device most readers use. The resolution is the ONS's own last sentence — whatever is essential in an annotation is also in the text.

*Flow.* With 240 people reading one scrolling piece, "Regarding the stepper versus scroller debate, we did not find a significant difference in engagement via our questionnaire"; "Overall, participants comprehended the story well, scoring on average 4 out of 5. We did not find any major differences in comprehension across condition pairs"; the engagement gain belonged to "visuals and navigation feedback (e.g., static vs. animated transitions)", not to scrolling; and "this could be localized to this particular story". Chapter 3's summary of this study — engagement up, comprehension flat — is corrected here on the first half. The one controlled recall test of a format called scrollytelling, with 381 people and four media, found that "scrollytelling and video lead to a significantly more extensive recall than audio and in part text media. However, when considering exposure time, text turns out to be the most efficient medium" — recall .65 against text's .61, at 355 seconds against 275 — and its stimulus was "a 529-word text interrupted by an information graphic and three video clips", not a chart-driven scroller; its sample was German, three-quarters female, recruited through social networks. The practitioners who made the form say what they know: "Whether scrollytelling increases interest and comprehension is up for debate, but it doesn't hide content." The field's own review: "There is limited empirical evaluation of the effectiveness of interactive articles", "large publishers are unwilling to share internal metrics, and laboratory studies may not generalize to real world reading trends", "It is also important to remember that not everything needs to be interactive", and "More research is needed in order to identify the cases in which interactivity is worth the cost of creation".

### The principle, and the house's shape

The oldest sentence in the explorable-explanations tradition is from 2011: "the filter description works as a static explanation -- it can be read like normal text. The reader is not forced to interact in order to learn. The reader interacts if they wants to go deeper, if they have piqued curiosity or unanswered questions" (the grammar is the page's); "There are no UI elements screaming for attention. The reader is not transported off to a separate 'interactive' context. Instead, the reader simply nudges the examples that the author has already presented." Version 1 drew from this and from the null a house shape it called a poster beside an open door: an authored layer that is short, front-loaded and complete in itself; an exploratory layer reachable from the first screen; each linked to the other at every step. The evidence this chapter could read gives the shape three mechanisms and takes nothing from it. The taxonomy's most common structure may open on "an interesting default view" rather than a stem of text, which is the poster. Linking a sentence to its view raised engagement, which is the door's hinge. And a title steers the reading only when the chart agrees with it, which is the reason the poster says what the view shows and nothing more. The null, had it been readable in full, would have supplied the fourth — that an authored introduction does not buy exploration — and section 6's required-interaction evidence supplies the stronger claim that a toll before the figure costs readers. It is carried as a bet, recorded as one, and the shape stands on the three that were read.

## 5 · Written sentences at scale

Chapter 1 took from version 1 the rule that a dashboard's first screen is a sentence; a dashboard for every place in a region needs a sentence for every place. This section reports the one official method for that at scale, the newsroom method it borrows from, and what readers make of text they know to be generated.

### The official precedent, in its makers' words

For Census 2021 the ONS published an article for every local authority in England and Wales. Its own account: "Interactive maps, charts and dashboard are all wonderful ways of communicating data, but we need to pull out the narrative and the story to help guide our users through the numbers." "Theo Jolliffe and Tim Kershaw created 331 individual pages – one for each local authority area of England and Wales." "Each article was different according to what the data told us. This was no 'fill in the blanks' exercise. The story would highlight notable differences in the area compared with others." "Checking the articles took time and care from the Census Outputs team and our content designers." "Semi-automation is not a labour-saving tool. It doesn't mean we can do things more quickly. It means we can do things we never could before." "Within a few days of publication in December 2022, every individual area's page had been read." The tools were "analysis in Python, front end development in Svelte, data visualisation in d3.js and old-fashioned journalism". The pages carry their own account: "The articles have been written and semi-automated by our data journalists and are a new publishing format for the Office for National Statistics"; "Topics are chosen and ordered automatically based on how relevant they are for each area, however all data are checked before publishing"; "There is an article like this for every local authority district in England and Wales". The published tooling is "A library of utilities to make Natural Language Generation (NLG) for semi-automated journalism a bit easier", which renders templates "into structured JSON for each place" with helper functions that turn numbers into approximations, described changes, ranks and fractions; no large language model is involved. The method is rule-and-template generation from a table, with topics ranked by relevance per place, checked by people, and labelled on the page.

### The newsroom method, read for its mechanics

The research-institute guide to automated journalism describes the same four steps for the newsroom: the software "collects available data", "algorithms employ statistical methods to identify important and interesting events in the data", the software "classifies and prioritizes the identified insights by importance", and "arranges the newsworthy elements by following predefined rules to generate a narrative"; "trained journalists pre-write text modules and sample stories with the appropriate frames and language and adjust them to the official style guide of the publishing outlet". Its condition: "Automated journalism works for fact-based stories for which clean, structured, and reliable data are available". Its scale and its error record, as reported: "the automation allowed for the generation of more than 3,000 stories per quarter, compared to about three hundred stories that AP reporters and editors previously created manually"; an AP executive "reports that automation has decreased the rate of errors in AP's company earning reports from about seven percent to only about one percent, mostly by eliminating typos or transposed digits" — the source reporting a claim. And its failure case, which is the case a dashboard should keep: a July 2015 report "wrongly reported that the company missed expectations and that the share price had fallen by seventy-one percent since the beginning of the year when, in fact, it had more than doubled during that period. The reason for the error was that the algorithm failed to realize that the Netflix stock underwent a seven-to-one split". A generated sentence is only as true as the slot it fills, and a change in the series it does not know about becomes a confident falsehood. The guide's limit is the one to print beside any generated line: "Algorithms cannot ask questions, explain new phenomena, or establish causality". The label the AP used was one sentence: "This story was generated by Automated Insights using data from Zacks Investment Research."

### What readers make of generated text

A meta-analysis of "12 studies with a total of 4,473 participants" found "no difference in readers' perceptions of credibility, a small advantage for human-written news in terms of quality, and a huge advantage for human-written news with respect to readability" — credibility d = 0.0, quality d = 0.5, readability d = 2.8 — and that "participants provided higher ratings for credibility, quality, and readability simply when they were told that they were reading a human-written article". The texts were short, routine, data-driven stories in sport and finance; none was a statistical-area narrative. The finding for a dashboard is two-sided: generated sentences are believed as much as written ones, and read far less willingly; and telling the reader which is which changes the rating. The house's own rule (Chapter 6) that a templated claim is still a claim is joined by a second: a generated sentence is labelled as generated, in the ONS's manner, and written in the house's voice by the people who write the templates, because the readability gap is the template's to close.

### What this settles for the sentence per place

The method exists, it is official, it is open, and its maker says what it costs: it is "not a labour-saving tool", every page is checked, and its purpose is to do "things we never could before". A dashboard that promises every place its own sentence inherits that cost. It also inherits the method's shape — topics ranked per place, phrases built by rule from numbers, a human check, a label — and Chapter 6's discipline for every generated claim: recipe, base, boundary, and the series break it must know about before it speaks.

## 6 · What an interaction must earn

Version 1 said every interaction starts owing and pays with a named purchase. This section reports the taxonomies that say what interactions exist, the framework that names what they cost, the measured cases, the standards that govern hover and touch, and what the phone removes.

### The taxonomies, and what their authors claim for them

The best-known map of interactions for visual analysis has "12 task types grouped into three high-level categories": "data and view specification (visualize, filter, sort, and derive); view manipulation (select, navigate, coordinate, and organize)", and "analysis process and provenance (record, annotate, share, and guide)". Its rows describe the last four as "Record analysis histories for revisitation, review, and sharing", "Annotate patterns to document findings", "Share views and annotations to enable collaboration", "Guide users through analysis tasks or stories". Version 1 said the authors called the taxonomy descriptive and not prescriptive; they do not use those words. What they say is: "Validating and evolving this taxonomy is a community project that proceeds through feedback, critique, and refinement"; "by attempting to provide an abstracted picture of a domain, taxonomies may be incomplete"; "these examples do not constitute an exhaustive survey". The earlier taxonomy by intent — "Select: mark something as interesting"; "Explore: show me something else"; "Abstract/Elaborate: show me more or less detail"; "Connect: show me related items"; and reconfigure, encode, filter — came from a survey of "59 papers and 51 systems" yielding "311 individual interaction techniques", and its authors said both that interaction "has garnered a relatively low level of attention from the Infovis community" and that "the categories are not collectively exhaustive". The field's attempt at a definition calls itself "a thinking tool" (abstract †). None of the three is evidence that any interaction is needed; each is a completeness check, and version 1's use of the first as one — noting that the process-and-provenance group is where evidence displays under-build — stands.

### What an interaction costs

The cost side has a framework and no sizes. From "484 papers", "61 interaction-related usability problems reported in 32 user studies" were placed in "seven costs": "(1) Decision costs to form goals; (2) System-power costs to form system operations; (3) Multiple input mode costs to form physical sequences; (4) Physical-motion costs to execute sequences; (5) Visual-cluttering costs to perceive state; (6) View-change costs to interpret perception; (7) State-change costs to evaluate interpretation" (abstract †; the author describes interaction cost as "poorly understood"). The framework names costs; it does not weigh them. The weighing comes from the measured cases.

*The largest direct test.* Two crowdsourced experiments, 530 and 2,149 recruited, on a Bayesian reasoning task read from icon arrays, comparing a static chart against checkbox filters, drag-and-drop, hover-linking and tooltips. "adding interaction to a static Bayesian reasoning visualization does not improve participants' accuracy on a Bayesian reasoning task"; "In some cases, it can significantly detract from it"; "We find no statistically significant difference in accuracy between participants using different interaction techniques"; "interaction is not as unambiguously good as we often believe"; "a well designed static visualization can be as, if not more, effective than an interactive one"; the authors recommend "a cautious use of interactivity in cases where it does not add additional information to a visualization", and suggest the added controls "may have induced an extraneously high cognitive load". Use of the control when offered: "Out of the 246 participants assigned to cbAll, only 43% used the checkboxes on the visualization"; "out of the 371 participants assigned to cbAll, only 44% actually interacted". And the sentence that is quoted most and should be quoted least: "Seventy percent of the participants who interacted with the visualization answered correctly, while only 40% of those who did not interact answered correctly" — which the authors then confound: "No interaction could be indicative of participants who were either confused about the task or were simply clicking through to get paid", and "future work is needed to isolate if this is an artifact of participant engagement, or an effect of interacting". People who interact do better; interaction does not make people do better. The two sentences are different and a dashboard's analytics will show the first and tempt the second.

*A required interaction.* "A total of 3371 members of a demographically diverse Internet panel" chose between two treatments from static pictographs or interactive ones they had to build. "The interactive graphic conditions had significantly worse survey completion rates (1110/1695, 65.5% vs 1316/1659, 79.3%, P < .001) than the static image conditions"; "respondents who viewed interactive graphs were less likely to select the dominant treatment option (234/380, 61.6% vs 343/465, 73.8%, P < .001) in the strong dominance condition"; "Interactivity, however visually appealing, can add to respondent burden and distract people from understanding relevant statistical information" (abstract †; health risk graphics, 2011). A toll before the figure lost fourteen points of completion and twelve of correct choice.

*A guided introduction.* "We performed a between-subject experiment with 596 participants in total" — three studies summed — found "there are no significant differences between the answer correctness of the questions with and without onboarding" for four chart types, a gain only for one unfamiliar form (read once), readers who "may not take enough time with the onboarding concept itself to carefully read through the text" and who "were overconfident about their ability to solve problems", and a comment the authors quote: "for highly familiar visualization types no onboarding is needed". In the third study eight students said the tutorial was "not needed and that they just started working". A dashboard-tour system since built lets readers switch "to a free exploration at any time", was tried with "six end users", "All users responded positively", and had no baseline; its authors name the problem — "A primary challenge in measuring the effectiveness of an onboarding experience is the subjectivity of learning".

*Detail on request.* The usability tradition's definition: "Initially, show users only a few of the most important options", "Offer a larger set of specialized options upon request"; it "defers advanced or rarely used features to a secondary screen, making applications easier to learn and less error-prone". Its two conditions, in the original's words rather than version 1's: "You must get the right split between initial and secondary features", and "It must be obvious how users progress from the primary to the secondary disclosure levels"; "You have to disclose everything that users frequently need up front"; "designs that go beyond 2 disclosure levels typically have low usability because users often get lost when moving between the levels". It is expert opinion from 2006 with one cited study. The one experiment on staged detail and trust, in a different domain — an emotion-from-text predictor, 74 analysed and twelve interviewed — found that more detail raised what people expected before use ("Participants anticipated greater accuracy for the word-level system") and not their trust after it ("no overall differences in trust after using the two different system versions"); preference split evenly; "incremental feedback is distracting and undermines simple heuristics users form about system operation"; "users may benefit from initially simplified feedback that hides potential system errors"; and the authors' principle: "advanced information and explanation is provided on an 'as needed' basis", with "a 'How was this rating calculated?' button" as the example. The public design systems record their own evidence on hiding. GOV.UK's details component: "There is evidence that some users avoid clicking the link to show more details, as they think it will take them away from the page"; "Do not use the details component to hide information that the majority of your users will need". Its accordion: "Accordions hide content from the user. Not all users will notice them or understand how they work"; "Do not use an accordion for content that all users need to see". Its tabs: "Tabs hide content from users and not everyone will notice them or understand how they work", and "This component has not yet been tried in research with users". The NHS's expander: "We tested several expanders in our information about type 1 diabetes where users felt overwhelmed by the amount of information"; "They tested well and seemed to meet users' emotional needs"; "there's a risk that expanders will distract users". So the split that section 4's evidence and this one agree on: text that carries the message goes on the face, where it is wanted and steers; detail that most readers do not need goes one step away, with the step obvious; and a hidden thing that the majority needs is a defect.

*Search.* The one added control with a measured welcome is the one that lets a reader find themselves. With 830 people, search beside a full display was used by two-thirds to four-fifths in four of five cases, the data sought "was often deeply personal", and time with data items rose (all read once). Its purchase is personal relevance, which is Chapter 2's finding about what turns a decision and the first row of version 1's table.

### The purchase list, re-priced

Version 1 listed seven purchases in a table: personalisation, collection, chosen comparison, adjacency, reweighting, filter and drill, prediction before reveal. This chapter's evidence re-prices each. *Personalisation* (enter your place) has the search evidence and Chapter 2 behind it; it is the one interaction readers reach for. *Collection* (place your mark) is Chapter 9's human dot plot, weakened there as a measure and kept as a form, and Chapter 10's contribution, with its lawful basis and withdrawal tiers. *Chosen comparison* (choose your peers) is Chapter 7's declared-peers rule made a control; its cost is Lam's decision cost, and its guard is that the default peers are an endorsement and say so. *Adjacency* ("close to your taste, new to you") is a recommendation, and Chapter 10 found that a recommendation built on who a reader is runs into the regulator's inference test and PECR's "not about who uses it"; its purchase is real and its basis must be chosen first. *Reweighting* has the one public index's record — tens of thousands of indexes against millions of visitors — and Chapter 5's finding that the default weighting is itself the index. *Filter and drill* is Chapter 10's table builder, with every query-time rule attached. *Prediction before reveal* has about four points per hundred when the guess is followed by an explanation, on data of middling familiarity, one to two on unfamiliar data, and nothing on familiar; it is skippable and visual, and its guess is collectable only under Chapter 9's and Chapter 10's rules. Two purchases this chapter adds: *linking* (a sentence to its view and back), which raised engagement; and *sharing a state* (section 7), which the taxonomy files under provenance and the evidence on dashboards files under "a jumping off point for questions" and "the 'data dump'". And the rule survives as a procedure: an interaction names its row and its cost, or it is cut; the static reading works first; and what the analytics later show about who used it is read with the Bayesian study's caution.

### Hover, touch, keyboard and size

The standard that governs content on hover is Level AA and its three conditions are exact. Where hover or focus "triggers additional content to become visible and then hidden" (read once): *dismissible* — "A mechanism is available to dismiss the additional content without moving pointer hover or keyboard focus" ("unless the additional content communicates an input error or does not obscure or replace other content"); *hoverable* — "the pointer can be moved over the additional content without the additional content disappearing"; *persistent* — "The additional content remains visible until the hover or focus trigger is removed, the user dismisses it", "or its information is no longer valid". The W3C's reasons: such content "often leads to accessibility issues"; "the user may not have intended to trigger the interaction"; "the user may not know new content has appeared"; "the new content may interfere with a user's ability to do a task"; for magnification users, "The magnified view generally follows the mouse pointer or keyboard focus" and "the pointer can obscure a significant area of the additional content"; "Users with disabilities may require more time for many reasons"; and "Content which can be triggered via pointer hover should also be able to be triggered by keyboard focus". The word "touch" does not appear on that page. The touch case against hover rests on two other things: the practitioners — "Because tooltips are initiated by a hover gesture, they can be used only on devices with a mouse or keyboard"; "They are not normally available on touchscreens"; "Tooltips are hard to discover because they often lack visual cues"; "Don't use tooltips for information that is vital to task completion" — and the sister criterion on dragging, which says "This requirement is separate from keyboard accessibility because people using a touchscreen device may not use a physical keyboard". No measured study of tooltip discovery or use was reached; the one experiment with a tooltip condition found no accuracy gain and did not report how many hovered.

Three further criteria bind every control on a dashboard. Keyboard, Level A: "All functionality of the content is operable through a keyboard interface without requiring specific timings for individual keystrokes". Pointer gestures, Level A: "All functionality that uses multipoint or path-based gestures for operation can be operated with a single pointer without a path-based gesture", with the W3C's own example a map whose pinch-to-zoom has "plus/minus buttons". Dragging, Level AA and new in 2.2: "All functionality that uses a dragging movement for operation can be achieved by a single pointer without dragging", because "Some people cannot perform dragging movements in a precise manner" — a map "has up/down/left/right buttons to move the view as well". And size, Level AA and new in 2.2: "The size of the target for pointer inputs is at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels, except when" undersized targets are spaced so that 24-pixel circles centred on them "do not intersect another target", or the target has an equivalent elsewhere, is inline, is set by the user agent, or is essential — and the W3C's explanation of "essential" names the chart: "If there are many pins close together, the spacing between pins and neighboring pins will often be below 24 CSS pixels"; "It is essential to show the pins at the correct map location, therefore the Essential exception applies"; "A similar example is an interactive data visualization where targets are necessarily dense"; and then, "authors are strongly encouraged to provide equivalent functionality through alternative means to the extent practical". The ladder of target sizes, for the record: 24 CSS pixels at AA; 44 at AAA ("at least 44 by 44 CSS pixels"); Apple's "at least 44 points x 44 points"; Google's "48x48dp results in a physical size of about 9mm"; and the one lab study, twenty right-handed people recruited in a computer-science department, on a 2005 handheld, "9.2 mm for discrete tasks and targets of 9.6 mm for serial tasks". A dense chart may lawfully fall under the floor; the equivalent route — a table, a list, a search — is what the standard asks for in return, and it is Chapter 8's table twin by another name.

The public design systems have drawn their own conclusion. None of GOV.UK's, the NHS's or the ONS's has a tooltip component. The ONS exempts tooltip-only charts from its definition of "interactive", and its own chart components make the tooltip reachable without a mouse — "Use the Tab key to move focus into the chart. Once inside, use the arrow keys to navigate between data points"; "As you move, tooltips will be announced to describe each point" — with "A url for a fallback image to display instead of the chart or iframe visualisation if JavaScript is disabled" and "Download options displayed below the chart". That is the house's figures-before-script and table twin in a national design system's words. The Analysis Function's chart guidance "does not cover interactive visualisations", and its dashboard guidance says "Dashboards rarely meet full accessibility requirements".

### What the small screen removes

The corpus evidence on what happens to a data display on a phone is consistent. From "53 news articles gathered from twelve sources, to produce a corpus of 231 visualization examples": "The most common action in our corpus is to remove content from the mobile visualization"; removing elements was three times commoner than adding; and "many visualizations removed the interactivity completely from the mobile version rather than redesigning the interactive capabilities". From "378 public facing visualizations" in large and small versions, "76 design patterns" and a named trade-off — "a density-message trade-off where authors seek to balance goals of maintaining graphical density with those of preserving the message or intended takeaways" — with hover labels and tooltips among the first things dropped (read once). The modelling paper that followed states the danger: "transformations can alter relationships or patterns implied by the large screen view, requiring authors to reason carefully about what information to preserve while adjusting their design for the smaller display". And the two phone experiments: "With results from 87 crowd workers", linear layouts were read in "between 2.8 and 5.4 seconds" against "3.3 and 6.9 seconds" for radial ones, with similar errors; "96 crowdworker participants performed nine trend comparison tasks", and "those using a small multiples design consistently completed tasks in less time, albeit with slightly less confidence than those using an animated design". The chapter's rule from this is that the phone is the first version and not a lesser one, because it is the device most readers use, it has no hover, and what is removed for it is what the maker has decided is not the message. Design the small screen first and the message survives; design the large one first and the phone gets whatever was left.

## 7 · Citable states

Version 1's rule — every reachable state has a stable, citable address — rested on the taxonomy's provenance group and the house's ambition that a council paper could point at a view. This section gives it an evidence base, which turns out to be large and mostly about decay.

### Why a dashboard is cited

The dashboard studies say what readers do: "it's really a jumping off point for questions"; "By far the most common tool switch was the 'data dump'". Chapter 2 found that the official reader acts on documents and that a figure travels into a paper; Chapter 10 found that a figure, once published and read, cannot be withdrawn. Every one of those is a citation of a state — a view, on a day, with its filters set — and a dashboard that changes under the citation has either misled the citer or made a liar of them.

### How addresses decay

The founding statement is from 1998: "What makes a cool URI? A cool URI is one which does not change. What sorts of URI change? URIs don't change: people change them." "There are no reasons at all in theory for people to change URIs (or stop maintaining documents), but millions of reasons in practice." The excuses it lists are the ones a studio will hear from itself: "We just reorganized our website to make it better."; "I didn't think URLs have to be persistent - that was URNs."; "We would like to, but we just don't have the right tools." And the duty: "It is the the duty of a Webmaster to allocate URIs which you will be able to stand by in 2 years, in 20 years, in 200 years" (the doubled word is the page's).

The decay is measured. In legal citation: "more than 70% of the URLs within the above mentioned journals, and 50% of the URLs within U.S. Supreme Court opinions suffer reference rot"; "Our finding is that 49.9% of the links cited in the Supreme Court opinions no longer had the cited material"; in three law reviews, "only 29.9% of the HRJ links, 26.8% of the HLR links, and 34.2% of the JOLT links contained the material cited". The study's own two words matter for a dashboard: "Link rot refers to the URL no longer serving up any content at all. Reference rot, an even larger phenomenon, happens when a link still works but the information referenced by the citation is no longer present, or has changed". In scholarship: "For over one million references to web resources extracted from over 3.5 million articles", "We find one out of five STM articles suffering from reference rot", rising to "seven out of ten" among articles that cite the web at all; and of the content behind links that still work, "We find that for over 75% of references the content has drifted away from what it was when referenced" — "184,065 out of 241,091, which equals 76.35%" — with "representative snapshots" existing "for about 30% of all URI references". On the web at large, as of October 2023, "A quarter of all webpages that existed at one point between 2013 and 2023 are no longer accessible"; "38% of webpages that existed in 2013 are not available today, compared with 8% of pages that existed in 2023"; "Overall, 21% of all the government webpages we examined contained at least one broken link" (US pages; no UK equivalent was found); and "54% of Wikipedia pages contain at least one link in their 'References' section that points to a page that no longer exists". Content drift, not link rot, is the dashboard's problem: the address works and the number has changed.

The national safety net does not catch it. The UK Government Web Archive "started archiving in 2003", has "archived over 6,000 websites" at "around 100 a month", and says of what it can hold: "Interactive content usually cannot be archived." "Some examples would be: interactive maps, site search, contact forms, and some interactive animations." A dashboard is outside the archive of record unless it makes its own states archivable. The vocabulary for doing so is a standard: Memento "bridges the present and past Web", "facilitates obtaining representations of prior states of a given resource by introducing datetime negotiation and TimeMaps", and defines the thing a citable state is — "A Memento for an Original Resource is a resource that encapsulates a prior state of the Original Resource" — reached through "A TimeGate … capable of datetime negotiation".

### What a citation of data needs

The data-citation principles were written for datasets, and their seventh is written for dashboards. "Data should be considered legitimate, citable products of research"; "whenever and wherever a claim relies upon data, the corresponding data should be cited"; "A data citation should include a persistent method for identification that is machine actionable, globally unique, and widely used by a community"; "Unique identifiers, and metadata describing the data, and its disposition, should persist — even beyond the lifespan of the data they describe"; and — *Specificity and Verifiability* — "Data citations should facilitate identification of, access to, and verification of the specific data that support a claim", "sufficient to facilitate verifying that the specific timeslice, version and/or granular portion of data retrieved subsequently is the same as was originally cited". The registration agency's practice: "For major content changes, we recommend assigning a new DOI and linking the new DOI to the previous DOI with related identifiers"; "For minor content changes, the same DOI may be used with updated metadata". The UK curation guide's three ways to cite a changing dataset: "With the time slice approach, the citable entity is the set of updates made to a dataset during a particular time period"; "At regular intervals or at the request of a citing author, a snapshot is taken of the entire dataset and made citable"; "The citable entity is the result of a particular query on a dataset run at a certain time" — and "The recommendation of the Research Data Alliance Working Group on Data Citation (RDA WGDC) is however to use partial snapshots". "If a dataset exists in several versions, be sure to cite the exact version you used."

The Research Data Alliance's short recommendation is the nearest thing to a specification for a citable dashboard state, and their headings are reproduced because each is a design decision: R1 Data Versioning; R2 Timestamping; R3 Query Store Facilities; R4 Query Uniqueness; R5 Stable Sorting; R6 Result Set Verification; R7 Query Timestamping; R8 Query PID; R9 Store Query; R10 Automated Citation Texts; R11 Landing Page; R12 Machine Actionability; R13 Technology Migration; R14 Migration Verification. In the authors' words: "Apply versioning to ensure earlier states of data sets can be retrieved"; "Ensure that operations on data are timestamped, i.e. any additions, deletions are marked with a timestamp"; "Provide means for storing queries and the associated metadata in order to re-execute them in the future"; "Ensure that the sorting of the records in the data set is unambiguous and reproducible"; "Compute fixity information (checksum) of the query result set"; "Assign a new PID to the query if either the query is new or if the result set returned from an earlier identical query is different due to changes in the data"; "Generate citation texts in the format prevalent in the designated community for lowering the barrier for citing the data"; "Make the PIDs resolve to a human readable landing page". And the principle: "Instead of providing static data exports or textual descriptions of data subsets, we support a dynamic, query centric view of data sets", "identifying data sets by storing and assigning persistent identifiers (PIDs) to timestamped queries that can be re-executed against the timestamped data store". A dashboard's filter is a query; its state is a query's result on a day; and the recommendations say what makes that result citable — version the data, timestamp the operations, store the query, identify it, checksum the result, resolve it to a page a person can read, and generate the citation.

### How public dashboards do it

*The fullest working model.* On 6 October 2026 a chart page at Our World in Data carried the reader's selection in its address — the page's own canonical URL for a filtered view read `?country=~GBR&time=1950..2020`, and its filtered download carried the same — offered "Download full data" and "Download displayed data" ("Includes only the entities and time points currently visible in the chart"), stated "Last updated" and "Next expected update", and gave two citations: one for the page, one abbreviated "If you have limited space (e.g. in data visualizations)". The citation did not point at the live page. It ended "Retrieved October 6, 2026 from https://archive.ourworldindata.org/20260910-005924/grapher/life-expectancy.html", "archived on September 10, 2026"; the archived page carried a banner with the archive time and links to "the previous version", "the next version" and "the live version". The organisation's own account of the change, July 2025: "Our citation instructions now point to archived versions of charts, to ensure that any data points you are referring to in the cited chart remain stable and unchanged"; "The new archived versions, in contrast, will never change or update"; an archived embed "will always show the exact data, text, and other content as it does on the day of embedding; it is frozen in time", "will not be deleted", and "may contain outdated data that has since been updated in the live version of the chart, or data that has since been found to contain quality issues"; a live embed "will likely change in the future" and "in rare cases may be deleted". Three layers, then: the selection in the address; a dated, unchanging snapshot of data and text; a generated citation naming the snapshot and the retrieval date. And one gap the chapter records: the archive URL in the citation drops the reader's selection, so the data and the text are frozen and the view is not.

*The official statistics convention.* An ONS dataset page lists "All versions" — nine, each dated, numbered "Version: n", downloadable, the latest marked, earlier ones labelled "Corrections" with "View superseded version" links — and the office's policy says what the labels mean: "Revisions are updates to previously published statistics, analysis or data that improve quality by incorporating improved methods, additional data sources or statistics that were unavailable at the point of initial publication"; "Correction of errors are amendments that are made to published statistics in response to the identification of mistakes following their initial publication"; "Correction of errors are reported at the top of the affected release in a blue collapsible banner titled 'Correction'"; and "The fact that the publication has been updated is clearly stated with details of when and why the change was made". The office's area tool puts the area in the address path and its code in every outward link (read once). GOV.UK's convention keeps the address and marks the content: a withdrawn page stays "on the site, but it does not appear in internal search results and there'll be a banner to it to explain that the content is no longer current"; a live example reads "This publication was withdrawn on 24 February 2022" above "Updates to this page" with every dated change down to "First published"; and the office's own policy, above, is that a correction is announced "at the top of the affected release" with "when and why" — an official statistic is never silently replaced. The health agency's dashboard puts the area in the address, offers "Tabular data" and "Download" beside every chart, stamps "Last updated" and "Up to and including", publishes an API "to easily extract and save data", and offers no snapshot and no citation block.

*The pattern's origin and its snag.* State-as-URL was described in a 2007 collaborative visualisation system: "The browser's location bar always displays a URL that links to the current state of the visualization, defined by the settings of filtering, navigation, and visual encoding parameters"; "the browser's back and forward buttons are tied to the visualization state"; and its known flaw — "These different parameter settings result in different URLs, and yet provide exactly the same visualization view" — so two addresses can name one state and address equality is not state equality. The practitioners' documentation adds the hard limit: "Some browsers have a limit of about 2,000 characters for the length of a URL, so if the bookmark URL is longer than that, it will not work properly in those browsers", and the alternative, "saving the state to the server".

### What a citable state is, then

From the standards, the measurements and the one working model: a state is citable when its address encodes the selection in a canonical order (so that one state has one address); when the data and text behind it are a dated, unchanging snapshot that the address or the citation names (because links rot and content drifts); when the snapshot can be found from the live view and the live view from the snapshot (previous, next, live); when the citation text is generated, carrying the canonical address with its selection, the snapshot date, the data version and the retrieval date, so that the frozen data and the chosen view travel together and nobody writes it by hand; and when what is shared or cropped from it — the house's crop test — carries its provenance, its floor and its date with it. The official reader will paste it into a paper; the paper will outlive the series; and the only question the maker can control is whether the paper's reader, a year on, finds what the citer saw.

## 8 · How a display reads from the data layer

The brief's last clause for this chapter is how a display "reads from the data layer and responds to it". This section states the architecture that makes a state reproducible, then asks what of the seven records sits on a face, what a gap looks like, how a felt distribution and a map are drawn, and what the trust evidence says about the furniture.

### A view is a function

The grammar of graphics names the separation. "A grammar of graphics is a tool that enables us to concisely describe the components of a graphic"; "Data are obviously a critical part of the plot, but it is important to remember that they are independent from the other components: we can construct a graphic that can be applied to multiple datasets"; "Data are what turns an abstract graphic into a concrete graphic". The interactive grammar makes the view a compiled object: "A unit specification describes a single Cartesian plot, with a backing data set, a given mark-type, and a set of one or more encoding definitions" — "unit := (data, transforms, mark-type, encodings)" — and interaction a parameter of it: "In Vega-Lite, a selection is an abstraction that defines input event processing, points of interest, and a predicate function for inclusion testing"; "Selections parameterize visual encodings by serving as input data, defining scale extents, or by driving conditional logic"; "Once selections are defined, they parameterize visual encodings to make them interactive — visual encodings are automatically re-evaluated as selections change". The document-binding library states the lowest layer: "With D3, designers selectively bind input data to arbitrary document elements"; "The data operator binds input data to selected nodes"; "The delineation of enter, update and exit allows precise control of the element lifecycle". The 2007 system defined the state as "the settings of filtering, navigation, and visual encoding parameters".

Put together — and the putting together is the chapter's, since no one source says it — a view is a function of three things: a data version, a specification (transforms, marks, encodings, the words that go with them), and a selection. Change any one and the view changes; fix all three and the view is reproducible. That is why section 7's state can be cited at all, why Chapter 8's degraded states are states and not errors (the data version is "last good copy, aged"), why Chapter 10's query-time rules attach to the selection and not to the chart, and why the house's data contract — the repository's own rules for its data — is the first of the three and not an appendix to them. A dashboard that cannot say which data version, which specification and which selection made a view cannot say what the view is.

### What of each record sits on the face

Seven chapters each left a record and each asked how much of it belongs on the face. The evidence on the question is thin, mixed and worth stating exactly.

*Metadata on a chart.* Two crowdsourced experiments, 64 and 144 people, four real-world charts, six kinds of metadata (source, cleaning, perceptual challenges, encoding explanation, creators, intended audience — read once): "visualizations with metadata were perceived as more thorough than those without metadata, but similarly relevant, accurate, clear, and complete"; "metadata did not impact the accuracy of the information extracted from visualizations, but may have influenced which information participants remembered as important or interesting"; readers thought the annotated charts likelier to have been "chosen for a presentation to policymakers". The sentence "metadata significantly increased trust" is not in the paper. *Sources revealed.* In 42 interviews, "17 of 42 participants changed their rankings after the sources of the data visualizations were revealed" and "25 of 42 participants opted to keep their initial rankings"; the charts moved up "all came from academic or government sources that were heavily criticized in the first set of rankings"; "The most recurring theme in our analysis were decisions framed or driven by personal experience"; and the authors' caveat, "we must consider how political biases and beliefs may negate the impact of even the most lauded institutions". *"Disclosure" and choice.* In a workshop paper with 77 crowd workers, "perceived clarity, amount of disclosure. and thoroughness significantly predicted individuals' selection of a Google Maps-like application with either less information or more information" (the stray full stop is the page's), while "Trust and perceived accuracy did not significantly predict which navigation application visualization participants decided to use" (it tested four transparency dimensions, not source cues). *The factors.* A position paper lists what makes information trustworthy — "accuracy (free from error), currency (up-to-dateness), coverage (completeness), objectivity …" — and says "Disclosure of the underlying data and the designers' decisions about data, representation, and interaction can increase trustworthiness", while warning that readers "might also use superficial cues as indicators for trust". *The superficial cue, named.* The critical study of conventions: "We focus on four conventions which imbue visualisations with a sense of objectivity, transparency and facticity" — "two-dimensional viewpoints", "clean layouts", "geometric shapes and lines", "the inclusion of data sources" — and of the last, "It suggests that the designer feels confident that s/he has been truthful to the data, and this in turn suggests that there may be no need for the user to check", since "People who are not specialists in data are unlikely to be able make sense of raw data anyway". *Beauty.* "Across five-preregistered studies (3,405 participants, 1,219 graphs)", "graph beauty predicts increased trust in real-world graphs across three important domains", making a graph more beautiful "causally increased trust", and "This effect was fully mediated by people's inferences of the general competence of the graph maker" (abstract †; a preprint, not peer-reviewed as far as the record shows). The rhetoric paper had already filed source notes under persuasion: "A number of visualization rhetoric techniques observed in our sample work to signal the transparency and trustworthiness of the presentation source", "Data provenance strategies include citing and/or linking data sources, additional references, methodological choices, and relevant facts, as well as annotating exceptions and corrections". And the critical-design principle that answers it: "Disclosing some of these decisions is a way to establish trust between visualization creators and viewers. When the designer's intentions and decisions are concealed, it is difficult to trust a visualization and engage with the presented issue".

Read together, the evidence says four things. A source line and a method note make a chart *look* more thorough and do not make its reading more accurate; they move some readers and not others, and move them through who the source is rather than what the method was; they are, as the critics say, themselves a signal that the reader need not check — and a beautiful chart is trusted for the competence it implies, whatever its data. None of that is a reason to leave the records off the face. It is the reason the face carries them in a form that can be *followed* and not only *seen*: a status in text (Chapter 4's code; the design system's chip) that names the kind — provenance tier, recipe, claim rung, comparison method, freshness, who was asked, held or withheld — and that is one step from the record itself, so that the reader who wants to check can, and the reader who takes the chip as a signal has at least taken a true one. The furniture, then: on the face, the sentence and the figure, the period and the stamp (Chapter 1; Chapter 8), the unit and base (Chapter 4), the floor and the kind of any nothing (Chapters 4 and 10), who was asked and how many (Chapter 9), the comparison's method in a word (Chapter 7), and the status chips that name the rest; one step away, the record each chip opens; on request, the method page — the house's "how this argues" — and the data. The test is the one the design-system research supplies by contrast: nothing the majority needs is one step away, and nothing one step away is hidden behind a control a reader will think "will take them away from the page".

### A cell that is not shown

Showing a gap as a gap has measured consequences, and they point in more than one direction. With 202 analysed (210 recruited, "35 participants for each between-subjects treatment", 30 and 50 per cent of values missing at random), "Most real-world datasets contain missing values yet most exploratory data analysis (EDA) systems only support visualising data points with complete cases", and "This omission may potentially lead the user to biased analyses and insights"; "In the average estimation task, when compared to a baseline of no imputation, we find that all uncertainty representations reduced bias (desirable), but may also reduce precision (undesirable)"; "an average participant will exhibit the greatest magnitude of bias in the no imputation (baseline) condition"; and "participants in every uncertainty representation were less certain about their response when compared to the baseline". Leaving the gap out was the most biased way to show it; showing it with its uncertainty cost precision and confidence. An earlier study of three ways to draw a gap found that "emptiness plus explanation … induced significantly higher degree of decision-confidence than the visualization technique fuzziness" and "yield the highest number of risky choices of the three" (abstract †). The study the field cites most on the question — two crowdsourced experiments on "how the methods used to impute and visualize missing data may influence analysts' perceptions of data quality and their confidence in their conclusions" — could be read only at its abstract, and its findings are not stated here from memory (abstract †). The official rule for a line stands: "Breaks in time series can occur due to missing data points and changes in methods", "it must always be highlighted", "Do not join the points either side of the missing data point, even if the line is dotted or dashed", and "Displaying the data as individual points may be better".

For the three nothings this gives a form and a caution, and the two studies are about different comparisons. Against a blurred gap, an explained empty cell raised decision confidence; against a gap silently dropped, a gap shown with its uncertainty lowered confidence and bias together. So a cell not shown is marked, never blank (Chapter 4), and its kind is stated — withheld, unmeasured, held — and nothing is imputed into a withheld cell; the held category is drawn at its coarser resolution and never as a flagged unit (Chapter 10); and the maker expects that a gap shown rather than dropped will lower readers' confidence in the panel around it, which is the gap doing its work and not a defect to design away. The explained gap also raised risk-taking in the one study, which is a reason to say what the gap means and not only that it exists. The prototype's test is the three nothings side by side on one face, tried with readers for whether they can tell them apart and what each does to their confidence in the neighbouring figure.

### A felt distribution, and the funnel

Chapter 9 required a felt measure to be shown as a spread with its bands and its sample, never a point alone, and Chapter 3 settled the display of uncertainty — frequencies over probabilities, countable forms, the number beside any word. This chapter adds only the first-screen consequence: a distribution is a slower glance than a number (Chapter 3's comparison cost), so the face carries the band and the n in words — the sentence says what the spread says — and the shape sits beside it, one glance down and not one step away. Chapter 7's funnel plot, which shows a place against its limits rather than its rank, is the same object for a counted measure: a comparison that cannot be read as a league table because the limits are drawn. Both are forms the public has no schema for (Chapter 3), and both therefore carry their reading in their title.

### A map that does not stigmatise

The official rules for the form are short. "Choropleth maps should display data as standardised rates, not absolute values"; "use the unemployment rate (percentage of the population unemployed in each area) rather than the total number of unemployed people in each area"; "Using absolute values can be misleading because they are distorted by the size of the area"; "Typically, use a scale with five discrete colour bands"; "Consider a map if you have data values for geographic areas and the pattern in the data you want to show is primarily geographic"; "Avoid using a map if you only have data for a few geographic areas". The vendor's documentation says why: "larger areas will naturally draw your attention, especially if they're styled with darker colors". The practitioners' guide: choropleths are for data "standardized to show rates or ratios (never use choropleth with raw data/counts)", and "Above all else the goal of data classification is to put places with similar rates in the same class, and separate places with very different rates into different classes". The colour schemes are three: "Sequential schemes are suited to ordered data that progress from low to high", "with light colors for low data values to dark colors for high data values"; "Diverging schemes put equal emphasis on mid-range critical values and extremes at both ends of the data range"; "Qualitative schemes do not imply magnitude differences between legend classes".

On the breaks, the design system and the experiment disagree. The ONS: "The standard method we use for setting breaks is the Jenks natural breaks, which creates an effective visualisation with most datasets." The one controlled comparison, "seven methods using responses by fifty-six subjects in a two-part experiment involving nine series of U.S. mortality maps": "Quantiles and minimum boundary error methods were best suited for these general map-reading tasks"; "The advantages of careful optimization procedures in classification seem to offer no benefit over the simpler quantile method"; and "Using matched legends across series (when possible) increased map-comparison accuracy by approximately 28 percent" (abstract †). For a dashboard whose maps change with the filter, the last finding is the one that matters: a legend that re-breaks with every view makes the views incomparable, and the official reader comparing two months will be wrong by the method.

On area and darkness, the numbers are recent and large. "an online study with 260 participants was conducted" on "the 'area-size bias' (i.e., the neglect of small areas, since these are less dominant in perception than larger ones)" and the "dark-is-more bias": large areas were detected by 91.5 and 91.9 per cent of readers; "the smaller areas had detection rates of only 71.5% (maximum) and 60.0% (minimum)"; "small areas were missed by approximately 30–40% of map users, which is definitively too much"; without a legend 92.6 per cent found the maximum and 85.4 per cent the minimum from lightness alone; asked which of two maps without legends had the larger values, "The majority (93.1%) selected that map that showed an overall darker appearance" when the strict answer was that they could not know, and "The underlying, actual data classification is not questioned by about 95% of the participants"; shown legends with different class limits, "The majority (43.6%, 50.6%, 47.9%) still relied on the dominant color lightness, neglecting the different class limits"; and the author endorses no remedy — "Much more comparative empirical research is needed to find (sub-)optimal solutions". The remedies' own evidence: among cartograms, with 33 people and seven tasks, "Contiguous cartograms more or less preserve localities, region shapes, and adjacencies, and give the best performance for almost all the tasks" and "Rectangular cartograms are a clear outlier and they should be used carefully"; but the one head-to-head against a conventional map, as the field's survey reports it, found "choropleth/graduated circles are more effective (as measured by accurate responses) and more efficient (as measured by faster responses) than the cartograms" (secondary). No evaluation of dot-density or hexagon tile maps was reached, and no measured study of red as danger on a map; the nearest measured fact is dark-is-more. Chapter 10's rules — no red or dark for low; large empty areas not dominating; "Missing data is not the same as a zero value" — stand on the statistical system's guidance, and this section adds the measured mechanism beneath them: a third of readers will not see a small area, nine in ten will read the darker map as the one with more without a legend, and about half will still do so with one. A map on a first screen is therefore drawn in rates, with declared breaks held constant across views, with a form or an inset that gives small areas size, with its table twin, and with its sentence saying what the darkest shape means before the eye decides.

## 9 · The failures: "what am I looking at" and "people don't use it"

The brief's failure register names two failures this chapter owns. The first has mechanisms in Chapter 3 and this one; the second has precedents, and the precedents say something unexpected about where the failure comes from.

### What the retired dashboards say about themselves

The UK government's own cross-government dashboard ran until "March 15 2021". The National Audit Office in 2017: "GDS publishes data on 802 government services on an online Performance Platform"; "But only 118 services publish data on costs per transaction"; "In an internal review in 2015, GDS found that there was a lack of clarity about the purpose of the Performance Platform"; "Some data have not been updated since March 2016". The service's own re-discovery in 2016: "In 5 weeks, we visited 14 organisations and spoke to 55 people doing various roles"; a theme headed "Analysis cannot be automated; data without context is dangerous for decision-making"; "The capture of performance metrics that are then immediately available on dashboards is an attractive idea but does not provide the full context and detail that is required for decision making"; "The value of a dashboard is in provoking a conversation, to raise awareness of something requiring attention"; and "The Performance Platform has to stop trying to be all things to all people, and become a focused product that meets our primary user's needs". The closure: "For GOV.UK Performance, we are moving away from the existing platform to a model which sees each department publish service data individually", with the data "findable through data.gov.uk, and available for download in spreadsheet format". No usage figure was published with the closure. The stated record is unclear purpose, stale and incomplete data, and a move to publishing the data instead of the dashboard.

The other retirements read the same way. A US federal dashboard's undated open letter says it is being sunset "eliminating a costly, inefficient process and allowing agencies to focus on higher value activities", that "Effective April 2026" agencies would move to a reduced state, and that "it does not fully deliver on that promise today". A university's pandemic resource "is ceasing its data collection efforts due to an increasing number of U.S. states slowing their reporting cadences" and because "the federal government has improved its pandemic data tracking enough to warrant the CRC's exit". A volunteer project closed because its work "is properly the work of federal public health agencies". The UK's own pandemic dashboard was decommissioned and its data archived as a 1.4 GB download with the caveat that "It has not been possible to fully validate these archived datasets against the latest version of the decommissioned Coronavirus (COVID-19) in the UK dashboard"; its stated reason for decommissioning was not found. Across every case read, the stated reason, where one is stated, is on the supply side — upstream data drying up, cost, duplication, an unclear remit — and none cites measured public non-use. No peer-reviewed study of why public dashboards stop being used was found, and the one interview study of dashboard users says the non-users "are absent from our study". The Analysis Function's 2026 guidance now writes retirement into the lifecycle: "Retire your dashboard if it is not being maintained or user feedback indicates that it is no longer: useful; relevant; providing value for money", with a banner "at least six months before retirement", the original state preserved, data and metadata kept with a note that they are no longer updated, and "retaining any code used for the dashboard in a public GitHub repository marked as archived".

### What that means for the two failures

"People don't use it" has, in the public record, no measured instance and several explained ones, and the explanations are Chapter 8's: the data stopped, nobody owned it, it tried to serve everyone. The failure the brief names is real; its documented cause is upstream of the first screen. A dashboard that wants to avoid it keeps its series alive (Chapter 8), publishes its data beside its views (section 7; the Performance Platform's successor is a spreadsheet), names its primary reader (Chapter 1), and measures its own use — which Chapter 10 found it may do under the statistics exception only as "how your service is used, not about who uses it" — because the field's own review says the reach figures do not otherwise exist: "large publishers are unwilling to share internal metrics". A dashboard that publishes its own reach, aggregate and dated, would be the first public evidence of its kind and the only evidence the studio will have of whether the failure has occurred.

"What am I looking at" is Chapter 3's schema failure and this chapter's first-screen failure: a form the reader has no schema for, under no sentence, behind a control. The evidence on the three remedies this chapter has read: the sentence (titles first, 65.6 per cent; a title remembered over its chart; "put the most important insights at the top"); the familiar form (no onboarding needed "for highly familiar visualization types", and for the unfamiliar ones a gain for one form only, on which the paper's abstract and body disagree); and the explained gap (an empty cell with its reason raises confidence where a blur does not). A tour does not fix it; a title, a schema and a label do.

## 10 · What holds true regardless

Seven truths, each with its grounds; then the record beneath them. They are stated for any public dashboard; the nightlife case in section 12 is one application.

**1 · The first screen is the product and it is a sentence: it says one thing, to a named reader, in words that lead, and it works before any interaction; a dashboard is a form to be justified, and the burden of understanding is the maker's.** "Only use a dashboard if you have clear justification"; dashboards "put the burden of interpreting and understanding data onto the user"; "Titles, headers, and primary overview components were consistently visited first"; "users only read around 28% of content on a page"; readers recall the title's message over the chart's; most readers touch nothing optional (Chapter 3). *(Sections 1, 3, 4; ONS; Analysis Function; Sultanum & Setlur; Kong, Liu & Karahalios; Chapter 3.)*

**2 · Every first screen is a landing pattern and an endorsement: it is chosen from the six, named, and designed as the anchor it will be; nothing is asked of the reader before something is given; and the small screen is the first screen.** No study compares the patterns, so the choice is declared as a reading; the first-shown view anchors (Chapter 3); search is used beside content and configuration-first is taken up by one or two in a hundred; "shows the main message without needing any interaction"; "Over half" (ONS) to "Two-thirds" (Analysis Function) of the ONS's readers arrive on a phone or tablet and "80% … do not scroll past the first quarter"; the phone has no hover and removes what the maker did not protect. *(Sections 3, 6; ONS; Feng et al.; OECD; Hoffswell, Li & Liu; Kim, Moritz & Hullman.)*

**3 · The authored account and the open door stand side by side, with neither a toll on the other: every written line links to the view that shows it and every view to its line; text on the face is not clutter and steers what is taken away; a title that contradicts its chart is remembered over it, and a caption that points at a weak feature loses to the chart's strong one; so the sentence says what the view shows.** "The reader is not forced to interact in order to learn"; "an interesting default view or annotations"; linking "significantly increased user engagement"; "heavily annotated charts were not penalized"; "Perceptual content relating to patterns or trends should be positioned in the title"; "readers rely on the chart and report a higher-prominence feature"; the null carried as a bet. *(Sections 4, 5; Victor; Segel & Heer; Zhi, Ottley & Metoyer †; Stokes et al.; Kim, Setlur & Agrawala; Boy et al., secondary.)*

**4 · An interaction is paid for: it names what it buys and what it costs, the static reading works first, nothing crucial sits behind hover, click, drag or motion, hover content obeys the standard, every control works by keyboard and single pointer, and dense marks that must be small get an equivalent route.** Seven costs; "does not improve participants' accuracy … In some cases, it can significantly detract from it"; 43–44 per cent used a filter when offered; a required interaction cut completion from 79.3 to 65.5 per cent; no correctness gain from onboarding except one unfamiliar form; "some users avoid clicking the link to show more details"; 1.4.13's three conditions and its reasons; 2.5.1, 2.5.7, 2.5.8 and the dense-visualisation exception with "equivalent functionality through alternative means". *(Sections 2, 6; Lam †; Mosca, Ottley & Chang; Zikmund-Fisher et al. †; Stoiber et al.; GOV.UK Design System; WCAG 2.2; NN/g.)*

**5 · A state a reader can reach is a state they can cite: a canonical address that encodes the selection, a dated and unchanging snapshot of the data and text it rests on, and a generated citation that carries the address, the snapshot's date, the data version and the retrieval date — because links rot, content drifts, and the national archive usually cannot hold an interactive page.** "URIs don't change: people change them"; 49.9 per cent of Supreme Court links; "one out of five" articles; "over 75% of references the content has drifted"; "Interactive content usually cannot be archived"; "timeslice, version and/or granular portion"; R1–R14; "citation instructions now point to archived versions of charts". *(Section 7; Berners-Lee; Zittrain, Albert & Lessig; Klein et al.; Jones et al.; The National Archives; FORCE11; RDA WGDC; Our World in Data; Heer, Viégas & Wattenberg.)*

**6 · A view is a function of a data version, a specification and a selection, and the display says so: the records' kinds sit on the face as statuses in text, the records one step away, the method on request; a cell not shown is marked, explained and never blank; and a source line is made followable, because seen alone it is a signal that there is no need to check.** "independent from the other components"; "unit := (data, transforms, mark-type, encodings)"; "re-evaluated as selections change"; metadata "more thorough" but not more "accurate"; 17 of 42 moved by a source and 25 not; "no need for the user to check"; beauty trusted through "the general competence of the graph maker"; leaving the gap out was "the greatest magnitude of bias"; "emptiness plus explanation" raised confidence; "Do not join the points either side of the missing data point". *(Section 8; Wickham; Satyanarayan et al.; Burns et al.; Peck, Ayuso & El-Etr; Kennedy et al.; Lin & Thornton †; Sarma et al.; Andreasson & Riveiro †; Analysis Function; Chapters 4, 8, 10.)*

**7 · A map is the hardest first screen: rates not counts, breaks declared and held constant across every view, a form or inset that keeps small areas legible, a table twin, and a sentence that says what the darkest shape means before the eye decides — because dark is read as more and small is missed by a third.** "standardised rates, not absolute values"; quantiles over natural breaks and "matched legends … approximately 28 percent"; "small areas were missed by approximately 30–40% of map users"; "The majority (93.1%) selected that map that showed an overall darker appearance"; about half still read by lightness with a legend; cartograms read worse than a choropleth with circles in the one head-to-head; "Red traditionally spells danger" (Chapter 10). *(Section 8; ONS; Brewer & Pickle †; Schiewe; Nusrat, Alam & Kobourov; Nusrat & Kobourov, secondary; UKSA.)*

### Beneath the view: the face sheet

Chapters 4 to 10 each left a record beneath a dashboard's numbers, and whether the seven are one document is answered in Chapter 16. This chapter leaves something different in kind: not an eighth record about the data but a sheet about the *surface* — one per view, which says what of the seven records sits on this face, what the view says and to whom, what it costs to touch, and how it is cited. It is called the *face sheet*, it has twelve lines, and its first line is written before the view is drawn.

| Line | What it states |
|---|---|
| 1 · The sentence | What this view says, in the house's words and limits, to which of the named readers; the question it answers; whether it is written or generated, and if generated, the template, the check and the label |
| 2 · The pattern and the first state | Which landing pattern (for a first screen) or which default state (for any view); why; declared as the endorsement it is; what the empty state shows, if there is one |
| 3 · What sits on the face | From each record — provenance set, recipe card, claim record, comparison record, service record, measure record, safeguards record — the status that is on the face in text, the record one step away, and what is on request; the stamp and the period beside the figure |
| 4 · The nothings on this view | Which cells are not shown, under which floor or hold; how each is marked and explained; the category-level form of any held layer |
| 5 · The interactions | Each control: its purchase (from the table), its costs (from the seven), what it changes, and the static reading that works without it; what is only here (nothing crucial) |
| 6 · Hover, keyboard, pointer, size | How content on hover meets the three conditions; keyboard and single-pointer routes for every control; targets under 24 CSS pixels and their equivalent route |
| 7 · The small-screen form | What is removed, kept and re-laid for the phone; what the message is and that it survives; figures before script; the table twin |
| 8 · The state and its address | The selection parameters and their canonical order; the data version and specification version the view was built from; the degraded state and how it reads |
| 9 · The snapshot and the citation | How this state is frozen (date, what it contains, where it lives, previous/next/live); the generated citation text; what a cropped or shared copy carries |
| 10 · Words and text | Title length; annotations and where their content is also carried; the chart's reading in its title; the generated label |
| 11 · Map and colour | For any map: rates not counts; the breaks and that they are held across views; the legend; the small-area form; the stigma check (no red or dark for low; large areas not dominating; missing not zero) |
| 12 · Tests and review | The two-readers test result; the walk (Chapter 10) for this view; the glance and small-screen checks; the review date |

**A filled example, invented for the method.** *1.* "Fewer late rooms this quarter than any since 2023." Nine words; written for the three readers alike; after a postcode is entered, a district line generated from a template, checked, labelled "sentence generated from the data; checked by hand". *2.* Message-then-input: the regional sentence and one small chart first, then the postcode box; the chart's example district rotates weekly and the page says so, so that the choice reads as the endorsement it is. *3.* On the face: period and stamp; the unit ("rooms open after 1 a.m."); provenance tier chip; claim rung chip ("counted, not estimated"); comparison method in a word ("against its own history"); the disclosure floor's kind on any withheld cell; one step away: each record; on request: the method page. *4.* Two districts under the disclosure floor, marked "withheld: too few to show"; one scene's rooms held at category level, shown as a district count with the held status and its reason. *5.* Postcode entry (personalisation; decision cost low); period toggle from a fixed list (filter; treated as a table builder; floor per view); peer choice from the declared set (comparison; default peers named as the endorsement); "read this as a sentence" link from every chart (linking); share (state). Nothing is only behind a control. *6.* Tooltips redundant to the labels at the mark, dismissible, hoverable, persistent, keyboard-reachable; map pinch has plus/minus; drag-to-pan has arrows; dense venue dots under 24 px with the table as the equivalent route. *7.* Phone first: one sentence, one chart, the box; the map one tap down with its table above it; no hover content anywhere load-bearing. *8.* `?area=…&period=…&peers=…` in that order; data version 2026.3; spec version 11.2; degraded state "last good copy, aged 9 days" shown with its stamp. *9.* Snapshot taken at each release, named by date, linked previous/next/live; citation generated with retrieval date, snapshot date, data version; a cropped share carries the stamp, the floor note and the snapshot date. *10.* Title eight words; annotations' content repeated in the text beneath; generated label present. *11.* Rate per 10,000 residents; five quantile bands fixed across periods; small districts drawn with an inset; no red for low; districts without a figure marked as held, withheld or unmeasured, never blank. *12.* Three-phones test run with one reader from each group — each gave the sentence, found the figure and named a step; two-readers test run — the account-only and views-only readers described the same quarter; the walk run by a regular — two low-confidence claims, one banded; review in six months.

The example is a shape and not a plan; its sentence, its districts and its parameters are placeholders.

## 11 · Where the choice is yours

*Which of the six landing patterns, and which exemplar.* The evidence names none as best. The chapter requires that the choice be made, written in line 2, and that the exemplar be understood as an endorsement.

*How long the sentence is.* The house rule is five words where possible and ten at most; the official limits are fifteen words and sixty characters. Which the Vibe Map uses, and whether a generated sentence is held to the poster rule, is the studio's.

*Whether to generate sentences per place at all.* The method exists and its maker says what it costs. A studio may write ten sentences by hand and generate none; the chapter requires only that any generated sentence be checked and labelled.

*How much furniture before the sentence.* A stamp, a unit, a floor note and a status chip are each defensible on the face; together they are a paragraph. That the records' kinds appear on the face as text is Fixed; which kinds, in what order, and how far below the sentence, is the studio's, with the design system's evidence that hidden things the majority need are a defect and the reading evidence that long text is skipped.

*Which interactions to build.* The purchase list is a procedure, not a list of requirements. A dashboard may ship with a postcode box and a share button and nothing else.

*Whether to hover at all.* A tooltip that repeats the mark's label, keyboard-reachable, harms nobody; a studio may also decide that the label at the mark is enough.

*How states are frozen.* At every release, on request, or on share; as a static page, a data file or a rendered image. The standards say what a snapshot must do; the studio decides when and how.

*The map's form.* Choropleth with an inset, hex tiles, a cartogram, dots, or no map on the first screen. The evidence prices each; the choice is a design decision within Chapter 8's budget.

*The breaks.* Quantiles have the experiment; natural breaks have the design system; either is defensible once declared and held constant across views of the same measure — a change of measure or of population is a new legend, declared as one.

*Scrollytelling and the editorial layer.* Free, above the dashboard, carrying its states and their snapshots.

*What the dashboard measures about itself, and whether it publishes it.* Under Chapter 10's rule; the chapter's view is that publishing it would be the first public evidence of its kind.

## 12 · A nightlife illustration — method only

A studio has a sentence for a region and wants a first screen. The chapter's method, as a sequence, with no finding about any place.

It writes line 1 before anything is drawn: the sentence, within the house's word limit, for the reader the view is for, and decides whether a district's version of it will be written by hand or generated and checked. It chooses the landing pattern and writes why; if the first thing a reader sees is one district as an exemplar, it writes that the district was chosen and that the choice will be read as a recommendation.

It lays the face out for a phone: the sentence, one figure or one small chart that the sentence rests on, the period and the stamp beside it, the unit, and the input. It marks beside the figure the kinds — counted or estimated; against its own history; who was asked and how many, for a felt figure — as text chips, and makes each chip open its record. It writes the "how this argues" page for the reader who will check, and does not mistake it for a reason the others will.

For every control it writes the purchase and the cost, and checks that a reader who touches nothing still gets the sentence and the figure. It keeps the tooltip, if there is one, as a repetition of what is at the mark, dismissible, hoverable, persistent, reachable by keyboard; it gives the map plus and minus and arrows; it puts the venue table under the venue dots because the dots are too small to be targets.

It decides the address: the parameters in a fixed order, so one state has one address. It decides the snapshot: at each release, a dated copy of the data and the text, linked to the one before and the one after and to the live view, and a citation generated from it. It writes what a cropped share carries.

For the map it uses a rate, chooses its breaks and holds them across every period and every peer set, gives the small districts size — an inset, say — keeps red away from low values, and writes the sentence that says what the darkest shape means. It draws the withheld districts as withheld and the held rooms as a district count under a held status, and never as empty.

Then it gives the first screen to one reader of each of the three user groups, on their own phones, and asks each what the dashboard says, which figure it rests on, and what they would do next. If the answers are the sentence, the figure and a step, the first screen works. If they are "what am I looking at", or the sentence without its figure, it starts again at line 1.

## What proves a break works

The test for a departure from one of this chapter's rules is two conversations and a phone. **First, give the first screen to one reader of each of the three user groups the studio tests with (Chapter 2), on their own phone, and ask three things: what the dashboard says, which figure it rests on, and what they would do next.** If each gives the sentence, points to the figure and names a next step, the first screen works whatever pattern it took — the second and third questions are there because the title evidence says a reader will repeat a sentence whether or not the view supports it; if any answers "what am I looking at", or repeats the sentence and cannot find its figure, the schema, the sentence or the device has failed and the break has cost the reader. **Second, give one reader the authored account alone — the sentence and the written lines, with the charts covered — and one the views alone with their titles but without the written account, and ask both what the place is like.** If they agree, the two layers are one dashboard and the sequencing break has cost nothing; if they diverge, the authored account says something the views do not show, and the "how this argues" page is where to look for the crack.

For a break in the interaction rules, the test is the Bayesian study's: show the static reading working without the control, then watch one real reader meet the control cold, and if they do not use it, remember that most will not and the surface they saw was the product. For a break in the hover rule, the test is a touchscreen and a keyboard, in that order. For a break in the citation rule, the test is a year: cite the state, change the data, and see whether the citation still finds what was cited. For a break in the map rules, the test is the 260-person study's: ask a reader to point to the smallest area and the largest value, without the legend, and count.

## Words this chapter uses

- The *sentence* is a view's headline message — what it says, to whom — written within the house's word limit; the *title* is the sentence as it sits above a chart.
- The *first screen* is what a reader sees on arrival before doing anything; its configured state is the *default view*, in Chapter 3's sense; a *landing pattern* is one of the six shapes a first screen takes, named in section 3.
- A *view* is a panel in one state; a *state* is the set of values of every selection that makes a view; a *query* is the request for a state; an *address* is the URL that encodes one; a *snapshot* is a dated, unchanging copy of the data and text behind a view; a *version* is a numbered edition of a dataset.
- The *face* of a panel is its visible surface, and the chapter distinguishes three distances from it — *on the face* (seen without acting, including by scrolling the page the panel is on), *one step away* (opened from the face by one action and shown beside it), and *on request* (a separate page reached by a link, such as the method page); Nielsen's phrase "progressive disclosure" appears only inside quotation marks, because Chapter 10 reserved "disclosure" for the statistical sense.
- The *authored account* is what the maker wrote — the sentence, the lines beside the charts, any text generated by rule — and *exploration* is what the reader does to make states; the word "story" appears only in quotations and in the names of the genres that use it.
- An *interaction* is anything a reader does that changes what is shown, and a *control* is the thing they do it with; *hover* is a pointer resting without a click; a *tooltip* is content shown on hover or on keyboard focus; *touch* is a pointer that cannot hover.
- An interaction's *purchase* is what it buys, version 1's word, kept; its *costs* are Lam's seven, named in section 6.
- *Generated text* is sentences produced by rule from data, which the ONS calls "semi-automated".
- An *empty state* is an input-first screen before input, and is never called a nothing; the *three nothings* are Chapter 10's.
- A *small screen* is a phone; "mobile" appears in quotations and figures.
- *Rhetoric* is Hullman and Diakopoulos's sense — how a display argues — and the house's "how this argues" page is its statement of its own.
