## The question

**What has to be true of a number before it can be shown as evidence — and what does a dashboard owe its reader about where the number came from, what it counts, what it leaves out, and what it may be compared with?**

---

## The 60-second version

> A dashboard shows numbers. A number is not evidence until five things about it are settled, and most dashboards settle none of them on the face of the display.
>
> First, its **grain**: what one row of the data is — a person, a venue, a night, a ticket — and what one unit of time is. These are choices, made before any analysis, and the author of the canonical paper on tidy data admits that defining them in general is "surprisingly difficult". European law defines eight different things a "business" can be. The UK counts a hospital bed as occupied if someone is in it at midnight, and a US study found the count at midnight sits well below the late-morning peak. A "day", a "month" and a "night" are measurement rules, and the rule changes the count.
>
> Second, its **denominator**: a count against what. A rate exists so that places of different sizes can be compared; the choice of base is the least visible and most consequential decision in a display, and the smallest places sit at both ends of any ranking by arithmetic alone.
>
> Third, its **provenance**. The UK's Code of Practice, European law, the Treasury's guide to analytical assurance and three machine standards converge on the same minimum: who is responsible, what source, what method, what period it describes (separately from when it was published and when it last changed), what status it has, what quality it claims, and what has been withheld. Official usage defines "reliability" as how little a number moves when it is revised; a UK quarterly growth figure typically moves half a percentage point over three years, and the regulator calls that normal.
>
> Fourth, its **nothings**. The studio names six kinds — zero, free, unavailable, unknown, not collected, suppressed. The official world already distinguishes most of them, splits one of them in two, and has no code at all for "free", which it treats as a price of zero on a real quantity. The UK and European statistical offices both ban the letters "NA" because nobody can tell whether they mean not applicable or not available. The inventor of the relational database wrote in 1986 that zero is "far too valuable" to stand for missing; the database standard kept one null anyway, and the one-line idiom that turns "no rows" into a displayed 0 is in every warehouse. The only official rule for drawing a gap on a chart says: do not join the points either side, "even if the line is dotted or dashed. Joining points implies we know something about the data."
>
> Fifth, its **comparability**. A comparison is valid "to the extent that differences between statistics can be attributed to differences between the true values" — which means everything else must be held equal or declared: definition, period, geography and boundary vintage, method, disclosure treatment, breaks, revisions, the comparator group and how it was chosen, precision, and what was done with outliers. Finance arrived at the same list independently: a like-for-like figure is a defined figure, and if you change the definition you restate the comparators.
>
> Behind these sit three facts about data itself. A total is a statement about its largest contributors — the long tail of cultural markets is mostly ones and zeros, and in an artificial music market, letting people see what others chose increased both inequality and unpredictability. Size does not cure selection: in the one case where it has been measured, a self-selected dataset covering one in a hundred people was worth a random sample of four hundred, because what matters is the tiny correlation between being recorded and the value recorded. And an outlier is a question before it is a decision: the standard definition is a point that arouses "suspicions that it was generated by a different mechanism", the rule behind every box plot flags an "outlier" in a third of perfectly ordinary samples, two-fifths of papers that claim no exclusions have numbers that do not match, and the good-end outlier — the small place doing unexpectedly well — is a hypothesis about mechanism that has the same undeclared-threshold problem as the bad end.
>
> So the canonical truths are these: a number is undefined until its unit, its period and its base are stated; nothing is never one thing; every figure carries its provenance and the record outlives the number; a comparison declares what it holds equal; a total speaks for the biggest; size does not cure selection; and an outlier is investigated, declared, and shown with and without.

## 1 · What evidence asks of a number

Chapters 1 to 3 treated a dashboard as an argument addressed to a reader. An argument rests on evidence, and evidence is a stronger word than data. Data is what the system recorded. Evidence is data that has been made fit to bear a conclusion, with the fitness shown. The distance between the two is the subject of this chapter, and it is where the second and sixth items on Carly's failure register — the fancy spreadsheet that tells you big numbers but not what happens, and the display that breaks or is not reliable — mostly come from.

The sharpest single statement of the standard is the UK statistics regulator's. In its 2023 perspective on quality, the Office for Statistics Regulation defines quality as statistics that "fit their intended uses, are based on appropriate data and methods, and are not materially misleading", and then gives the test: "Publishing data and statistics that materially mislead is unacceptable. If the statistics do not bear the weight of the decisions made using them, producers should immediately review whether they should continue to publish them." A number on a dashboard is being asked to bear the weight of a decision; the question is whether it can, and whether the reader can tell.

The regulator also reports what readers say they need in order to tell: "Some users have told us during our designation review that they would like prominent, succinct quality information, which helps them decide whether the statistics are suitable for their own use." Something prominent and short enough to be read at the point of use, by a reader whose use the producer did not anticipate. It should be said at once that this is users' testimony to a regulator; no study was found for this chapter that measures whether provenance shown on the face of a display changes what a reader decides, and section 5 returns to that gap.

The Treasury's guide to analytical assurance — *The AQuA Book*, first published in 2015 and replaced by a new edition on 30 July 2025 — supplies a distinction this chapter uses throughout. In the 2015 wording, which the 2025 edition keeps in substance: "verification activities ask whether the analysis has been conducted correctly; validation activities ask whether the correct analysis has been performed". A number can be computed flawlessly from the wrong data, at the wrong grain, against the wrong base, for a purpose it was never assured for. The book exists because of a case of exactly that: the 2012 InterCity West Coast rail franchise competition, which collapsed when, in the National Audit Office's words, there were "significant errors in the tool the Department used to calculate how big a loan it would require bidders to have", and — the sentence that matters here — "The model had been designed to inform internal discussions and received no extra quality assurance once the Department decided to use it to calculate the loan, a key commercial decision". The arithmetic was the smaller fault; the larger was a number re-used for a decision heavier than the one it had been built to bear. "Fit for purpose" means fit for *this* purpose, and purposes drift.

The 2025 AQuA Book names four roles in any piece of analysis, and they are worth carrying into dashboard work because a dashboard rarely assigns them. The *commissioner* "requests the analysis and sets out their requirements" and "accepts the analysis and assurance as fit for purpose". The *analyst* "designs the approach, including the assurance" and "carries out their own assurance". The *assurer* "must be independent from the analysts" and "reviews the assurance completed by the analyst". The *approver* "scrutinises the work of the analyst and assurer" and confirms that it "has been appropriately assured". Assurance, it says, "should be proportionate to the potential effect it will have and the size and complexity of the analysis". The Macpherson review of 2013, which led to the first edition, recommended that "There should be a single Senior Responsible Owner for each model ("Model SRO") through its lifecycle" and that quality assurance cover "inputs, methodology and outputs". A public dashboard is an analytical model with a user interface. Somebody owns it; the reader should be able to find out who.

The academic literature on data quality arrived at the same breadth from the consumer's side. In 1996 Richard Wang and Diane Strong defined data quality "as data that are fit for use by data consumers" — borrowing the phrase from the quality engineer Joseph Juran — and, from a first survey of 137 people that produced 179 candidate attributes and a second of 355 usable returns that rated 118 of them, found that consumers judge it on twenty dimensions in four groups, not on accuracy alone. The group they call *intrinsic* contains accuracy, but also objectivity, *believability* and *reputation*. That finding is the bridge back to Chapter 1: a correct number from a source the reader does not believe is, to that reader, low-quality data. Provenance is part of what the number's quality consists of, by the consumer's own account.

Three vocabularies for quality are used below and should be told apart. The European Statistical System's, written into Regulation (EC) No 223/2009 and adopted by the UK, is for *statistics*: relevance, accuracy, timeliness, punctuality, accessibility and clarity, comparability, coherence. The UK Government Data Quality Framework of 2020 is for *datasets* and uses the six dimensions of the Data Management Association: completeness, uniqueness, consistency, timeliness, validity, accuracy. The Code of Practice for Statistics is for *producers* and asks about their conduct. A dashboard needs all three, because it is a dataset, shown as statistics, by a producer.

## 2 · Why people believe what they believe

The beliefs this chapter tests are less often stated than Chapter 3's, because they are rarely noticed as beliefs. Each is given fairly here and met in the sections that follow.

### Belief one: a count is a fact

"There were 412 events last month." The number of events feels like a thing that was simply counted. But "event", "last month" and the implied base (412 of what, in how big a place) are each a choice, and the count changes with every one of them. Section 3 is about the first two; section 4 about the third.

### Belief two: more data is better data

If a feed covers millions of transactions, it must be more trustworthy than a survey of a few hundred people. The intuition is strong, the vocabulary of "big data" encourages it, and it is wrong in a precise and quantifiable way. Section 9.

### Belief three: a blank is nothing, and nothing is small

An empty cell, a flat line at zero, a missing row: the instinct is that absence is harmless, or that it is the same as zero, or that it affects only the cell it sits in. Section 6 shows that statistics, databases and surveys each had to invent a vocabulary to stop that instinct doing damage.

### Belief four: the average describes the typical

A mean, a total, a per-capita figure: these feel like descriptions of a population. In a skewed distribution — which is what every cultural market is — they describe its largest members. Section 8.

### Belief five: the long tail is a law

"Twenty per cent of the venues produce eighty per cent of the revenue" is said as if it were a law of nature, often with the name Pareto attached. The man who popularised the principle later apologised for its name, and the empirical shape of a cultural market's tail is more extreme and less lawful than the slogan. Section 8.

### Belief six: an outlier is an error

When a value is far from the others, the practised reflex is to trim it. The statistical literature's standard definition says an outlier is a point suspected of having been "generated by a different mechanism" — which is a reason to investigate, not to delete — and the field's own audits find that deletion is frequent, silent, and shaped by what the analyst wanted to find. Section 10.

### Belief seven: two numbers with the same name can be compared

Two "footfall" figures, two "visitor spend" estimates, two years of the same series: if they share a name, they are comparable. European law defines comparability as the extent to which differences in the numbers reflect differences in reality rather than differences in method, and gives three axes along which that must be shown. Section 7.

### Belief eight: "reliable" means accurate

In ordinary speech it does. In official statistics it means something narrower and more useful — how little a number changes when it is revised — and the difference matters for any display that overwrites yesterday's figure with today's. Section 5.

## 3 · Grain — the unit of observation, and the unit of time

### What a row is

Hadley Wickham's 2014 paper "Tidy Data", the standard statement of how analytical data should be shaped for analysis, gives three rules: "In tidy data: 1. Each variable forms a column. 2. Each observation forms a row. 3. Each type of observational unit forms a table." The second rule is the one this section is about. An observation "contains all values measured on the same unit (like a person, or a day, or a race) across attributes". The row of the table *is* the grain, and what the row is — a person, or a day, or a race — is decided before anything is counted.

Wickham is candid about how slippery that decision is. "For a given dataset, it's usually easy to figure out what are observations and what are variables, but it is surprisingly difficult to precisely define variables and observations in general." If the author of the canonical paper finds the definition hard in general, a dashboard that never states its grain is asking its readers to guess at something its builders may not have settled.

Official statistics has made the distinctions explicit because it had to. Eurostat's glossary separates three things a dashboard usually treats as one. The *reporting unit* is "the entity from which the recommended data items are collected" — who sent the data. The *observation unit* is "the entity on which information is received and statistics are compiled in the process of collecting statistical data" — what the data is about. The *statistical unit* is "the entity for which the required statistics are compiled", which "may be an observation unit … or an analytical unit, which statisticians create by splitting or combining observation units with the help of estimations or imputations". A ticketing platform reports; a venue is observed; a "scene" may be an analytical unit nobody reported and nobody observed, assembled from the other two. Each is a legitimate grain. Confusing them is where a count stops being a fact.

How many things can a "business" be? Council Regulation (EEC) No 696/93, which still governs the statistical units of the European production system, defines eight in its annex. The *enterprise* is "The smallest combination of legal units that is an organizational unit producing goods or services, which benefits from a certain degree of autonomy in decision-making". The *local unit* is "An enterprise or part thereof (e.g. a workshop, factory, warehouse, office, mine or depot) situated in a geographically identified place". The *enterprise group* is "An association of enterprises bound together by legal and/or financial links". Between them sit the institutional unit, the kind-of-activity unit, the unit of homogeneous production, the local kind-of-activity unit and the local unit of homogeneous production. A count of "venues" is a different number under each: an operator with four rooms in one building is one enterprise, one local unit and perhaps four kind-of-activity units; a chain is one group and many local units. The regulation's own preamble gives the reason this matters for comparison: "Only if the Member States use common definitions of statistical units will it be possible to provide integrated statistical information with the reliability, speed, flexibility and degree of detail required". Grain is standardised because without a shared unit no two counts can be set beside each other.

The social-science textbook statement adds the one further point the dashboard needs. The unit of analysis "refers to the person, collective, or object that is the target of the investigation", and it can differ from the unit at which data were collected — one may survey individuals and report on organisations. When the unit of analysis is larger than the unit of collection, the move between them is an aggregation, and section 8 is about what aggregation does. One consequence for display is our reading rather than any source's: a figure should carry not only what its unit is but how many of them it rests on. Chapter 3 adopted that convention for experiments (*n* is how many people took part); the same number beneath a dashboard figure — twelve venues, four hundred events, one platform — tells the reader how much weight the figure can bear before any statistics are done, and section 4 shows why the smallest counts need it most. When it is smaller — inferring something about individuals from an area figure — the move is the ecological fallacy, which belongs to Chapter 7 and is only named here.

### What a period is

A number refers to a period, and the period is a choice. Eurostat's definition: "A reference period is the time period for which statistical results are collected or calculated and to which, as a result, these values refer. The time period may be either a calendar year (reference year), a fiscal year, a semester, a quarter, a month and even a day." Some data "relate to one specific time, a reference time point" — a stock, like a population on 1 January, rather than a flow, like births in a year. And, the sentence that every dashboard with a "last updated" stamp should take to heart: "The reference period should be distinguished from the publication time, the period or point in time at which the statistical data are published." Two dates, not one. Section 5 adds a third.

The UK's own harmonised practice is to use the financial year: "Most regular or continuous Government social surveys use financial year as the reference period though there are exceptions", because "There appears to be a significant need for annual figures on a common time basis". A local dataset on calendar years and a national one on financial years are not comparable until someone says which is which.

Months are not equal containers, and official statistics corrects for it. Eurostat defines the *calendar effect* as "The variation caused by the changing number of particular week days or holidays in different months or other time periods", and *calendar adjustment* as the method for removing it; its examples are that "Easter can fall in March or in April, depending on the year", that "1 May can fall on a Sunday", and "The occurrence of a leap year". A month with five Fridays is a longer measurement window, for anything that happens on Fridays, than a month with four. A monthly series that does not say whether it is adjusted will show a rise and fall that is the calendar, not the world.

A transaction can have more than one legitimate date. In securities markets the *trade date* is "the day your order to buy or sell a security is executed" and the *settlement date* is "the day your order is finalized and on which funds and the securities must be delivered"; since May 2024 US securities settle one business day later, and "business day" excludes weekends and exchange holidays. A ticket has a sale date, an event date and a settlement date; a grant has an award date and a payment date; a licence has an application date and a decision date. "When did it happen" is a convention, and a series that silently switches from one to another has a break in it.

### The statistical day, and the midnight census

The clearest case of a period that is a rule rather than a fact is the hospital bed. NHS England's definitions for the quarterly bed return (KH03) state: "For wards open overnight, an occupied bed day is defined as a bed which is occupied at midnight on the day in question." The NHS Data Dictionary carries the same wording. A day's occupancy is a single instant. For day wards the rule is different and explicitly capped: "If more than one patient has occupied a bed during the same day this should be reported as only one bed day, therefore occupancy rates should not exceed 100%" — a definition that makes a count unable to exceed its own ceiling.

Is the midnight instant representative of the day? A study in the *Journal of Hospital Medicine* by Pierce and colleagues, published in 2024, measured hourly census on a hospital medicine service and concluded, in the words reproduced by the trade press (the journal page was not reachable — secondary), that "Measuring ADC at midnight, as traditionally done, may underestimate workload and therefore contribute to staffing shortages and physician burnout." The census peaked around 11 a.m.; the gap between the day's peak and its trough was reported as 9.1 patients, about 5.5% of the peak, and the midnight count sat a reported five or six patients below the early-morning one. The effect is modest in size; its importance is that it is systematic and that the rule producing it is written into the official definition. An earlier reliability study by Simon and colleagues (abstract only †) found that two of five accepted patient-day methods systematically underestimated patient days and that the error depended on the proportion of short-stay patients, "by up to 7.6%". The lesson is general and belongs to no sector: when an activity has a daily rhythm, the instant or window chosen to stand for the day decides how much of the activity is counted, and the official definition does not have to justify its choice. The UK's own night time economy statistics define their window as "activity that takes place between 6pm and 6am" — a twelve-hour period that straddles the calendar midnight, so that the statistical day and the calendar day disagree about which date an event belongs to. Which date a thing that crosses midnight is assigned to is a measurement rule that every dashboard with an evening in it must state. The chapter takes the window only as an official definition and goes no further into the sector; that knowledge is the studio's.

### Writing the date down

The GOV.UK open standard requires ISO 8601 for dates and times in government systems: "List date and time elements in descending order of size (years, months, days, hours, minutes, seconds, milliseconds and microseconds)." Its stated benefits include "an easier way to sort documents by date and filename" — the official way of saying that a date written largest-unit-first sorts correctly as text. The standard also provides for the offset from Coordinated Universal Time: "2017-05-16T10:30:56+01:00 is 16 May 2017 10:30 and 56 seconds with an offset showing British summer time (BST), one hour ahead of UTC". A dashboard that spans a clock change has one day of 23 hours and one of 25 in it; a count per day across that boundary is not comparable unless the offset was recorded. The ISO page gives the plainer reason: "01/05/22 could mean January 5, 2022, or May 1, 2022." The standard is for data, not for people: the Decision Book's companion rule — ISO in the file, natural-language dates in the display — follows from GOV.UK's own scope, which is "machine-readable format in government systems".

### The same market at two grains

A last point about grain, because it is easy to think it affects only precision. It can reverse a finding. Studies of recorded-music streaming at the *market* grain — total plays by track — find concentration in the head (section 8). A 2018 study by Datta, Knox and Bronnenberg at the *individual* grain, using "a unique panel data set of individual consumers' listening histories across many digital music platforms" (abstract †), found that "adoption of streaming leads to very large increases in the quantity and diversity of consumption" and that "even after half a year, adopters play substantially more, and more diverse, music". The same market looks concentrated when the row is a track and diversifying when the row is a listener. Neither is wrong. A dashboard that shows one and implies the other has changed its grain without telling anyone.

## 4 · Denominator — a count against what

### The vocabulary

The clearest public statement of the vocabulary is the US Centers for Disease Control's epidemiology primer, which is used here because it is plain and because epidemiology is the field that was forced to get this right. "A ratio is the relative magnitude of two quantities or a comparison of any two values." "A proportion is the comparison of a part to the whole. It is a type of ratio in which the numerator is included in the denominator." "In epidemiology, a rate is a measure of the frequency with which an event occurs in a defined population over a specified period of time." And the reason rates exist at all: "Because rates put disease frequency in the perspective of the size of the population, rates are particularly useful for comparing disease frequency in different locations, at different times, or among different groups of persons with potentially different sized populations; that is, a rate is a measure of risk."

Two refinements from the same source are worth carrying. The *population at risk* is the denominator of an incidence proportion — "the proportion of an initially disease-free population that develops disease" — and it is the size of the population *at the start* of the period. And *person-time* puts time directly into the base: the denominator is "the sum of the time each person was observed, totaled for all persons", representing "the total time the population was at risk of and being watched". For anything that opens and closes, starts and stops, or is observed for unequal lengths of time, person-time (or venue-months, or event-nights) is the honest base, and a count per place that ignores how long each place was observed is comparing unequal windows.

### Compared with what

Edward Tufte's chapter on visual and statistical thinking turns on two cases that are both denominator cases, though he does not use the word. On John Snow's map of the 1854 cholera deaths, the dots show deaths but not people: "an area of the map may be free of cases merely because it is not populated." On the night before the *Challenger* launch, the engineers' chart showed the rockets that had suffered seal damage and omitted the ones that had not: "Missing are 92 % of the temperature data, for 5 of the launches with erosion and 17 launches without erosion." The flights without damage were the denominator of the comparison, and they were left off. Tufte's recurring question, "Compared with what?", is the denominator question in three words. (His principles on the chapter's last page — displays should be "documentary, comparative, causal and explanatory, quantified, multivariate, exploratory" — are Practice; the two cases are the evidence.) David Hand makes the same point about the same chart in his 2020 article on dark data: "what was missing from the graph, however, were points for all those previous launches which had had no problems with the seals." A chart that omits the zeros has chosen its denominator by omission.

### The reference class

Philosophers have a name for the fact that a rate depends on the class you put the case in. Alan Hájek's 2007 paper (abstract †): "The reference class problem arises when we want to assign a probability to a proposition (or sentence, or event) X, which may be classified in various ways, yet its probability can change depending on how it is classified." A venue's "closure rate" is one number among venues of its size, another among venues in its city, another among venues of its type, and each is correct. The display chooses the class; the reader should be able to see that a choice was made and what the others would show.

### Finance's own denominator habit

Retail finance changes its denominator openly and calls the result *like-for-like*. Tesco's 2025/26 preliminary results define it in a footnote: "Like-for-like (LFL) sales growth is a measure of growth in Group sales from stores that have been open for at least a year and online sales (at constant exchange rates, excluding VAT and fuel)." The base is the constant estate; new and closed stores are stripped out so that growth means growth, not expansion. The European Securities and Markets Authority's guidelines on such "alternative performance measures" require that issuers "should define the APMs used and their components as well as the basis of calculation adopted" and that "The definition and calculation of an APM should be consistent over time" — and where a definition changes, the issuer must "provide restated comparative figures". Section 7 returns to this; the point here is that a sector with money at stake treats the denominator as something to be defined, published and held constant.

### The small-denominator outlier

The arithmetic that produces the "aggregation of the biggest" also produces its mirror: the smallest units are the most extreme. Andrew Gelman and Phillip Price's 1999 paper carries the title "All maps of parameter estimates are misleading" and the abstract states the problem: "very high (and low) observed rates are found disproportionately in poorly-sampled areas." Their kidney-cancer example: "Because of this stochastic noise, a disproportionate fraction of low-population counties are observed to have extremely high (or low) cancer rates when compared to typical counties in the United States." The small place has the highest rate and the lowest rate because a handful of events moves its rate a long way. The paper's second finding is the one practitioners miss: the obvious fix — shrinking small-area estimates toward the mean — produces the opposite artefact, so that "the highest adjusted rates tend to be found disproportionately in well-sampled areas", and "Mapping county posterior means or highlighting the counties with highest posterior means leads to new problems." Ranked by raw rate, the small places top the table; ranked by adjusted rate, the big ones do. Neither ranking is a finding about the places.

The display answer, from the same tradition, is David Spiegelhalter's funnel plot (2005; abstract fragments only †), in which "an estimate of an underlying quantity is plotted against an interpretable measure of its precision", with control limits that form a funnel, so as to "avoid spurious ranking of institutions into 'league tables'". Public Health England's technical guidance states the rule for small numbers plainly: "It is particularly important to provide confidence intervals when numbers are small, and hence the random component of variation is likely to be large." Chapter 7 takes up comparison without a league table; this section's contribution is that the rank order of small units is largely a property of their size.

## 5 · Provenance — what a number must carry

### The regulator's list

The Code of Practice for Statistics, edition 3.0, organises itself as ten Standards for Official Statistics — 1 Show integrity, 2 Lead responsibly, 3 Be transparent, 4 Manage data responsibly, 5 Prioritise quality, 6 Be rigorous, 7 Be open about quality, 8 Be relevant, 9 Be clear, 10 Be accessible — each with numbered practices. Chapter 1 cited the previous edition's three quality principles (Q1 suitable data sources, Q2 sound methods, Q3 assured quality); their work is now done by Standards 6 and 7, and anything in this book that cites "Q1–Q3" should be read as pointing there.

Standard 6, *Be rigorous*: "Producers must use suitable data sources and sound methods, and assure the quality of the statistics across the production and release processes". Its practices include 6.6 — "Use data that are coherent when aggregated, consistent over time and comparable across and within geographies at regional, national and local levels, where possible" — and 6.7 — "Base methods on national or international good practice, scientific principles or professional consensus. Identify potential bias and address limitations. Use recognised standards, classifications and definitions. Explain reasons for deviations from these standards and any related implications for use". Practice 6.10 asks producers to "monitor relevant quality dimensions for both input data and the statistics, such as completeness and validity, accuracy and reliability, coherence and comparability, and timeliness. Quantify statistical error, including bias, and produce measures of confidence, where possible".

Standard 7, *Be open about quality*: "Producers must prominently explain the quality of the statistics, including strengths and limitations, and communicate the uncertainty in estimates". Practice 7.2: "Report on the key quality dimensions such as accuracy and timeliness, and, where possible, give estimates of error and confidence for the statistics. Summarise how uncertainty in the estimates may impact use by using qualifying words, numbers or graphics". Practice 7.3: "Explain the nature of data sources and why they were selected, anticipating possible areas of misunderstanding or misuse". Practice 7.4: "Be clear about the methods used. Explain quality issues related to the methods, systems and processes, including the extent to which the statistics are representative and comparable across the UK and internationally." Practice 7.5: "Give advance notice to users of planned changes to methods and sources that will impact the statistics. Explain the nature and extent of the change, and provide a consistent back series where possible". Practice 7.6: "Clearly flag where statistics are being developed and tested."

Three practices from other standards complete the set that bears on a display. Practice 3.9: "Release revisions and corrections of errors transparently and as soon as possible in line with the organisation's published policy, being clear about the nature and scale of change". Practice 3.10 requires that "the status of the statistics is clear in the release" — accredited, official, or in development. Practice 4.5: "Protect the confidentiality of individual and business information when producing statistics." Practice 9.3: "Provide comparisons to support interpretation and signpost other relevant statistics … Explain the consistency and coherence with other related statistics and sources". Practice 9.5: "Aid understanding by highlighting potential misinterpretations." Practice 10.5: "Ensure statistics are reproducible. Support data and statistics to be shared, accessed and linked, using common data standards with associated metadata".

Read together, these practices are a list of what a number must carry on the face of its release: its source and why it was chosen; its method and any deviation from standards; its quality, with error and confidence where possible, in "qualifying words, numbers or graphics"; its comparability across time and geography; advance notice of change and a back series; its status; the nature and scale of any revision; the misreadings it invites; and the metadata that lets someone else reproduce it. The Decision Book calls this "the full provenance set" and ships it with every figure; the Code is where the set comes from.

Two further features of the third edition matter for a dashboard that is not an official statistic. First, the Code now carries separate *Standards for Public Use*, which apply to any public body using numbers in public — explicitly including "management information, research and economic modelling" — and ask it to "Be clear about where statistics, data and wider analysis used in public communications come from. For example, cite sources in publications and on social media"; not to use them "in a misleading way. This includes not cherry-picking figures, taking figures out of context or placing undue certainty on them"; and to "Clearly communicate any key context and limitations". These did not exist in the 2018 edition and are the nearest thing in UK regulation to a rule about what a public dashboard must show. Second, a non-public body can adopt the Code through *voluntary application*: it publishes a "TQV statement" — TQV being the Code's three pillars, Trustworthiness, Quality and Value — explaining its approach to each of the ten standards and is listed on a register. The regulator is clear about what that is and is not: "We do not have a formal role in regulating the voluntary application of the TQV principles but we are responsible for managing the scheme", and any intervention "will be made on an informal and advisory basis". The badge is self-awarded; the discipline is in the statement.

### The European dimensions, and what "reliable" means

Article 12 of Regulation (EC) No 223/2009 defines the quality dimensions that the UK's framework inherits, and three definitions carry weight here. *Accuracy* "refers to the closeness of estimates to the unknown true values". *Timeliness* "refers to the period between the availability of the information and the event or phenomenon it describes" — which presupposes a declared reference period, since without one the distance cannot be measured. *Comparability* "refers to the measurement of the impact of differences in applied statistical concepts, measurement tools and procedures where statistics are compared between geographical areas, sectoral domains or over time" — three axes, taken up in section 7.

*Reliability* is where ordinary language and official language part. The UK Government Statistical Service defines it as "the closeness of early estimates to subsequent estimated values", and the European handbook files it under data revision rather than accuracy. "Reliable", in this usage, is a statement about how little a number moves between the first time it is published and the last. A dashboard that calls a live feed "reliable" because it refreshes on time is using the ordinary sense; a reader from the statistical world will hear the other, and the two can point in opposite directions — a feed can be punctual and heavily revised.

The scale of revision in a mature series is instructive. The Office for National Statistics reports that for quarterly GDP from 1961 to 2025, the mean absolute revision was 0.2 percentage points three months after the first estimate and 0.5 percentage points at thirty-six months; for the period from 2020 onward it was 0.7 at thirty-six months, and the overall figure in the 2025 Blue Book (the annual re-estimation of the national accounts) was "slightly larger than in recent Blue Books, at 0.66 percentage points, but this includes the whole coronavirus (COVID-19) pandemic period for the first time". The office states the trade-off openly: "There is a trade-off between the timeliness and accuracy of estimates of gross domestic product (GDP). As additional information becomes available, we have a more complete picture of economic activity in that period." The regulator, reviewing complaints about GDP revisions in 2023, concluded that "Revisions to early, near real-time estimates are normal and unavoidable and are an inherent part of the evolving measurement process", that "the more extreme criticisms that ONS received are misplaced", and — the recommendation that applies directly to display — that ONS "should present such information in a central place so that users do not face the challenge of having to navigate through earlier publications and articles to piece the story together." A number that will be revised is a number with a history, and the history should be where the number is.

The ONS distinguishes two things that look alike. *Revisions* are "updates to previously published statistics, analysis or data that improve quality by incorporating improved methods, additional data sources or statistics that were unavailable at the point of initial publication." *Corrections* are "amendments that are made to published statistics in response to the identification of mistakes following their initial publication." The Analysis Function's table shorthand encodes both: [p] "data points are yet to be finalised, or are expected to be revised"; [r] "a data point has been revised since it was first published". A provisional figure that is shown without its [p] is a promise the display did not know it was making.

### Vintages — the third date

Economists keep the history explicitly. The Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis's ALFRED database "allows you to retrieve each economic data release (vintage) that was available on a specific date in history", because "economic data for past observation periods are revised as more accurate estimates become available. As a result, previous vintages of data can be superseded and may no longer be available from various data sources." The Philadelphia Fed's real-time dataset, from the same tradition, consists of "vintages, or snapshots, of time series". A *vintage* is the dataset as it stood on a given release date. The same observation period has many vintages, and a number therefore has at least three dates: the period it describes, the date it was first issued, and the date of the version you are looking at.

The machine standards agree on the three. The W3C's Data Catalog Vocabulary, version 3 (2024), gives a dataset *dct:issued*, "Date of formal issuance (e.g., publication) of the resource"; *dct:modified*, "Most recent date on which the resource was changed, updated or modified", with the note that an absent value "MAY indicate that the resource has never changed after its initial publication, or that the date of last modification is not known, or that the resource is continuously updated"; and *dct:temporal*, the period the resource covers, which it recommends be given as a closed interval. A dashboard's single "last updated" stamp collapses all three, and usually reports the one that matters least — the time the container was refreshed, which says nothing about the age of the contents; Chapter 1's heartbeat is the opposite thing, the visible age of the data.

### A grammar for provenance

The W3C's PROV data model (2013) defines provenance as "A record that describes the people, institutions, entities, and activities involved in producing, influencing, or delivering a piece of data or a thing", and reduces it to three nouns and their relations. An *entity* is "A physical, digital, conceptual, or other kind of thing with some fixed aspects". An *activity* is "Something that occurs over a period of time and acts upon or with entities; it may include consuming, processing, transforming, modifying, relocating, using, or generating entities." An *agent* is "Something that bears some form of responsibility for an activity taking place, for the existence of an entity, or for another agent's activity." The relations are generation, usage, derivation ("A transformation of an entity into another"), attribution ("The ascribing of an entity to an agent"), association, and delegation. It is a grammar: *this number* (entity) *was generated by this method* (activity) *run by this body* (agent) *from this source* (derivation). Every line of the regulator's list fits one of its slots.

The FAIR principles for data stewardship (Wilkinson and colleagues, 2016) add two lines that a dashboard rarely honours. R1.2: "(Meta)data are associated with detailed provenance." And A2: "Metadata are accessible, even when the data are no longer available." The second is a design requirement in disguise. A display that overwrites yesterday's number with today's, keeping no record of what it said yesterday, fails it; the description of a figure should outlive the figure.

### The minimal set

Putting the Code, the European dimensions, PROV, DCAT and FAIR beside each other, the same minimum appears in each, under different names. For any figure a display shows:

- **who is responsible** for it (PROV agent; the Code's producer; the Macpherson review's single responsible owner);
- **what source** it was derived from, and why that source was chosen (PROV derivation; Code 7.3);
- **what method** generated it, and any deviation from a recognised standard (PROV activity; Code 6.7, 7.4);
- **what period** it describes — the reference period, as a closed interval (DCAT temporal; Eurostat);
- **when it was issued and when it last changed** — separately from the period (DCAT issued, modified; the vintage);
- **what status** it has — provisional, revised, estimated, in development (Code 3.10, 7.6; the table shorthand);
- **what quality** it claims, with error and confidence where they exist, in words, numbers or graphics (Code 7.2);
- **what was withheld or altered** for confidentiality (Code 4.5; section 6);
- **what it may be compared with** (Code 6.6, 9.3; section 7);
- and the record must **outlive the number** (FAIR A2).

The Decision Book's own list — "source, reference period, geography and vintage, method, revision status, disclosure treatment, illustrative flag" — matches this with two omissions, the responsible party and the issued/modified dates, and one addition, the illustrative flag, which the standards do not have and which is a Nightlife Culture contribution worth keeping.

One gap in the evidence should be stated before leaving provenance, because the chapter's thesis rests near it. Every source above says what a number *should* carry; none measures what happens when it does. No study was found that tests whether a provenance panel on a public display is opened, read, or changes a reader's decision or trust. The nearest evidence is in earlier chapters — Chapter 1's finding that trust attaches to the producer's conduct, Chapter 2's that parliamentary readers judge research by the credibility of its source before its method — and neither is about a panel on a screen. The provenance rules below are therefore Standard: they are what the regulator and the standards bodies require, adopted; they are not an empirical claim about readers.

## 6 · The six kinds of nothing

The studio names six things a blank can mean: *zero* (counted, and there were none); *free* (a price of nothing); *unavailable* (the value exists but could not be obtained); *unknown* (asked, and no answer); *not collected* (nobody measured it); *suppressed* (withheld on purpose). Chapter 1 said this chapter would define them. The way to do that is to set them against what statistics, databases and surveys already distinguish, because each of those worlds had to solve the same problem and none of them solved it with one blank.

### Why the mechanism matters more than the amount

The statistical theory of missing data begins with Donald Rubin's 1976 paper, whose abstract (†; the paper is paywalled) states the question in terms a dashboard builder should borrow: when is it "appropriate to ignore the process that causes missing data". Not how much is missing; whether the *process* that made it missing can be ignored. The three answers are now standard, and the plainest definitions are in a 2009 *BMJ* paper by Sterne and colleagues. *Missing completely at random*: "There are no systematic differences between the missing values and the observed values. For example, blood pressure measurements may be missing because of breakdown of an automatic sphygmomanometer". *Missing at random*: "Any systematic difference between the missing values and the observed values can be explained by differences in observed data. For example, missing blood pressure measurements may be lower than measured blood pressures but only because younger people may be more likely to have missing blood pressure measurements". *Missing not at random*: "Even after the observed data are taken into account, systematic differences remain between the missing values and the observed values. For example, people with high blood pressure may be more likely to miss clinic appointments because they have headaches".

The third is the one that bites. A venue that does not report because it is struggling, a scene that does not appear in a ticketing feed because it does not sell tickets, a place with no data because nobody thought to collect it there: these are missing not at random, and the gap is correlated with the thing being measured. And the uncomfortable fact, stated by Sterne — "it is not possible to distinguish between missing at random and missing not at random using observed data" — and by the National Research Council's panel on missing data in clinical trials, chaired by Roderick Little: "With incomplete data, assumptions about both the missing data mechanism and the full data model are unverifiable from the data." You cannot tell from the data you have why the data you do not have is absent. The panel's answer is a sensitivity analysis: the analyst "should assess the robustness of the treatment effect inferences by conducting a sensitivity analysis" — show what would change if the nothing were something. Sterne's version for publication: "Where complete cases and multiple imputation analyses give different results, the analyst should attempt to understand why, and this should be reported in publications." "The risk of bias due to missing data depends on the reasons why data are missing" — the reason, not the rate.

How the field actually behaves is documented. Schafer and Graham's 2002 review found that "Case deletion, also known commonly as listwise deletion (LD) and complete-case analysis, is used by default in many statistical programs", that "When the missing data are not MCAR, results from case deletion may be biased, because the complete cases can be unrepresentative of the full population", and that "ad hoc edits may do more harm than good, producing answers that are biased, inefficient (lacking in power), and unreliable." Twelve years later, Bell and colleagues reviewed the 77 randomised trials published in the four leading medical journals in the second half of 2013: 95% had missing outcome data; "The most commonly used method to handle missing data in the primary analysis was complete case analysis (33, 45%)"; 8% used multiple imputation; 35% reported any sensitivity analysis. The default — drop the incomplete rows and say nothing — is the default in the best-refereed science there is. A dashboard that aggregates over whatever rows arrived is doing the same thing with less scrutiny.

### Dark data

David Hand's 2020 book *Dark Data* gives the subject a taxonomy of fifteen types, and the two that matter most here are the first two: "DD-Type 1: Data We Know Are Missing" and "DD-Type 2: Data We Don't Know Are Missing". His definition, from a short article the same year: dark data are "data you do not have. They might be data you thought you had, or hoped to have, or wished you had." The danger is in Type 2, where "we are unaware of them", and his examples are a city's pothole-reporting app, whose reports cluster where "ownership of cars and expensive smartphones is more likely to be concentrated in wealthier areas", and tweets during Hurricane Sandy, where "those regions transmitting fewer tweets may have been doing so not because the storm had less impact but simply because there were fewer Twitter users with fewer smartphones to tweet them." Both are maps that look complete. The six kinds of nothing are, in Hand's terms, an attempt to turn Type 2 into Type 1 — to make every absence a known absence with a stated reason. Several of his other types reappear in this chapter under other names: Type 3 "Choosing Just Some Cases" and Type 4 "Self-Selection" are section 9; Type 7 "Changes with Time" and Type 8 "Definitions of Data" are section 7; Type 9 "Summaries of Data" is section 8; Type 11 "Feedback and Gaming" is Chapter 5's.

### What the database did

The relational database's inventor saw the problem in 1986 and proposed two kinds of null. E. F. Codd's paper distinguishes the *A-mark*, "missing and applicable" — "Suppose that, represented in the database, there is an object z of type Z and, at this time, the value of P for this object is unknown. Then, P would be assigned an A-mark" — from the *I-mark*, "missing and inapplicable": "suppose that property P is inapplicable to the particular object z. Then P would be assigned an inapplicable-value mark." His examples: "an employee with a missing-but-applicable present salary — his record would have an A-mark in the salary attribute"; "an employee with an inapplicable sales commission (such an employee does not sell any products at this time) — his record would have an I-mark." He insisted these were marks, not values: "A basic principle of the relational approach to missing information is that of recording the fact that a db-value is missing by means of a mark … There is nothing imprecise about a mark".

And he condemned the practice of using zero for missing: "In at least one well-known (and otherwise sound) DBMS product, zero has been chosen as the value to indicate missing information in all numeric attributes. I consider the number zero to be far too valuable in its normal role in all kinds of business activities — for example, as a real number representing the actual quantity of a part in stock or the actual quantity of currency owed by one or more customers. Therefore, I do not consider zero to be an acceptable choice to be reserved by a DBMS for denoting a missing numeric db-value." He went further: "it is unacceptable to reserve any specific numeric value or any specific character string value to denote the fact that a db-value is missing."

The industry did not follow him. The SQL-92 standard, which every relational database still implements, states: "A null value is an implementation-dependent special value that is distinct from all non-null values of the associated data type. There is effectively only one null value and that value is a member of every SQL data type." One bucket for every kind of nothing. The consequences are documented in the PostgreSQL manual, which is quoted here because it is the clearest statement of what every warehouse does. "Ordinary comparison operators yield null (signifying "unknown"), not true or false, when either input is null." Aggregates skip nulls silently: *sum* "Computes the sum of the non-null input values"; *avg* "Computes the average (arithmetic mean) of all the non-null input values" — so an average over a column with unknowns is an average of the knowns, with no flag. *count(\*)* "Computes the number of input rows" while *count(column)* "Computes the number of input rows in which the input value is not null", and the two differ by exactly the nothings. And the sentence that explains a thousand dashboards: "sum of no rows returns null, not zero as one might expect … The coalesce function can be used to substitute zero or an empty array for null when necessary." `coalesce(sum(x), 0)` is the one-line idiom by which "no rows arrived" becomes a displayed 0. It is in almost every reporting query ever written, and it is Codd's prohibited substitution, automated.

Hugh Darwen, co-author with C. J. Date of the standard critique of SQL's null, shows the arithmetic going wrong — "SELECT SUM(X) + SUM(Y) FROM T is not equal to SELECT SUM(X + Y) FROM T" — and proposes the cure in 2012 lecture slides: do not keep one table with one kind of blank; keep separate tables for the known salaries, the salaries whose amount is unknown, and the people who have no salary. "Don't combine multiple meanings in a single relvar" (a relvar is a table, in relational theory's vocabulary). One table per meaning of a blank is the same instinct as one label per kind of nothing, arrived at from the database side. (The slides are a lecture, not a paper, and are marked Practice.)

### The official symbols for nothing

The UK Government Analysis Function's guidance "Using symbols and shorthand in tables" (2022) is the vocabulary the Decision Book already adopts, and it is given here in full because each code is a kind of nothing with a name. [b] "break in time series … data before the break cannot be directly compared with data after the break". [c] "confidential … a data point would give away confidential information … These data points must be replaced with this shorthand". [e] "estimated". [f] "forecast … a calculated future value instead of an observed value". [low] "a low figure but not a real zero … data points that appear as zero in a rounded table, but are not actually zero". [p] "provisional". [r] "revised". [u] "low reliability". [w] "none recorded in survey … This could be because there were not any recorded by the survey or because none exist in the population". [x] "not available … unavailable for reasons other than those described in this list. For example, where data is not collected in a region". [z] "not applicable … For example, in tables of employment where people under 16 cannot legally be employed". And the rule on zero: "A zero or '0' should only be used when a data point is a true zero."

Three things in the guidance are rules in their own right. First, *No to NA*: "We do not recommend using 'NA' to describe cells with no data. This is because this shorthand is ambiguous. Some people may read it as 'Not Applicable', while other people may read it as 'Not Available'." Second, no symbols: "we do not recommend using symbols such as '. :', '*' or '†' anymore because they can be difficult to see and screen reader software can find them hard to recognise" — the shorthand goes in square brackets, as letters. Third, from the companion guidance on spreadsheets, the blank-cell rule, which is subtler than "never leave a cell blank". Blank cells are acceptable only when "There is only one reason a cell in a table may be left blank", the table is marked up correctly, and "There is a note above the table, in a cell in column A, explaining that some cells are left blank and why". Otherwise: "If there are several reasons a cell in a table may be left blank you will need to use shorthand to explain why a particular cell has no data." More than one kind of nothing forces a label on every nothing. The studio's thesis is, in UK government guidance, an accessibility rule — guidance, not law, though it is the guidance the regulator's Code points to.

Eurostat's flags make the same distinctions with different letters and add one. Its table: "0 | real zero"; "0n | less than half of the final digit shown and greater than real zero"; ": | not available"; "- (or z) | not applicable"; "c | confidential"; "b | break in time series"; "d | definition differs, see metadata"; "e | estimated value"; "p | provisional value"; "u | low reliability". Its footnote: "Never use NA or ND, or other variations to indicate data not available." Its blank rule: "empty table cells should be omitted. Therefore, flags for not available and confidential data should be always included." And a caution that belongs in any style guide: "It is good practice to not overload tables with too many flags, which make it difficult for readers to grasp the main data patterns within tables quickly."

The international data-exchange standard, SDMX, has the richest official list. Its observation-status code list distinguishes, among others: A "Normal value"; O "Missing value" — "to be used when no breakdown is made between the reasons why data are missing"; M "Missing value; data cannot exist" — "the impossibility to collect a statistical value (e.g. a particular education level or type of institution may be not applicable to a given country's education system)"; L "Missing value; data exist but were not collected" — "for example, when some data are not reported/disseminated because they are below a certain threshold"; Q "Missing value; suppressed" — "when data are suppressed due to statistical confidentiality considerations"; H "Missing value; holiday or weekend" — "Used in some daily data flows"; N "Not significant" — "a value which is not a 'real' zero (e.g. a result of 0.0004 rounded to zero)"; K "Data included in another category", with a mandatory matching W "Includes data from another category" on the cell that absorbed it; E "Estimated value", for an estimate made by the sender; and I "Value imputed by a receiving agency". A separate list, the confidentiality status, records *why* something was withheld: primary confidentiality "due to small counts"; primary confidentiality "due to dominance by one unit", or two; secondary confidentiality set by the sender; secondary confidentiality set by the receiver; and, amusingly, F for "Free (free for publication)".

Two public-health vocabularies show how far this goes in practice. The US surveillance system's weekly data legend: "U: Unavailable — The reporting jurisdiction was unable to send the data to CDC or CDC was unable to process the data." "-: No reported cases — The reporting jurisdiction did not submit any cases to CDC." "N: Not reportable — The disease or condition was not reportable by law, statute, or regulation in the reporting jurisdiction." "NC: Not calculated — There is insufficient data available to support the calculation of this statistic." The dash is deliberately not zero; it sits between zero and unknown. And the UK's Understanding Society survey assigns nine negative codes to its missing values, all negative so they can never collide with an answer: -1 "Don't know"; -2 "Refused"; -7 proxy respondent; -8 "Valid skip" — "the person was never asked this question as they were not eligible for it"; -9 "Missing by error or implausible"; and four codes for not-in-this-sample.

### How the six map

Set the studio's six against these and three things appear.

| The studio's kind | What the official vocabularies say |
|---|---|
| **Zero** — counted, none | "0", "only … when a data point is a true zero"; Eurostat "0 real zero". Three neighbours that are *not* zero: [low] / "0n" / SDMX N (rounds to zero but is not); [w] "none recorded in survey" (a sample zero that may not be a population zero); and, in the statistics of tables, the *structural* zero of a cell that is "not possible" against the *sampling* zero of a cell that has "positive probability but we didn't observe anything there" (a teaching note's wording; the textbooks are not open). |
| **Free** — a price of nothing | No scheme has a code. Every scheme treats it as the value 0. The ONS, handling services that became chargeable such as university fees in 1998 and the London congestion charge in 2003, states the position: "We treat the new product as if it were already included in an existing item index with zero price but with non-zero quantity equal to its consumption in the base period." Free is a value with a quantity behind it. Economists who argue GDP under-measures free digital goods propose a separate measure (GDP-B †) rather than a code — because the problem is not that free is missing but that zero-price times quantity is zero by construction. |
| **Unavailable** — exists, not obtained | [x]; Eurostat ":"; SDMX L "data exist but were not collected"; SDMX H holiday or weekend; CDC U; survey -9; Codd's A-mark; SQL NULL. The Australian Bureau of Statistics once also had "n.y.a. — not yet available", a temporal kind the studio does not name. |
| **Unknown** — asked, no answer | [x] (the UK shorthand has no finer code, so *unknown* and *unavailable* share a symbol — a limit of the UK scheme the studio's six improve on); survey -1 "Don't know" and -2 "Refused" — a split the studio does not make; Codd's A-mark. |
| **Not collected** — nobody measured | The official world splits this in two. *Could not exist*: [z] not applicable; SDMX M "data cannot exist"; Eurostat "-"; Codd's I-mark; survey -8 valid skip; CDC N not reportable. *Could exist but nobody asked*: [x] "where data is not collected in a region"; SDMX L; survey -10/-11/-20/-21 (not in this sample or study). |
| **Suppressed** — withheld | [c], and "must be replaced with this shorthand"; Eurostat "c"; SDMX Q plus a confidentiality-status code saying why (small count; dominance; secondary); CDC NP "notifiable but not published". |

First: the official world holds, as the studio does, that a blank must say which kind it is, and the official vocabularies are finer than the studio's in two places and coarser in one. Second: it splits "not collected" into *not applicable* (the value cannot exist) and *not asked* (it could, and nobody did), and every scheme — UK, European, international, database, survey, surveillance — makes that split. Whether "not applicable" is a seventh kind or a sub-kind of the fifth is a decision for the studio, and this chapter flags it rather than making it. Third: nobody has a code for free, and the reason is instructive — free is not a nothing. It is a zero in the price column and a count in the quantity column, and a display that files it with the nothings has lost the quantity. One more kind that the vocabularies name and the studio's six do not: SDMX's K, "included in another category" — the nothing that is actually hiding inside a neighbouring cell. A sub-total that absorbed a suppressed row is the commonest case.

### Imputation, and what a display owes

When a nothing is filled in, the fill is an estimate, and the standards say so in two ways. The US National Center for Education Statistics' standard 4-1-7: "All imputed values on a data file must be clearly identified as such", with the guideline that "Imputed data should be flagged in associated 'flag' fields. The imputation method should be identified in the flag", and standard 4-1-6 requiring that "documentation indicating the weighted proportion of imputed data must be presented for all published estimates". The Analysis Function's companion guidance asks producers to "Include high-level information on any adjustments made to the data and statistics before publication (for example, imputation or seasonal adjustment)", to publish "imputation rates", and to put quality information where it will be seen: it "should be prominent, easy to find, clearly signposted and not hidden away in background appendices or footnotes". The American Statistical Association's ethical guidelines put the same duty on the person: the ethical practitioner "Communicates data processing and transformation procedures, including missing data handling."

The UK's table shorthand has no code for "imputed"; imputation hides inside [e] "estimated". SDMX and Eurostat go one better and record *who* estimated — sender or receiver, national office or Eurostat — a distinction few dashboards make, and one that matters whenever a platform fills gaps in data it did not collect. The method the statistical literature recommends, multiple imputation, is recommended precisely because it refuses to present one invented number as if it were observed: in Schafer and Graham's description, "each missing value is replaced by a list of m > 1 simulated values", the analyses are combined, and the result carries "standard errors that reflect missing-data uncertainty as well as finite-sample variation". A filled gap has an uncertainty that a measured value does not, and the display owes the reader the difference.

### Drawing a nothing

There is exactly one official rule found for drawing a gap on a chart, and it is in the Analysis Function's chart guidance under "Breaks in time series", quoted in full: "Discontinuities in time series can occur due to missing data points and changes in methods. The best way to deal with discontinuity varies depending on the data and the story you are telling. But, it must always be highlighted. You do not always have to use a line for a time series with a discontinuity. Displaying the data as individual points may be better. If you do use a line, do not join the points either side of the missing data point, even if the line is dotted or dashed. Joining points implies we know something about the data." The approved example marks the gap with two vertical dashed lines and a note that data was not collected in 2020; the rejected example is a dashed join. The Decision Book's current rule — "Show gaps explicitly — dashed, hatched, or broken, with a note" — can be read as permitting the dashed join the standard rejects.

Two cases show what happens when a nothing is drawn as a number. The CDC's provisional death counts carry a technical note: "The data shown on this page may be incomplete, and will likely not include all deaths that occurred during a given time period, especially for the more recent time periods"; deaths are "65% complete within 2 weeks, 85% complete within 4 weeks, and 95% complete within 8 weeks of when the death occurred." The right-hand end of every lagged series is a not-yet shown as a low number, and a line that runs to the edge of the chart draws a decline that is a reporting delay. And in October 2020 Public Health England reported that 15,841 positive test results from 25 September to 2 October were not included in daily figures because "some files containing positive test results exceeded the maximum file size that takes these data files and loads then into central systems" — a pipeline limit, in the official statement's words; the press attributed it to a spreadsheet row limit, which the statement does not say. For eight days the published numbers were lower than the truth, and nothing on the display distinguished a low count from a dropped file. The kind of nothing was *unavailable*; the display showed *zero*.

### Suppression, and its opposite

Suppression is the sixth kind and the one Chapter 1 traced to its root. The Analysis Function's introduction to statistical disclosure control: "The suppression of unsafe cells is known as 'primary suppression'", after which "other cells must also be suppressed to prevent the values of the unsafe cells being calculated by subtraction from the marginal totals" — secondary suppression — and the suppressed cell is shown by "replacing the value within the cells with a 'c' or some other symbol." It is never blanked, because a blank would be indistinguishable from the other five kinds. The Decision Book's disclosure rules are a correct reading of this guidance.

The 2021 Census chose the opposite trade. Rather than suppress small cells, the ONS swapped rare records between areas and perturbed counts: "Where there is a cell count of one in a published output, there is a high chance that it could be a swapped record, a perturbed count or an imputed record, or a combination of these", and "the totals of all cells may in turn be different … the differences should be small and so should not change the conclusions of any analysis or research." A table with no blanks and no literal small numbers: every cell is quietly [e], and the small counts a local reader would lean on are the least literal. Each approach is defensible; each puts a different kind of nothing behind what looks like a number, and a display built on either should say which.

## 7 · Comparability — what a comparison must declare

### The definition

European law defines comparability as "the measurement of the impact of differences in applied statistical concepts, measurement tools and procedures where statistics are compared between geographical areas, sectoral domains or over time". Eurostat's operational manual gives the definition this chapter leans on most: comparability is the "Extent to which differences between statistics can be attributed to differences between the true values of the statistical characteristics." A comparison is valid *to the extent that* the difference you see is a difference in the world and not a difference in how the two numbers were made. Everything that is not the thing you are comparing must be held equal or declared. The European Statistics Code of Practice turns that into practice under Principle 14: statistics "are compiled on the basis of common standards with respect to scope, definitions, units and classifications in the different surveys and data sources"; "Statistics are comparable over a reasonable period of time"; and "Statistics from the different data sources and of different periodicity are compared and reconciled." The UK Code's practice 6.6 asks for data "coherent when aggregated, consistent over time and comparable across and within geographies at regional, national and local levels, where possible".

The regulator concedes that perfect comparability does not exist and offers a working standard instead. Its 2025 review of UK-wide statistics introduces "meaningful comparability" — "the minimum level of comparability that is needed for drawing firm conclusions and for shaping action" — and separates coherence (consistency within and between related sources, with differences explained) from comparability (the degree to which statistics can be meaningfully compared across geographies and time). Differences in method or definition, it says, must be documented and judged case by case against what the user needs the comparison for. That is the honest position for a dashboard: not "these are comparable" but "these are comparable enough for this, and here is what differs".

### Breaks

When a definition changes, the series breaks, and the break has a symbol. The Analysis Function's [b] is "a break in a data series that means that data before the break cannot be directly compared with data after the break" — the cleanest one-line statement of what a break means for a reader. Eurostat's handling of the 2021 change in labour-force definitions shows what honest practice costs: countries supplied "either complete break-free series to Eurostat or correction factors" for the years 2009 to 2020, and the old tables were renamed with "the letter 'h' for historical series". Eurostat's quality grading rates comparability over time by the "number of data points from last methodological break": more than four is high, three or four medium, fewer than three low. The UK Code's practice 7.5 asks producers to "provide a consistent back series where possible". A dashboard that changed how it counts something and kept drawing one line through the change has a [b] it is not showing.

### Geography, and the vintage of a boundary

The UK's statistical geography policy is built on versioning, though it does not use that word. Statistics "should be built from aggregations of statistical building blocks". Codes, not names, identify areas: "Coding changes will be due to actual boundary changes"; "Codes are not re-used"; a name change alone does not produce a new code. The ONS keeps a Code History Database recording when codes were allocated and retired. The practical guidance is blunt: "Local Authority boundaries change frequently so ensure you use the correct year to avoid errors"; mismatches arise when "you are joining data to boundaries which have different codes in areas due to boundary changes"; and you "should avoid creating your own boundaries or modifying existing ones." English local government was reorganised in 2009, 2019, 2020, 2021 and 2023. Where small areas do not nest exactly in larger ones, the *best-fit* policy applies: lower geographies "do not fit exactly into the boundary of the higher geography; instead, they form an approximation to the shape of the boundary", and "Generally, the bigger the target geography, the less difference there will be." A figure for a named place is a figure for that place's boundary on a date, assembled by a stated method from building blocks; two figures for the same name across a boundary change are two different places unless the lookup is declared. The Decision Book's rules on boundary vintage and best-fit lookups are a correct reading of this policy.

### Definitions, and harmonisation

The UK's harmonisation programme exists because, in its own words, "Without information about how data and statistics can be compared, users may come to incorrect conclusions", and its example of a bad comparison is "surveys where the questions or definitions are different." Harmonised standards exist for age, household, national identity, sexual orientation, sex and gender, general health, disability, unpaid care, marital status and more. Where a dashboard asks a question it wrote itself — about who attends, who works, who feels welcome — the harmonised standard is the comparator that makes the answer joinable to anything else, and the cost of inventing a category is that the figure stands alone for ever.

### Finance's convergent list

Financial regulation reached much the same requirements for a different audience — investors reading company accounts — and the resemblance is, in our reading, a sign that the list is general rather than a statistical habit; the two regulators are both European bodies, so the independence is of purpose rather than of origin. The European Securities and Markets Authority's guidelines on alternative performance measures — figures like "like-for-like sales" that are not defined by accounting standards — require that issuers "should define the APMs used and their components as well as the basis of calculation adopted"; that "A reconciliation of the APM to the most directly reconcilable line item, subtotal or total presented in the financial statements of the corresponding period should be disclosed"; that "APMs should be accompanied by comparatives for the corresponding previous periods"; and that "The definition and calculation of an APM should be consistent over time. In exceptional circumstances where issuers … decide to redefine an APM, the issuer should: i. Explain the changes; ii. explain the reasons why these changes result in reliable and more relevant information … and iii. provide restated comparative figures." Define it; reconcile it to something audited; show the comparator; keep the definition; if you change it, restate the past. Tesco's footnote does all four in three sentences, and adds that its figures "are presented for the 52 weeks to 22 February 2026 to aid comparability" — a trading year of 52 weeks rather than a calendar year, because weeks are equal containers and months are not.

### Choosing what to compare with

A place compared with "the national average" is compared with something that contains it and does not resemble it. The alternatives are *statistical neighbours*, and how they are chosen is itself a modelled claim that must be declared. Three UK methods show the range. The Chartered Institute of Public Finance and Accountancy's Nearest Neighbours model uses "40 metrics using a wide range of social-economic indicators", chosen to be "descriptive of characteristics of the area each authority administers and not of the way in which resource of services are taken into account"; its own page concedes that "the outcome ultimately relies on the indicators and mathematical procedures used." Public Health England's technical briefing explains the mechanism: "For each area, the nearest neighbour is derived by calculating the 'Euclidean distance' to all the other areas' data once they have been standardised to a normal distribution." The Department for Education's children's-services model, published in full in 2025, is the auditable version: 23 background variables reduced from 50 by correlation analysis, standardised to mean 0 and standard deviation 1, "Weighted Euclidean distance on the standardised background variables", ten neighbours per authority, weights set by iterative optimisation, variables "updated annually". And the ONS area classifications group places by "a standard K-means clustering technique" on sixty census variables into eight supergroups and twenty-four subgroups.

Two cautions from the public-health briefing are rules in themselves. First, circularity: "if the selected comparator includes the factor being compared, the comparison becomes less meaningful. For example, comparing employment indicators with other areas that have a similar Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) will not provide full context as employment indicators make up 22.5% of the IMD calculation." The comparator model must not contain the outcome. Second, that the easy comparators are not performance measures: "comparing areas to other areas within the same region or to a national value may not indicate performance." A dashboard that says "similar places" owes the reader the list, the variables and the method — and if it cannot supply them, it should say "places we chose".

### The declaration

Assembled from the sources above, each element sourced, this is what a comparison on a display must declare if the reader is to judge whether the difference is in the world:

1. **Same definition and classification** — or the difference stated (Code of Practice 6.7; European Code 14.3; harmonisation; ESMA).
2. **Same reference period and periodicity** — or reconciled (Regulation 223/2009; European Code 14.2, 14.4; ESMA comparatives).
3. **Same geography and same boundary vintage** — with the lookup named where best-fit was used (Code 6.6; geography policy).
4. **Same method and collection mode** — or the change announced with a back series (Regulation 223/2009; Code 7.4, 7.5).
5. **Same disclosure treatment** — so a suppressed or rounded cell is not read against an unsuppressed one ([c], [low]; Code 4.5).
6. **Breaks flagged** — [b], with the direction of the discontinuity if known (Analysis Function; Eurostat; Code 7.5).
7. **Revision status and vintage stated** — [p], [r], and which release each number came from (Code 3.9; section 5).
8. **Comparator group named and its method stated** — variables, distance, number of neighbours; and the comparator must not contain the outcome (PHE; DfE; CIPFA).
9. **Precision declared, especially for small bases** — intervals where numbers are small; a funnel, not a rank (Code 7.2; PHE; Gelman & Price; Spiegelhalter).
10. **Outlier handling declared** — the rule, the threshold, and the figure with and without (section 10).

Filled in for an ordinary case, the declaration is shorter than it looks. For a comparison of two councils' monthly counts of licensed premises: *same definition* (premises licences under the 2003 Act, both councils' registers); *same period* (calendar months, 2024, as at the first of the following month); *same geography* (2023 local authority boundaries, codes stated; no best-fit); *same method* (register extract, both; one council's register was migrated in March 2024 — [b]); *same disclosure* (none — counts above the threshold); *breaks* (March 2024, council B); *revisions* (council A's register is live and re-extracted monthly, [r] on prior months); *comparator* (the two were chosen by the reader, not modelled); *precision* (full counts; no interval); *outliers* (none removed). Ten lines, about eighty words. Ten items is long for a footnote. It is not long for a "how this compares" panel or a method page one tap away — provided, per Chapter 3, that the fact that a comparison is qualified is visible on the face and not only the qualification.

## 8 · Why aggregation hides the small

### A total is a statement about its biggest terms

The arithmetic is old. In 1905 Max Lorenz, introducing the curve that bears his name, warned that "average income is no safe criterion", showing a case "where the average income of the highest class shows a marked increase, although there was no change in the degree of concentration." The US standards body's handbook of statistical methods states the modern rule: "Extreme values in the tails distort the mean. However, these extreme values do not distort the median since the median is based on ranks." The ONS chooses the median as its headline measure of earnings because "the distribution of earnings is skewed, with more people earning lower salaries than higher salaries" and the mean is "highly influenced by those values at the upper end of the distribution". A sum is the mean multiplied by the count, so a total inherits the mean's dependence on its largest terms. In any skewed distribution, a total or a mean is, arithmetically, a statement dominated by its largest contributors. A median or a share is not. The second failure on Carly's register — big numbers that do not tell you what happens — is this arithmetic seen from the reader's side.

### Measuring concentration

The remedy is to show the shape, and there are four standard instruments. Lorenz's curve, in his words: "Plot along one axis cumulated per cents of the population from poorest to richest, and along the other the per cent of the total wealth held by these per cents of the population." Equal distribution gives a straight line; "With an unequal distribution, the curves will always begin and end in the same points as with an equal distribution, but they will be bent in the middle; and the rule of interpretation will be, as the bow is bent, concentration increases." The Gini index summarises the bow: it "measures the area between the Lorenz curve and a hypothetical line of absolute equality, expressed as a percentage of the maximum area under the line", from 0 for perfect equality to 100 for perfect inequality; or, equivalently, "the average share of mean consumption or income that needs to be transferred between two randomly selected individuals to achieve equality." The top-share is the plainest: "the percentage of total income in a population that is held by the tenth or top decile". And the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index, used by competition regulators, "is calculated by squaring the market share of each firm competing in the market and then summing the resulting numbers" — "For a market consisting of four firms with shares of 30, 30, 20, and 20 percent, the HHI is 2,600" — so that it "approaches zero when a market is occupied by a large number of firms of relatively equal size and reaches its maximum of 10,000 points when a market is controlled by a single firm". Under the 2023 US merger guidelines a market above 1,800 is "highly concentrated". The squaring is the point: the HHI makes the biggest count more than proportionally, which is exactly what a total does without saying so.

### The 80/20 slogan

The "Pareto principle" is the folk version of concentration, and its history is a caution against treating it as a law. Joseph Juran, who popularised it in quality management, wrote in 1975 a short piece titled "The Non-Pareto Principle; Mea Culpa" (read in a reproduction — secondary): "On subsequent challenge, I was forced to confess that I had mistakenly applied the wrong name to the principle." The cumulative curves in his handbook should have been "properly identified with Lorenz". The Juran Institute's current page credits Pareto for observing in 1895 "that a relative few people held the majority of the wealth (20%)" and Juran for the terms "vital few" and "trivial many". Nobody in this lineage states 80/20 as a law; Juran's framing is of an empirical pattern that recurs. The right way to use it on a display is as a question — how bent is this bow? — answered with a curve, a share or an index, not as a number assumed in advance.

### Why cultural markets are so skewed

Two mechanisms, and an experiment. Sherwin Rosen's 1981 paper on the economics of superstars opens with the phenomenon — "relatively small numbers of people earn enormous amounts of money and dominate the activities in which they engage" — and gives two reasons. Imperfect substitution: "Lesser talent often is a poor substitute for greater talent. The worse it is the larger the sustainable rent accruing to higher quality sellers because demand for the better sellers increases more than proportionately." And joint consumption: "A performer or an author must put out more or less the same effort whether 10 or 1,000 people show up in the audience or buy the book." Where the second holds — recordings, streams, broadcasts — the cost of reaching one more listener is near zero and the best can serve everyone.

Then the experiment. In 2006 Matthew Salganik, Peter Dodds and Duncan Watts built an artificial music market "in which 14,341 participants downloaded previously unknown songs either with or without knowledge of previous participants' choices." The abstract reports: "Increasing the strength of social influence increased both inequality and unpredictability of success. Success was also only partly determined by quality: The best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well, but any other result was possible." They measured inequality with the Gini coefficient — "In both experiments, we found that all eight social influence worlds (dark bars) exhibit greater inequality" — which ties the experiment to Lorenz's curve a century earlier. The finding that matters for a public display: showing people what others chose makes the distribution more concentrated *and* less predictable. A dashboard that ranks by popularity is a social-influence world. Chapter 1's truth that a published measure changes the behaviour it measures has, here, an experimental mechanism.

### The long tail, in the author's figures

Chris Anderson's 2004 thesis — that unlimited shelf space would let the aggregate demand for non-hits rival the hits — could not be read at source for this chapter and is not quoted. Anita Elberse's 2008 test of it in the *Harvard Business Review* (read in a reprint — secondary) could. On the Rhapsody streaming service: "the top 10% of titles accounted for 78% of all plays, and the top 1% of titles for 32% of all plays". On recorded music sales in 2007, of 3.9 million digital tracks, "an astonishing 24% sold only one copy, and 91%—3.6 million tracks—sold fewer than 100 copies". The tail is "becoming much longer and flatter", with "a rapidly increasing number of titles that sell very rarely or never", while the head concentrates further: "The number of titles in the top 10% of weekly sales dropped by more than 50%". Her advice to firms: "Don't radically alter blockbuster resource-allocation or product-portfolio management strategies. A few winners will still go a long way." The long tail is mostly a tail of ones and zeros. A total over such a distribution is a statement about the head; a mean describes almost nobody in it; and a display that shows the sum and calls it "the market" has shown the top tenth.

Two further points from the streaming literature are about grain and coverage rather than shape. The individual-level panel study (section 3) found diversification where market-level studies find concentration. And the data most studies of streaming use are head-only: Aguiar and Waldfogel's analysis of Spotify's effect on sales was limited to Spotify's public releases of "the top 50 songs", so that whatever it says about the tail is said about what it could not see. A feed that publishes its top fifty cannot support a claim about its bottom fifty thousand.

### The worked case: 78% and 32%

The studio's own finding, from its ticketing analysis, is that venues under 1,000 capacity put on 78% of paid events but take 32% of ticketing revenue. That is a *Nightlife Culture* finding; this chapter has not researched it, cannot verify it, and uses it only as the worked case the brief asks for. What the chapter can do is say what the finding needs in order to be evidence rather than a striking pair of numbers, applying the sections above to it.

It is a top-share statement read from the bottom: the complement of "venues of 1,000 and over take 68% of revenue from 22% of events". Its *grain* is the venue, so it needs the definition of a venue — enterprise, local unit or room (section 3) — the capacity threshold's source, and the number of venues and events behind each percentage. Its *period* needs stating, and whether event date or sale date was used. Its *denominator* is twofold — all paid events, all ticketing revenue — and both are the feed's, not the world's: the share of events that are ticketed at all, and the share of ticketed events the platform sees, are the coverage questions of section 9, and "free" events are, by the ONS's logic, zero-price events with a real quantity that the revenue share cannot see. Its *comparator* is implicit (small against large); the Lorenz curve of revenue by venue, with its Gini, would show the whole bow rather than one point on it, and would let a reader see whether the concentration is in the top 1% or spread across the top fifth. Its *provenance* needs the ten lines of section 5. And, as it happens, its two numbers are the same two numbers Elberse reports for Rhapsody — the top 10% of titles with 78% of plays and the top 1% with 32% — a coincidence of figures and not of meaning, since hers describe the head and the studio's describe the tail; it is noted here only so that nobody later mistakes one for a citation of the other. By this chapter's first truth, the finding is undefined until those are supplied: as it stands it is a striking pair of numbers, and the chapter owes it the same sentence it gives everyone else's. Supplying them is what would turn it into something the sceptical reader of Chapter 2 cannot wave away, and the prototype can supply them.

## 9 · Digital-trace, transactional and administrative data

### Size does not cure selection

The single most important result in this chapter for any dashboard fed by a platform is Xiao-Li Meng's 2018 paper in the *Annals of Applied Statistics*. Its subject is the error of an estimate made from a large but non-random dataset, and it decomposes that error into exactly three factors: "(1) a data quality measure, ρ_R,X, the correlation between X_j and the response/recording indicator R_j; (2) a data quantity measure, √((N − n)/n), where N is the population size; and (3) a problem difficulty measure, σ_X, the standard deviation of X." Quality times quantity times difficulty. The quality term is "the data defect correlation" — how much being *recorded* correlates with the *value* recorded — and it "precisely measures both the sign and degree of selection bias caused by the R-mechanism".

Then the number. Using 2016 US election polling, with a data defect correlation estimated at about −0.005 — half of one per cent — Meng shows that "the simple sample proportion of the self-reported voting preference for Trump from 1% of the US eligible voters, that is, n ≈ 2,300,000, has the same mean squared error as the corresponding sample proportion from a genuine simple random sample of size n ≈ 400, a 99.98% reduction of sample size". Two point three million self-selected records were worth four hundred random ones, and the correlation that did it was tiny. His Law of Large Populations says the problem gets *worse* with the size of the population: for studies "sharing the same (fixed) average data defect correlation", the error relative to a random sample "grows with the population size N at the rate of √N". And the Big Data Paradox, in his exact words: "the more the data, the surer we fool ourselves".

Every ticketing feed, streaming feed, card-spend feed, footfall sensor and app log is a recording mechanism with a defect correlation, and the correlation is almost never zero, because who gets recorded depends on who uses the platform, and who uses the platform is correlated with what is being measured. Meng's identity gives the dashboard builder two honest moves: estimate the defect correlation (by comparing the feed to a known random benchmark, where one exists) or report the feed's findings as *within-sample* comparisons — this platform's users, this year against last — and never as population claims.

### What went wrong with Google Flu

The best-known case study is Lazer, Kennedy, King and Vespignani's 2014 article in *Science* on Google Flu Trends, which at one point was "predicting more than double the proportion of doctor visits for influenza-like illness" that the CDC's surveillance showed. The article's body text was read in two independent teaching reproductions (secondary; the publisher page was not reachable), and three sentences carry the lesson. "'Big data hubris' is the often implicit assumption that big data are a substitute for, rather than a supplement to, traditional data collection and analysis." "Algorithm dynamics are the changes made by engineers to improve the commercial service and by consumers in using that service" — the search engine kept changing, and so did the people using it, so the mapping from searches to flu drifted. And: "The core challenge is that most big data that have received popular attention are not the output of instruments designed to produce valid and reliable data amenable for scientific analysis." A platform's data is a by-product of the platform's purpose, and the platform owes its data nothing.

### The ten characteristics

Matthew Salganik's 2018 textbook *Bit by Bit* gives the checklist, and each line is quoted from its own summary sentence. Three characteristics are, in his words, "generally helpful for research": *big* ("Large datasets are a means to an end; they are not an end in themselves"), *always-on* ("Always-on big data enables the study of unexpected events and real-time measurement"), and *nonreactive* ("Measurement in big data sources is much less likely to change behavior"). Seven are "generally problematic for research": *incomplete* ("No matter how big your big data, it probably doesn't have the information you want" — typically missing "demographic information about participants, behavior on other platforms, and data to operationalize theoretical constructs"); *inaccessible* ("Data held by companies and governments are difficult for researchers to access"); *nonrepresentative* ("Nonrepresentative data are bad for out-of-sample generalizations, but can be quite useful for within-sample comparisons" — Meng's conclusion in one sentence); *drifting* ("Population drift, usage drift, and system drift make it hard to use big data sources to study long-term trends"); *algorithmically confounded* ("Behavior in big data systems is not natural; it is driven by the engineering goals of the systems" — his example is a platform that "encouraged people with few connections … to make more friends until they reached 20 friends", so that a histogram of friend counts showed a bump at 20 that was the product's doing); *dirty* ("Big data sources can be loaded with junk and spam" — in one study of pager messages on 11 September 2001, "Removing the messages generated by this single automated pager completely eliminates the apparent increase in anger over the course of the day"); and *sensitive* ("Some of the information that companies and governments have is sensitive"). The pager case is also section 8's point in miniature: one unit dominated an aggregate and became a finding. The ten are a textbook's framework, marked Practice; each is a question to ask of a feed before a number from it is shown.

Two framings from the critical literature sit beside this. Kitchin and McArdle, examining 26 datasets, concluded that the traits that actually distinguish big data from small are "velocity (both frequency of generation, and frequency of handling, recording, and publishing) and exhaustivity" — and exhaustivity, the claim that the data covers "entire populations", is the claim Meng dismantles. boyd and Crawford's 2012 provocations (author draft — wording may differ from the published version) include the one that matters most here: "Big data and whole data are also not the same. Without taking into account the sample of a dataset, the size of the dataset is meaningless." And: "Twitter does not represent 'all people', and it is an error to assume 'people' and 'Twitter users' are synonymous: they are a very particular sub-set." The sentence holds with any platform's name in it.

### Administrative data

Administrative data — in the words of Administrative Data Research UK, "information created when people use public services, such as schools, the NHS, the courts or the benefits system" — sits between survey and trace. It is often near-complete for the population that touches the service and, as ADR UK says, "most of this data was not originally collected for research purposes". The ONS's 2023 working paper on errors in administrative and alternative sources gives the official taxonomy of what goes wrong: *measurement errors* (validity, reliability, missing data at unit and item level); *representation errors* (under- and over-coverage; selection); *processing errors* (data entry, coding and editing, model and imputation, unit errors, linkage, identification). "Unit errors" and "coverage" are this chapter's sections 3 and 4 by other names. Connelly and colleagues' 2016 review puts the researcher's duty plainly: administrative data "is not originally collected for the purpose of research"; "Researchers generally have no input into the design, structure and content"; it is "not documented in order to support data analysis"; so "researchers must put effort into uncovering the data generation processes which have governed how these data have been created", and "Understanding the processes of how and why administrative data are collected will be central to assessing the data's quality". A licensing register, a business-rates list, a grants database: each is an administrative source whose grain, coverage and purpose were set by someone else's operation, and the provenance line a dashboard owes for each is the answer to "why did this organisation record this, and who is not in it".

## 10 · Outliers — a signal before it is noise

### What an outlier is

The standard definition is Douglas Hawkins's, from 1980, reached here as quoted in Charu Aggarwal's textbook and in Osborne and Overbay (the book itself is not open): an outlier is "an observation which deviates so much from the other observations as to arouse suspicions that it was generated by a different mechanism." The operative word is *mechanism*. The definition does not say an outlier is wrong; it says it is suspicious, and what it is suspected of is having come from somewhere else. That is a reason to find out where, not a reason to delete.

The other canonical definitions agree in form. Grubbs (1969), as quoted in Hodge and Austin's survey: "An outlying observation, or outlier, is one that appears to deviate markedly from other members of the sample in which it occurs." Barnett and Lewis, likewise quoted: "An observation (or subset of observations) which appears to be inconsistent with the remainder of that set of data." The US standards handbook: "An outlier is an observation that appears to deviate markedly from other observations in the sample", with the two sentences that follow: "Outliers may be due to random variation or may indicate something scientifically interesting." "An outlier may indicate bad data." The data-mining survey by Chandola, Banerjee and Kumar reframes the whole subject from the point of view of someone who wants the anomalies: "Anomalies are patterns in data that do not conform to a well defined notion of normal behavior", and their importance "is due to the fact that anomalies in data translate to significant, and often critical, actionable information" — the hacked computer, the tumour, the stolen card, the failing sensor. They distinguish *noise*, "a phenomenon in data that is not of interest to the analyst, but acts as a hindrance", from anomalies, which are of interest; the same datum can be either, depending on who is looking. And they distinguish three kinds: a *point anomaly*, unusual against everything; a *contextual anomaly*, "anomalous in a specific context, but not otherwise" — a temperature normal in July and anomalous in January; and a *collective anomaly*, a set of related points that is unusual together. For a display of places over time, the contextual kind is the one that matters, and it cannot be found by any rule that ignores the context.

### What the tests assume

The common tests carry assumptions that decide their verdicts. Grubbs' test, per the standards handbook, "is used to detect a single outlier in a univariate data set that follows an approximately normal distribution" — one outlier, one variable, a bell curve — and "If the normality assumption for the data being tested is not valid, then a determination that there is an outlier may in fact be due to the non-normality of the data rather than the prescence [sic] of an outlier." Cultural data has the skewed shape of section 8 rather than a bell curve, so a normal-theory test applied to it will find outliers that are merely the tail.

Tukey's fences — the "1.5 times the interquartile range" rule behind every box plot — were a labelling rule for attention. Tukey's own terms, as quoted in a 2025 paper, were "outside" for points between the inner and outer fences and "far out" for points beyond; the handbook's are "mild" and "extreme". The rule's false-flag rate is a property of the rule: Dawson (2011) shows that "at least 30% of samples from a normally-distributed population of any size will be flagged as containing an outlier, while for small samples (N<10) even extreme outliers indicate little", and "for the normal distribution, the incidence of samples with flagged outliers never drops below about 30%, and above sample size 13 starts to rise." An automated alert on box-plot fences would fire on a third of perfectly ordinary datasets. The handbook's advice on what to do with a flagged point is the right one: "Outliers should be investigated carefully. Often they contain valuable information about the process under investigation or the data gathering and recording process. Before considering the possible elimination of these points from the data, one should try to understand why they appeared and whether it is likely similar values will continue to appear."

The rule most used in published research — flag anything more than three standard deviations from the mean — is self-defeating, and Leys and colleagues (2013) say why in three points: "Firstly, it assumes that the distribution is normal (outliers included). Secondly, the mean and standard deviation are strongly impacted by outliers. Thirdly … this method is very unlikely to detect outliers in small samples." The outlier inflates the yardstick used to catch it. Their survey of 127 published articles found most used mean-and-standard-deviation and none the robust alternative they recommend, the median absolute deviation, with the constant 1.4826 "linked to the assumption of normality" and thresholds of 3, 2.5 or 2 after Miller. Their reporting rule is the one this chapter adopts: "The threshold should be justified and the justification should clearly state that other concerns than cherry-picking degrees of freedom guided the selection." Frank Hampel's overview of robust statistics supplies the reason to prefer rank-based summaries throughout: the proportion of a dataset that can be corrupted before a statistic breaks is "for the median … 1/2" and "for the arithmetic mean … 0", and in "scientific routine data, not taken with utmost care", the fraction of gross errors "is typically between 1 percent and 10 percent".

### Artefact or finding

The practical triage is Osborne and Overbay's (2004), whose seven causes of outliers run from process to world. Their headings: "Outliers from data errors"; "Outliers from intentional or motivated mis-reporting"; "Outliers from sampling error"; "Outliers from standardization failure"; "Outliers from faulty distributional assumptions"; "Outliers as legitimate cases sampled from the correct population"; and "Outliers as potential focus of inquiry". The first six are artefacts of how the data was made; the seventh is a finding. Their rule for the artefacts: "If outliers of this nature cannot be corrected they should be eliminated as they do not represent valid population data points." Their rule for the rest: "Before discarding outliers, researchers need to consider whether those data contain valuable information that may not necessarily relate to the intended study, but has importance in a more global sense." And, quoting Barnett and Lewis: "Not all outliers are illegitimate contaminants, and not all illegitimate scores show up as outliers". The sequence is fixed: first ask which of the six artefacts it could be, with the provenance of section 5 as the checklist; only if none fits is it a finding; and only if one fits is it removed, with the removal declared.

### What the field actually does

Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn's 2011 paper on false positives lists the ordinary flexibilities an analyst has — "Should more data be collected? Should some observations be excluded? Which conditions should be combined and which ones compared?" — and finds that "it is common (and accepted practice) for researchers to explore various analytic alternatives, to search for a combination that yields 'statistical significance,' and to then report only what 'worked.'" Combining four such flexibilities produced a false-positive rate of about 61% at the conventional threshold. Their fifth requirement for authors: "If observations are eliminated, authors must also report what the statistical results are if those observations are included." Bakker and Wicherts (2014) measured how often removal goes unreported, in 2,667 results from 153 psychology articles. The finding is indirect and they say so: "we did find a discrepancy between the reported degrees of freedom of t tests and the reported sample size in 41% of articles that did not report removal of any data values." Two in five papers that claimed no exclusions had numbers that did not add up. They recommend "an outlier handling protocol" set before analysis and note that inconsistent thresholds permit "self-serving justifications". For a dashboard the translation is direct: the rule and threshold are written down before the data arrives, and any figure shown without a point is shown with it too, one tap away.

### The good-end outlier

The small place doing unexpectedly well is the outlier the brief most cares about, and it has a method. *Positive deviance*, defined in a 2004 *BMJ* paper by Marsh and colleagues (read in part — the full text was not reachable), is "an uncommon practice that confers advantage to the people who practise it compared with the rest of the community"; the approach is "Identifying individuals with better outcome than their peers (positive deviance) and enabling communities to adopt the behaviours that explain the improved outcome". Its origin is child nutrition in Vietnam, where the families of well-nourished poor children were found to be adding greens and tiny shrimps and crabs from the paddies to meals and feeding more often; the movement's own case study reports malnutrition falling 85% in the pilot, a figure that is the movement's and is marked as such.

Bradley and colleagues' 2009 version for healthcare organisations states the logic in four steps that are the right logic for a place-based display: "Identify 'positive deviants,' i.e., organizations that consistently demonstrate exceptionally high performance in the area of interest"; "Study the organizations in-depth using qualitative methods to generate hypotheses about practices that allow organizations to achieve top performance"; "Test hypotheses statistically in larger, representative samples"; "Work in partnership with key stakeholders … to disseminate the evidence". The second step is the one to hold on to: the outlier at the good end is a hypothesis about mechanism, generated by looking closely, and tested afterwards. A dashboard cannot do the second step. It can do the first properly and point at the second.

The method has the same disease as outlier removal, and the systematic review says so. Baxter and colleagues (2016), reviewing positive-deviance studies in healthcare: "many articles provided limited or no detail about what criteria constituted positive deviance"; "the criteria used often appeared arbitrary and cut-off points were not justified"; "comparison groups were rarely included"; and the rule that follows: "The methods and criteria used to identify positive deviants must always be stated regardless of whether they are exceptionally performing individuals, teams or organisations." The good-end and bad-end outlier need the same declaration — the rule, the threshold, the comparison — and the good-end one needs, in addition, the small-denominator caution of section 4, because the smallest units are over-represented at the top of any rate ranking by arithmetic before any mechanism is involved. A display that celebrates its best small performer has, until it has shown the funnel, not yet shown that there is anything to celebrate.

## 11 · What holds true regardless

Chapter 3 found it useful to separate a short list of canonical rules from the record of findings beneath them, and this chapter does the same. The seven are the rules a reader should be able to recite; the record is what they rest on.

### The seven

**1 · A number is undefined until its unit, its period and its base are stated.** What one row is (person, venue, night, ticket), what span of time it covers (and which instant or window stands for a day), and what it is counted against. Each is a choice made before analysis; each changes the count; the same market can look concentrated at one grain and diversifying at another. *Standard* (Eurostat units; Regulation 696/93; the reference period) and *Evidence* (Wickham; the midnight census; Datta et al.†). **Fixed (requirement).**

**2 · Nothing is never one thing.** A blank must say which kind it is. Zero is a value, used "only when a data point is a true zero", and never as a stand-in for missing; "NA" is banned because it is ambiguous; a blank is tolerable only when there is exactly one reason for it and the reason is stated; otherwise every nothing is labelled. The official vocabularies agree with the studio's six and split "not collected" into *not applicable* and *not asked*. *Standard* (Analysis Function; Eurostat; SDMX; Codd). **Fixed (requirement).**

**3 · Every figure carries its provenance, and the record outlives the number.** Who is responsible; what source and why; what method; what period, as a closed interval; when issued and when last changed; what status; what quality, with error and confidence where they exist; what was withheld or altered. A revised number is a number with a history, and the history belongs where the number is. *Standard* (Code of Practice 3.0; Regulation 223/2009; PROV; DCAT; FAIR). **Fixed (requirement).**

**4 · A comparison declares what it holds equal.** It is valid only "to the extent that differences between statistics can be attributed to differences between the true values"; so definition, period, geography and vintage, method, disclosure treatment, breaks, revisions, the comparator group and its method, precision, and outlier handling are each the same or each stated. The comparator must not contain the outcome. *Standard* (SIMS; European Code; Code 3.0; ESMA; geography policy) and *Practice* (PHE; DfE). **Fixed (requirement).**

**5 · A total speaks for the biggest.** In a skewed distribution a sum or mean is dominated by its largest terms; the median and the share are not. Beside any total, show the shape — a median, a top-share, a Lorenz curve or a concentration index — and treat "80/20" as a question to answer, not a law to assume. The long tail of a cultural market is mostly ones and zeros, and showing people what others chose makes it more concentrated and less predictable. *Evidence* (Lorenz; NIST; ONS; Rosen; Salganik, Dodds & Watts; Elberse — the last secondary). **Fixed** (requirement) for the first sentence; the choice of instrument is Adaptable.

**6 · Size does not cure selection.** A transactional or digital-trace feed is worth, as evidence, its quality times its quantity times the difficulty of the question, and in the one measured case a defect correlation of half of one per cent reduced millions of records to the worth of a few hundred random ones. Report such feeds as within-sample comparisons unless the selection has been measured; read every feed through the ten characteristics; never claim what a head-only feed cannot see. *Evidence* (Meng; Lazer et al. — secondary; Salganik 2018; boyd & Crawford). **Fixed (statement of the record).**

**7 · An outlier is a question before it is a decision.** The standard definition is a point suspected of a different mechanism; the standard rules for flagging one fire on ordinary data and assume a shape cultural data does not have; the field removes outliers silently and in its own favour. So: investigate against the six artefact causes first; write the rule and threshold down in advance; show the figure with and without; and treat the good-end outlier as a hypothesis needing the same declaration plus the small-denominator caution. *Evidence* (Hawkins via Aggarwal; NIST; Dawson; Leys; Simmons; Bakker & Wicherts; Gelman & Price; Baxter). **Fixed (sequence).**

### The record beneath them

Findings that support the seven and are worth knowing on their own; rules that follow from them are in the table, not repeated here.

- "Reliable", in official usage, means how little a number moves on revision, not how accurate it is; a UK quarterly growth figure moves about half a percentage point over three years and the regulator calls that "normal and unavoidable". (ONS; OSR.)
- The regulator's test for a statistic is whether it can "bear the weight of the decisions made using" it, and readers want quality information that is "prominent, succinct". (OSR 2023.)
- Verification asks whether the analysis "has been conducted correctly"; validation asks "whether the correct analysis has been performed"; the paradigm failure was a model re-used for a decision it was never assured for. (AQuA Book 2015/2025; NAO.)
- Quality, to the people who use data, includes believability and reputation as *intrinsic* dimensions: a correct number from a disbelieved source is low-quality data. (Wang & Strong 1996.)
- A number has three dates — the period it describes, when it was issued, when it last changed — and a dashboard's single stamp usually gives the least informative. (DCAT 3; Eurostat; ALFRED.)
- The reason a feed's data is missing matters more than how much is missing, and the reason cannot be learned from the data; the remedy is to show what would change if the nothing were something. (Rubin†; Sterne; NRC 2010.)
- Complete-case analysis — drop the incomplete rows and say nothing — was the primary method in 45% of trials in the four top medical journals in 2013. (Bell et al. 2014.)
- The database standard has "effectively only one null value"; aggregates skip nulls silently; `coalesce(sum(x), 0)` turns "no rows" into a displayed 0; the relational model's inventor called zero "far too valuable" to mean missing. (SQL-92; PostgreSQL; Codd 1986.)
- The one official rule found for drawing a gap: do not join the points either side, "even if the line is dotted or dashed". (Analysis Function 2022.)
- Free is a price of zero on a real quantity, not a nothing; no official scheme has a code for it. (ONS CPI methodology.)
- A lagged series ends in a not-yet dressed as a low number: 65% complete at two weeks, 95% at eight, in one case. (CDC NCHS.)
- The 2021 Census perturbed every cell rather than suppressing some, so a published "1" is not literal and totals need not add. (ONS.)
- Finance's rule for a defined measure — define, reconcile, show comparators, keep the definition, restate if changed — is the statistical rule arrived at independently. (ESMA 2015; Tesco 2026.)
- The smallest units sit at both ends of a raw-rate ranking; adjusting for it hands the top to the largest; the funnel plot is the display that is biased toward neither. (Gelman & Price; Spiegelhalter†.)
- A hospital bed is "occupied" if someone is in it at midnight; the midnight count sits below the late-morning peak; the official night time window runs 6pm to 6am and straddles the calendar day. (KH03; Pierce et al. — secondary; ONS.)
- Months are unequal containers and official series are adjusted for the number of Fridays and the date of Easter. (Eurostat.)
- Big data's distinguishing traits are velocity and exhaustivity, and exhaustivity — "entire populations" — is the claim that selection bias defeats. (Kitchin & McArdle.)
- In one dataset, one automated pager produced an entire apparent trend in public anger; one unit can be an aggregate. (Salganik 2018, citing Back et al.)
- The "Pareto principle" was misnamed by the man who named it, and its curves belong to Lorenz. (Juran 1975 — secondary.)
- Positive deviance is a method for the good-end outlier, and its systematic review found its cut-offs "often appeared arbitrary". (Bradley et al.; Baxter et al.)
- Grubbs' test assumes one outlier and a normal distribution; Tukey's fences flag a third of ordinary samples; mean-and-SD rules let the outlier inflate the yardstick; the median breaks down at half the data, the mean at none. (NIST; Dawson; Leys; Hampel.)

## 12 · Where the choice is yours

The seven are Fixed. Within them, these are open, and the chapter names the trade-off without making the call.

**Which grain to display.** Venue, operator, room, event, night, person: each is a legitimate unit, each answers a different reader's question, and the chapter's only rule is that the one shown is named and that the row count under it is stated. The choice between them is the studio's and the readers' of Chapter 2.

**Which instant or window stands for a day.** The midnight census is a rule, not a law. A display may choose any boundary that suits its subject, provided it says so and never changes it silently.

**Which denominator to lead with.** Per place, per resident, per venue-night, per ticket, per pound: the reference class is a choice. The rule is that the chosen base is visible and the alternatives reachable, and that a small base carries its interval.

**Which concentration instrument.** A median beside the mean is the minimum. A top-share is the plainest. A Lorenz curve shows the whole bow and asks the most of the reader. The Gini and the HHI compress it to a number and lose the shape. Which to show depends on the reader and the question; showing none is not an option under truth 5.

**Whether "not applicable" is a seventh kind.** Every official scheme splits "not collected" into *could not exist* and *nobody asked*. The studio's six do not. Whether to add a kind, split the fifth, or treat it as a sub-kind is a decision about the display's vocabulary, and it has consequences for how many states an empty cell can be in.

**How to show a nothing.** The standards fix that it is labelled and that a line is not joined across it. They do not fix whether the label is a code, a word, a pattern or a sentence; whether a gap is a blank, a hatched region or an annotation; or how a suppressed cell is distinguished from an unavailable one at a glance. That is a design decision governed by Chapter 3's rules on colour and words, and the design system's.

**How much provenance on the face.** The minimal set is ten lines. Chapter 3 established that what is crucial cannot live only behind a click; it did not establish that all ten lines are crucial at every moment. Which lines are on the face (period, status, and the fact that caveats exist, at minimum) and which are one tap away is a layout decision for Chapter 11, bounded by the Code's "prominently" and the regulator's "succinct".

**Suppress or perturb.** Two honest ways to protect small cells, each a different kind of nothing in a number's clothing. The ONS chose perturbation for the census; most tables choose suppression; the Decision Book chooses suppression with secondary suppression. The choice depends on what the reader is to do with small counts, and it should be made once and declared.

**Which outlier rule, and which threshold.** The median absolute deviation is the literature's recommended default and 2.5 a common threshold; other rules are defensible for other shapes. What is fixed is that the rule is written down before the data arrives, the threshold is justified, and the figure is shown with and without.

**Which neighbours.** CIPFA's, the ONS classification, a bespoke model on published variables, or none: the choice is open and must be shown, and the variables must not include the thing being compared.

## 13 · What this means for a nightlife dashboard

An illustration, not a specification. The sector knowledge is the studio's; the method is the chapter's. Chapter 1's definition set the case: a public display of the people, activity, value and support systems that sustain a place's nightlife, for named readers who are not in general.

Begin with the row. Before any chart, the display has decided whether a row is a venue, an operator, a room or an event, and the chapter's first truth says the decision is stated where the number is. A count of "venues" under 1,000 capacity is a count of local units, or of enterprises, or of rooms; the European regulation gives eight candidates, and the one chosen changes the 78%. Then the night. An event that starts at ten and ends at three belongs to one date by one rule and to two by another; the official night time window runs from six in the evening to six in the morning and does not match the calendar; the display's rule — and it may be any rule — is written down and never changed without a [b]. Then the base. Events per what: per resident, per venue, per venue-night; the smallest places will top and bottom any per-capita ranking by arithmetic, so the small place's rate carries its interval and sits in a funnel, not a league table.

The feed. A ticketing platform is a recording mechanism with a defect correlation: it sees ticketed events, on that platform, in the proportion that its users are the city's. Read through the ten characteristics it is big, always-on, nonreactive, incomplete (no demographics, nothing off-platform, nothing on the construct "vibe"), inaccessible beyond what the platform grants, nonrepresentative, drifting as the platform's user base and features change, algorithmically confounded by whatever the platform promotes, dirty, and sensitive. That is not an argument against using it. It is the list of lines the display owes the reader before it shows a number from it, and the reason its findings are reported as "on this platform, this year against last" rather than as the city. The free event is on the feed as a zero in the price column and a count in the quantity column, and a revenue share cannot see it; a display that leads with revenue has chosen, by its denominator, not to see the free event, and should say so.

The nothings. A venue with no row this month is one of six things — it had no events, its events were free, its data did not arrive, it did not answer, nobody asked it, or its figure was withheld because it was one of two — and the display, following the UK rule, labels each, because there is more than one reason a cell can be blank. The line of that venue's history is not joined across the month. The provisional figure wears its [p] until it is final. The month that was perturbed or imputed says who did it. And the small cell that was suppressed is marked, not blank, so that the reader can see that a scene exists there even though its number cannot be shown — which is Chapter 1's position carried through: a display built on official sources is blind exactly where a small scene is, and must say so rather than show a blank.

The comparison. Two places on the display are comparable to the extent that the difference is in the places, so each comparison carries the ten-line declaration, with the comparator group named and its method stated — and if the comparator was built from variables that include the measure being compared, it is not a comparator. The shape, then. Beside any total — revenue, attendance, events — the median venue, the top-tenth share, and the Lorenz curve of the thing by the unit, so that a reader can see whether the bow is bent at the top one per cent or the top fifth, and so that the 78%/32% is a point on a curve rather than a slogan.

And then the outlier, which is the brief's hope. The small venue whose numbers are far above its neighbours' is, in order: possibly a data error, a misreport, a sampling fluke, a definitional slip, a tail of a skewed distribution, a legitimate extreme — or the thing the display was built to find. The display cannot tell which; it can show the value with and without, state the rule that flagged it and the threshold, place it in the funnel so that its small base is visible, and point at the question — what is happening there? — that only someone standing in the room can answer. That is where this chapter hands over to Chapters 6 and 9: finding value in the data, and measuring a vibe, both begin with the outlier the chapter has refused to trim.

## What proves a break works

The Fixed rules above are mostly standards, and breaking a standard is different from breaking an experimental finding: the test is not whether a reader misreads but whether the figure can be *checked*. If you must show a number without its unit, period or base; a blank without its kind; a line joined across a gap; a comparison without its declaration; a total without its shape; a platform figure as the city; or a trimmed series without the untrimmed one — the test is: **hand the display to someone who was not in the room, with the decision it is meant to bear, and ask them to reconstruct how the number was made and what would change it.** If they can, from what is on the face and one tap away, the break has cost nothing. If they cannot, the number cannot bear the weight, and the standard was right. Log the break in the deviation entry Chapter 16 describes.

## What this chapter could not settle

- The Decision Book's gap rule ("dashed, hatched, or broken") can be read as permitting a dashed join across a missing point, which the standard it cites rejects; the book's rule here is "never join, dotted or dashed".
- The Decision Book's provenance set omits the responsible party and the issued/modified dates; both are added from the Code, PROV and DCAT, and the book's "illustrative flag" kept.
- The studio's six kinds of nothing merge *not applicable* with *not asked*, which every official scheme separates, and have no place for "included elsewhere" or "not yet available"; the mapping table is the evidence and the seventh-kind decision is flagged rather than made.
- Neither the Decision Book nor the design system has a rule on the unit of observation or the row count; the rule is made from Wickham, Eurostat and the regulation.
- No rule in either on the denominator, the reference class, or the funnel for small bases; made from the CDC primer, Hájek and Gelman & Price.
- No rule in either on reading a platform feed (coverage, selection, the ten characteristics); made from Meng and Salganik.
- No rule in either on outliers beyond "show the gaps"; the procedure is made from Leys, Simmons, Bakker & Wicherts, Osborne & Overbay.
- No rule in either on concentration measures beside totals; made from Lorenz, NIST and the ONS, and the 78%/32% currently stands without its curve.
- "Reliable" is used in the failure register and the design system in the ordinary sense; official usage means revisions. Both senses are stated; the rule is to use one.
- No evidence was found that provenance on the face changes a reader's decision; the chapter's thesis rests beside a gap. Section 5 says so, the provenance rules are marked Standard (adopted), not Evidence, and a probe is flagged.

## Checklist

- [ ] Every figure states what one row is and how many rows
- [ ] Every figure states its reference period as an interval, and separately when it was issued and last changed
- [ ] The rule for assigning boundary-crossing activity to a period is written down; changes carry [b]
- [ ] Dates are ISO 8601 with offset in data; natural in display
- [ ] Every rate names its denominator; alternatives reachable; time-in-observation used where spans differ
- [ ] Small-base rates carry intervals and sit in a funnel, not a rank
- [ ] The provenance set travels with every figure, and the responsible party is named
- [ ] Previous values and descriptions are kept when a figure changes; revisions visible in one place
- [ ] "Reliable" used only in the official sense; [p] and [r] shown
- [ ] Zero only for true zero; no "NA"; no bare symbols
- [ ] Every empty cell labelled with its kind; blanks only under the one-reason rule
- [ ] Not-applicable distinguished from not-asked (or the decision not to logged)
- [ ] Free shown as zero price with a quantity, never as a nothing
- [ ] No line joined across a gap; every gap highlighted and explained
- [ ] Imputed and estimated values flagged with method and author; imputation rates published
- [ ] Suppressed cells marked, never blank; secondary suppression applied; suppression or perturbation declared
- [ ] Where missingness may correlate with the measure, a sensitivity view exists
- [ ] Every comparison carries the ten-line declaration
- [ ] Definition and method changes marked [b], with a back series where possible
- [ ] Area codes with vintage; best-fit lookups declared
- [ ] Defined measures defined, reconciled, held constant, restated if changed
- [ ] Comparator groups named with method; no comparator contains the outcome
- [ ] Median and a concentration measure beside every total or mean of a skewed quantity
- [ ] No 80/20 assumed; the bow measured
- [ ] Popularity rankings declared as such, with the distribution
- [ ] Platform feeds reported within-sample unless selection is measured; coverage stated
- [ ] Every feed audited against the ten characteristics; audit published
- [ ] No claim about the tail from a head-only feed
- [ ] Administrative sources state why recorded and who is absent
- [ ] Outlier rule and threshold written before data arrives and justified (median absolute deviation as the default)
- [ ] Flagged points investigated against the six artefact causes before removal; removals declared
- [ ] With-and-without views for any removed point
- [ ] No fence-based or normal-theory alerts on skewed data without a stated false-flag rate
- [ ] Good-end outliers carry criteria, threshold, comparison group and base
