## Before you start

This is the sixteenth and closing teaching chapter of a research book written for Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio led by Carly Heath, about how dashboards work and why. The book exists to support the Vibe Map — a planned public, non-commercial, interactive account of the people, activity, value and support systems that sustain a place's nightlife culture, built first for the West of England. This chapter is different from the fifteen before it in one way: most of what it reads is the book itself. It assembles what the earlier chapters left — their rules, their records, their tests, their open questions and their quarrels with the house's design documents — and asks how a rule in this book is broken well. You do not need to have read the earlier chapters to follow the argument, because the chapter quotes what it needs and counts what it assembles; you will need them to use the register it leaves.

The chapter's conventions are the book's. Every finding is marked by **kind** — *Standard* (a published rule from a standards body, a regulator, a design system or a method text, with the sub-kind named: Standard (regulator), Standard (law), Standard (method), Standard (design system), Standard (official statistics)), *Evidence* (a measured result, with what was measured and on whom), *Evidence of method* (a published procedure whose results are reported — a sub-kind the chapters added to Chapter 0's five), *Reading* (this book's interpretation of evidence or standards), *Practice* (what a named body or project does, read for method), or *Nightlife Culture* (the house's own position) — and by **tier**: *Fixed* (holds in any dashboard), *Adaptable* (holds unless a stated reason overrides it) or *Decorative* (open). This chapter is where the tiers themselves are examined, so section 3 says what each has meant across the book and what it will mean in the register. A dagger (†) marks a figure or quotation taken from an abstract or a secondary account; "secondary" marks a source read only through another; "(read once)" marks a quotation returned once by the fetch tool without a presence check; "two passes" marks one returned identically twice without a presence check, and "three passes" three times. A presence check, or presence re-fetch, opens the page a second time and asks only whether the exact string is on it, with a false string beside it to show the tool will say no. Findings in the body carry their kind in italics at the end of a paragraph; tiers are given in the rules table. Flags in square brackets — `[Further research]`, `[Prototype flag]`, `[Validate with users]`, `[Revisit → Ch n]`, `[Revisit at end]`, `[Rich reference]`, `[Counterbalance → Ch n]` — are defined in Chapter 0; this chapter is where `[Revisit at end]` is answered.

## The question

When a rule in this book is to be broken, how is it done well — which rules have a right answer and which a defensible range, and does the book's own register keep the two apart; what separates an earned deviation from an expedient one, in this book and in the regimes other fields have built for letting a rule be broken on the record; what record is kept, with which lines, written when and read by whom, and are the book's three logs one; what test is run first, and of what form; what does the book's own assembly show about its rules, its records, its tests, its gaps and its open quarrels with the house's design documents; and what does the build inherit?

## The 60-second version

**The tier test holds, its borrowed sentences are real, and the book has not been applying it to itself.** The Decision Book's split — Fixed is "Uncertainty implies that there is something to be certain about", Adaptable is ambiguity, "With ambiguity there's no singular, correct answer" — is verified at source, as is "fear enlists its good friends reason and comfort"; the third borrowed line is a question at source, "How do you close the gap between I wonder and I know?", and the house's year for the second book is wrong (2022). Other fields draw the same three lines: the IETF's MUST, SHOULD and MAY, with the warning that imperatives "must be used with care and sparingly"; the road-design manual's "must" (cannot be varied), "shall" (varied only by approved departure), "should" (varied with recorded justification) and "may"; the C coding standard's mandatory, required (violable only with a formal deviation) and advisory. Against those, this book's own tables carry 486 rules of which 433 (89 per cent) say Fixed, 212 of them with no sense given — against 74 per cent Fixed in the Decision Book's register, and two chapters with no Adaptable rule at all. Chapter 0 named two senses of Fixed ("a statement of the record"; "sequence") that the tables use twice and never; the tables use two others ("as a stance"; "as a procedure") that Chapter 0 does not define. Eleven rules carry different tiers in different chapters. Sections 7 and 8 fix this — the reconciliations in 7, the register's form in 8 — and section 3 says why the count matters: a rule set in which nearly everything is Fixed is the condition under which, elsewhere, rules stop being read.

**Where rules may be broken on the record, the record is mostly bad.** The corporate-governance regime that invented "comply or explain" asked for context, rationale, mitigation and a time limit in 2012, said then that "a large majority" explained fully, and in 2021 scored its own regime: "Our sample identified 74 cases of non-compliance with the Code, but we found only four explanations that we considered high quality and offered an insight into the companies' approach to good governance." The accessibility regulations let a public body exempt itself after a mandatory assessment; the monitoring body's first-listed common issue is that "organisations claim disproportionate burden without having carried out an assessment beforehand", and "many accessibility statements are now out-of-date". Mandatory reason fields fill with "a space or random characters" (Wright et al., citing prior studies). Preregistered studies deviated in 25 of 27 cases and one disclosed everything. Most architecture decision records — 63 per cent of 5,800 — were "opened directly with a status of "accepted"", written after the fact. What the regimes agree on is the shape of a good entry — a reason with a shape, a reader other than the author, a time limit or review date, and publication by default — and what the evidence adds is when a written reason helps at all: before the decision, for a reader whose views are unknown (Tetlock, Skitka & Boettger); after commitment it becomes "defensive bolstering", and in two experiments requiring a justification changed neither accuracy nor strategy and only lowered confidence.

**A rule that fires too often is overridden by reflex.** Drug-safety alerts "are overridden by clinicians in 49% to 96% of cases"; a pooled rate for interaction alerts is "90% (CI95% 85–95%)"; and the dose–response is measured: "Likelihood of reminder acceptance dropped by 30% for each additional reminder received per encounter" (112 clinicians; 1.27 million advisories). About half of overrides were appropriate — "from 12% for renal recommendations to 92% for patient allergies" — so a high override rate indicts the rule as often as the person. Tiering by consequence works: where only the severest alerts interrupted, compliance was "29% vs. 10%" and "100% of the most severe alerts were accepted". Checklists cut deaths from 1.5 to 0.8 per cent where they were used and did nothing where they were mandated and self-reported at 98 per cent compliance; observed, "two-thirds of the items were checked" and teams "failed to pause or focus on the checks in more than 70% of cases". The lesson for a book with 433 Fixed rules is arithmetic: the Fixed register is short and its breaches stop the build; the long list is Adaptable and its departures are logged.

**The book's machinery, assembled.** Twelve records with 129 lines, four printed as lists and eight as tables, named six different ways; the test the chapters say they inherit — the removal test — leads in two chapters, while 27 of the 55 named tests are reader tests; 42 groups of rules stated twice in different words; 207 gaps, of which 48 are things the field has not studied and 38 are gaps in the house's own systems; 37 open items in the conflict logs, five of them direct conflicts the house has not resolved; seven chapters asking the Decision Book for a chapter it does not have; and Chapter 0, last revised after Chapter 2, with eleven conventions the later chapters quietly changed. The chapter resolves what it can (the eleven tier conflicts; the rules that pull against each other; the three logs, which become one log with a type; the twelve records, of which ten become one document written once per concept with two views, after Eurostat's "each concept is only reported upon once", and two join the log), re-states what it cannot as decisions for Carly, and writes the fixes Chapter 0 needs.

**What the build inherits.** The rule register, with every rule's tier — Fixed in one of three senses — its source, its chapter and the test that proves a break; a Fixed list short enough to be read, on an estimate of about two hundred that the build's first year tests; one deviation log with the nine lines the regimes and the evidence support — rule and tier, trigger, alternative, what it buys and costs, the test and its result, who decided and who checked, date, review date, status — written before the departure ships and reviewed at a date; the three build-time checks version 1 specified (crop; recipe; state-honesty), re-based on version 2's rules and extended by one (the removal check); and the record of the dashboard, one document, with ten sections — the twelve records less the two that are logs — and its maintenance and retirement built in. The pass test for the chapter is the Decision Book's own: take any decision being argued about, name its tier from the register, and if the tier cannot be found the register has failed, not the argument.

## 2 · What people believe about breaking rules, and what the evidence says

**Belief: a rule is safer if it is Fixed.** Where rules fire often and cannot be told apart, people stop reading them: drug-safety alerts are overridden in half to nineteen-twentieths of cases, and acceptance falls by a measured step with each extra rule fired in the same encounter. Where the severe rules are set apart from the rest, the severe ones are obeyed. A rule set in which nearly everything is Fixed is less safe than a short Fixed list, because the short list is the one that is read. Section 6. *(Evidence.)*

**Belief: requiring a written reason makes the decision better.** The evidence is narrower. Having to justify helps when the justification is owed before the decision, to a reader whose views are unknown — "preemptive self-criticism" — and hurts after commitment, when effort goes to "defensive bolstering"; in two experiments a justification requirement "neither changed how accurately people made a judgment, nor the judgment strategies" and "only decreased confidence"; mandatory reason fields become noise. A process record helps the next reader more than the writer: process accountability "promoted more effective knowledge sharing, improving accuracy among observers" (†). Section 5. *(Evidence.)*

**Belief: "comply or explain" produces explanations.** It produced, in the regulator's own 2021 sample, four good explanations in seventy-four departures, and "tick-box compliance" with "boilerplate language"; a quarter of UK deviations in a 2006 sample "were not properly justified"; "no provision in the UK for any statements by companies to be assessed by any regulatory body" (†). The regime works to the extent that someone reads the explanation, and its designers said so in 2012: "it is up to them to decide whether to accept or reject explanations." Section 4. *(Evidence; Standard (regulator).)*

**Belief: a deviation record protects the project.** It does when it is written before the departure and read afterwards. Where it is written after — 63 per cent of 5,800 architecture decision records were born "accepted" — it records a conclusion, not a decision; where nobody re-reads it, it decays — "36 percent of these waivers have not been reviewed in 10 years" (the Columbia board, secondary); "many accessibility statements are now out-of-date". Section 5. *(Evidence; Practice.)*

**Belief: a checklist makes the work safe.** A checklist made surgery safer where it was used as a team practice (deaths 1.5 to 0.8 per cent, eight hospitals) and not where it was mandated and self-reported (101 hospitals, no significant change); observed directly, a third of items went unchecked. "it is not the act of ticking off a checklist that reduces complications, but performance of the actions it calls for" (secondary). This book has fifteen checklists; section 6 says what that means. *(Evidence.)*

**Belief: this book's rules are mostly Fixed because dashboards are mostly a matter of right answers.** The Decision Book, which the book says it inherits, is 74 per cent Fixed; this book is 89 per cent, and 49 per cent of its Fixed rules give no sense of the word. Section 3 reads the difference as the book's and not the subject's. *(Reading.)*

**Belief: the book's records are twelve documents.** They are twelve sections of one document that has not yet been written as one, and the fields have precedents for both answers. Section 7. *(Reading.)*

**Belief: the removal test is the book's test.** It is the Decision Book's, and Chapter 14's; it leads in two of fifteen chapters. The book's commonest test is to put the display in front of the reader the rule protects and ask. Section 7. *(Reading.)*

## 3 · The tier test, tested

### What the house wrote, and where it came from

The Decision Book's tier test is a table and two sentences. The table: Fixed is "Uncertainty — there is a right answer", its test "Pass or fail, measurable", breaking it "Negligence"; Adaptable is "Ambiguity — no single correct answer", its test "Defensible or not, arguable", breaking it "The job"; Decorative is "Free", with no test, breaking it "Expected". The sentences: "Treating an Adaptable decision as Fixed is deference. Treating a Fixed decision as Adaptable is negligence." Its grounds are three borrowed lines from the Stanford design school's books, and this chapter went to the source for each. *(Standard (design system).)*

The first is verified and consecutive: "Uncertainty implies that there is something to be certain about. An absolute truth or fact exists. With ambiguity there's no singular, correct answer." — from *Navigating Ambiguity* (Andrea Small, Kelly Schmutte and the Stanford d.school; Ten Speed Press, 2022), read in the publisher's educators' guide, which reproduces the activity "from" the book; the sentence before it is "While ambiguity may contain uncertainty, they're different." The second is verified, and is the tail of a longer sentence: "When it comes to acting with courage within an established system, we often refrain because fear enlists its good friends reason and comfort." — from *Drawing on Courage* (Ashish Goel and the Stanford d.school; Ten Speed Press), whose publisher's page and copyright line say 2022 where the Decision Book says 2021. The third is a question at source: "How do you close the gap between I wonder and I know?" followed by "You make a prototype." — from *This Is a Prototype* (Scott Witthoft and the Stanford d.school; Ten Speed Press, 2022); the Decision Book prints it as an instruction. Two corrections for the house, then: a year, and a question mark. *(Practice; Standard (method).)*

### The fields' own three lines

The house did not invent the three-tier shape, and each of the fields that drew it adds a condition the house's table lacks.

The internet standards' vocabulary (RFC 2119, 1997): "MUST" "mean that the definition is an absolute requirement of the specification"; "SHOULD" "mean that there may exist valid reasons in particular circumstances to ignore a particular item, but the full implications must be understood and carefully weighed before choosing a different course"; "MAY" "mean that an item is truly optional". And the instruction the house should pin above its register: "Imperatives of the type defined in this memo must be used with care and sparingly. In particular, they MUST only be used where it is actually required for interoperation or to limit behavior which has potential for causing harm". The 2017 clarification adds that the words carry their meanings "when, and only when, they appear in all capitals", and that "a lot of normative text does not use them and is still normative". A SHOULD names who decides a deviation (the implementer) and the test (implications understood and weighed), and requires no record. *(Standard (method).)*

The international standards' directives (ISO/IEC Directives Part 2, ninth edition) define a *requirement* as an expression "that conveys objectively verifiable criteria to be fulfilled and from which no deviation is permitted if conformance with the document is to be claimed", a *recommendation* as one "that conveys a suggested possible choice or course of action deemed to be particularly suitable without necessarily mentioning or excluding others", and a *permission* as one "that conveys consent or liberty (or opportunity) to do something"; and they require that "The user of the document shall be able to identify the requirements he/she is obliged to satisfy in order to claim conformance to a document." (read once). There is no reasoned-deviation tier: a requirement admits none, a recommendation needs none. The W3C has two categories: "The main content of WCAG 2.2 is normative and defines requirements that impact conformance claims." and informative material "provides advisory information to help interpret the guidelines but does not create requirements that impact a conformance claim" (verified in the editors' source; check against the dated Recommendation). *(Standard (method); Standard (W3C).)*

Two engineering rule systems built the house's shape with the record attached. The road-design manual (National Highways, GG 101): "The verb 'must' indicates a statutory or legislative requirement. Note: Requirements with this verb form cannot be varied."; "The verb 'shall' indicates a requirement of the Overseeing Organisation. Note: Requirements with this verb form can be only be varied though the use of departures or in limited situations as relaxations." (as printed); a "should" names recommendations that "can be varied without recourse to the departures process, but require justification and a safety risk assessment where the recommendation is not followed"; a "may" can be varied "in accordance with internal review processes" (read once); a departure is "Variation or waiving of a requirement carried out in accordance with the Overseeing Organisation's procedures" and must be approved "before the design is finalised"; and "Justifications to support all design decisions shall be recorded by the design organisation as part of the project record." (read once). The C coding standard (MISRA Compliance 2020): "Mandatory Guidelines — Guidelines for which violation is never permitted;" "Required Guidelines — Guidelines which can only be violated when supported by a deviation defining a set of clear restrictions, requirements and precautions;" "Advisory Guidelines — Recommendations to be followed as far as is reasonably practical."; a deviation is "A violation which has been formally accepted and approved", recorded with the guideline, the circumstances, "The reason why the deviation is required", the background and the precautions; and two reasons are never acceptable — "Simply to satisfy the convenience of the developer" and "When a reasonable alternative coding strategy would make the need for a violation unnecessary" (read once). *(Standard (method).)*

Read together, the fields give the house four things its table does not state: a top tier that cannot be varied at all (statute; a mandatory guideline), above the Fixed tier that can be varied by approved departure; a middle tier whose departures need a recorded justification but no approval; the person who approves, named as someone other than the designer; and a list of reasons that are never earned. The last is the beginning of the deviation taxonomy the Decision Book promised and never wrote (section 4). *(Reading.)*

Behind all of them is the safety literature's account of what a rule is. Hale and Borys's review contrasts "a top-down classical, rational approach in which rules are seen as static, comprehensive limits of freedom of choice, imposed on operators at the sharp end and violations are seen as negative behaviour to be suppressed" with "a bottom-up constructivist view of rules as dynamic, local, situated constructions of operators as experts, where competence is seen to a great extent as the ability to adapt rules to the diversity of reality", and their framework "places the monitoring and adaptation of rules central to its management process" and asks for "regular and explicit dialogue" with the people who follow the rules (†). The house's Fixed tier is the first model and its Adaptable tier is the second; what the review adds is that the second needs a process for keeping its rules alive, which is what the logs are for. *(Evidence of method, †.)*

The Decision Book's own register, which the book says it inherits, is 258 Fixed of 347 (74.4 per cent), 75 Adaptable (21.6 per cent) and 14 Decorative. By run of chapters: Chapters 1 to 5 have 21 Adaptable rules in 140 (15 per cent); Chapters 6 to 10 have 5 in 193 (2.6 per cent) — Chapters 7 and 10 have none; Chapters 11 to 15 have 19 in 153 (12.4 per cent). Chapter 8 noticed it of itself: "Fixed", "Fixed as a procedure" and "Fixed as a stance" are nearly equal in number, with almost nothing Adaptable. *(Reading; the compilation.)*

The tier cells use 53 distinct wordings. Of the 433 Fixed rows (a cell can carry two qualifiers, so the senses below sum past 433), 212 say exactly "**Fixed**" with no sense; 102 say "procedure" ("as a procedure" in Chapters 4 to 13, "(procedure)" in 14 and 15); 90 say "stance"; 2 say "sequence"; none says "statement of the record"; 31 carry another qualifier — "(adopted)", "(declared)", "(unit)", "as defaults", "as disclosure", "as the planning assumption". Each is reconciled in section 7. *(Reading; the compilation.)*

### What the count means

Three readings are possible and the chapter takes the third. The first is that dashboards are a subject of right answers, so the book's 89 per cent is the subject's; the Decision Book's 74 per cent, on the same kind of material, says otherwise. The second is that the chapters' "Fixed as a stance" is a third tier in disguise — the house's position, held with reasons, which the Decision Book would call Adaptable with a strong default — and that re-labelling 90 rows would bring the book to about 70 per cent, near the Decision Book; that is part of the answer. The third is that the book has been using Fixed to mean "important", which is the drift Chapter 0's gap row foresaw, and that the cost is the one section 6 measures in other fields: a rule set in which nearly everything carries the strongest word is a rule set in which the word is read as noise. The road-design manual's and the coding standard's top tiers are short; RFC 2119's own instruction is "sparingly"; the clinical evidence is that acceptance falls with every extra rule that fires. *(Reading.)*

So the register in section 8 does three things to the tiers. It gives Fixed one of three senses, each with its test: *Fixed as a requirement* — a right answer that can be checked, whose breach is negligence, which the build enforces as a check that fails (contrast; disclosure thresholds; the floor; expressiveness in Chapter 14's sense); *Fixed as a sequence* — an order of operations that is the hard line while each step's form is open (construct before proxy; recognition before release); and *Fixed as a statement of the record* — a finding the book is confident of, from which a design rule may or may not follow, and which is not a rule at all but a truth, listed separately. Everything the chapters called "Fixed as a stance" becomes *Adaptable (house default)* — the house's position, held with its grounds, which a later display may leave with a logged reason — unless the chapter's own grounds show it to be a requirement, in which case it is one. And "Fixed as a procedure" becomes either a requirement (the procedure is the check) or a sequence, by its wording. The result, estimated from the compilation and confirmed when the register is built, is a Fixed list of perhaps two hundred rules, most of them requirements the build can test, and an Adaptable list of the rest — around two hundred and fifty before the forty-two duplicate groups collapse — with their grounds. That is the shape the fields recommend and the shape the evidence says is read. *(Nightlife Culture; Reading.)*

The W3C's fourth category, which Chapter 14 found the tiers lack — the "specific sensory experience" that may not be ignored and need not carry data — is not a tier of rule but a tier of *element*, and belongs in the boundary log's "tier claimed" line, where Chapter 14 put it. The register does not add it as a fourth tier of rule; it notes, under the Decorative tier, that an expressive element is Decorative only if it can be ignored, and otherwise is a sensory experience owing identification. *(Reading.)*

## 4 · The kinds of deviation, and the regimes that let a rule be broken on the record

### A taxonomy the house named and never wrote

The Decision Book's chapter on breaking rules promises in its subtitle "the taxonomy of deviation" and delivers it as one checklist line, printed twice: "Deviation classified honestly: ignorant, lazy, expedient or earned". No table, definition or paragraph sets the four out, in that file or in any of the fifteen beside it; "expedient" and "earned" appear as a pair in one rule's reason ("Turns an expedient decision into an earned one") and "expedient" once more in the chapter's list of failures — "*The undocumented expedient decision.* Indistinguishable from negligence at the next audit, including to you." — and "ignorant" and "lazy" appear nowhere else. Version 1 of this book inherited the line as an axiom and added a gloss of its own, "only the record separates expedient from earned", which is close to the Decision Book's reason and is not quite what the fields say. This book's Chapters 0 to 15 never use the four words as a taxonomy. So the first thing this section does is write the definitions the house owes itself, from the regimes that have had to draw the same lines, and the second is to say what separates the third class from the fourth. *(Practice; Reading.)*

**Ignorant.** The rule was not known, or could not be found, when the decision was made. The regimes treat this as a failure of the register rather than of the person: the Decision Book's own rule is "Give every rule in the system its tier", with the reason that "An untiered rule cannot be argued about productively"; the road-design manual requires that justifications "shall be recorded by the design organisation as part of the project record" (read once) precisely so that a later designer can find them; and the auditors' finding about records is that they survive but cannot be found — "Most of the data has been preserved … but it is poorly organised and indexed, and is mixed in with ephemera and duplicate copies" (Sir Alex Allan's review of government digital records, 2015). In the preregistration audit, eleven of thirty-eight plans could not be assessed at all because the plan was ambiguous, in several versions, not frozen or too thin — the record failed before any deviation could be judged (the agent's reading of the split; the exclusion counts are the paper's). An ignorant deviation is therefore logged against the register, not the designer: the entry says which rule was missed and why it was not findable, and the fix is to the register's index. This book's own count of eleven rules carrying different tiers in different chapters is a list of places where an ignorant deviation was waiting to happen. *(Standard (design system); Standard (method); Evidence; Reading.)*

**Lazy.** The rule was known and the departure was taken to save the person effort, with no reason that would survive being written down. Two regimes name this class in so many words. The coding standard: "Deviations must not be permitted: Simply to satisfy the convenience of the developer" (read once). The accessibility regulations' guidance: "You cannot take things like lack of time or knowledge into account in your assessment - or argue that making things accessible is a disproportionate burden because you've not given it priority."; and the monitoring body, in its report: "Lack of time or knowledge does not constitute a disproportionate burden". What a lazy deviation looks like in a log is also measured: "when mandatory free-text reasons are required, users often enter a space or random characters to move past the screen" (Wright et al. 2019, citing prior studies). A lazy deviation is a breach rather than a departure to be logged, and the entry — if there is one — is a space. *(Standard (method); Standard (regulator); Evidence.)*

**Expedient.** The rule was known, the departure was taken for a reason — speed, a deadline, a tool's default, an unmeasured lag — and the reason was real but either a reasonable alternative existed or the departure was not recorded at the time. The coding standard's second forbidden ground names the first half: a deviation is not permitted "When a reasonable alternative coding strategy would make the need for a violation unnecessary" (read once). The Decision Book names the second: "The undocumented expedient decision. Indistinguishable from negligence at the next audit, including to you." The National Audit Office's investigation of pandemic procurement is the public case: rules were relaxed for speed, and what went missing was the record of why — "we also found specific examples where there is insufficient documentation on key decisions, or how risks such as perceived or actual conflicts of interest have been identified or managed", with the head of the office's sentence that "it remains essential that decisions are properly documented and made transparent if government is to maintain public trust". The government's own architecture-decision standard gives the useful other side: a recorded decision lets a later team understand "whether a particular choice was made for the sake of expediency and can therefore be changed with little impact". An expedient deviation logged at the time, with its reason and the alternative it passed over, is the cheapest kind of decision to reverse; unlogged, it is the Decision Book's negligence. *(Standard (method); Standard (design system); Practice; Reading.)*

**Earned.** The rule was known; the departure was taken before it shipped; the reason was specific to the case and not generic; the alternative was named and declined for a reason; what the departure buys was stated and its cost named; a test was run and its result kept; someone other than the author read the entry; and the entry carries a date by which it is re-read. That list is the intersection of what the regimes below ask for. The preregistration literature gives the sharpest criterion for the reason: "One justifiable reason to deviate from a preregistered statistical analysis plan is to increase the validity of the scientific claim – even if doing so comes at the expense of the severity of the test." (Lakens 2024; read once in full, the first clause verified) — a reason that names what is bought and what is paid. The regulator of the comply-or-explain regime gives the reason's shape: "Set the context and background." "Give a convincing rationale for the approach being taken." "Consider any risks and describe any mitigating actions." "Set out when the company intends to comply (timescales)." (read once), with the 2012 paper's demand that explanations "should be specific to the company's position, not generic or off-the-shelf" (read once). The financial regulator gives the two-limb test for granting a departure: compliance "would be unduly burdensome or would not achieve the purpose for which the rules were made" and the departure "would not adversely affect the advancement of any of the regulator's objectives" (FSMA 2000 s.138A(4), as substituted; verified at legislation.gov.uk, 6 October 2026). The Decision Book's own fourth rule gives the purchase list: "Name what the break buys: attention, retention, comprehension, context, register or fit", because "An unnamed break is a mistake made confidently". *(Standard (method); Standard (regulator); Standard (law); Standard (design system); Reading.)*

**What separates expedient from earned.** Not the record alone. Sixty-three per cent of 5,800 architecture decision records were born accepted — "opened directly with a status of "accepted" or equivalent", "thereby bypassing the deliberative context that ADRs are intended to capture" — a record exists for every one of them, written after the fact, and it records a conclusion, not a decision. Four things separate the classes, and the record is the place they are visible: the timing (written before the departure ships, not after — the quality vocabulary keeps a word for each, a *deviation permit* "prior to its realization" (read once) and a *concession* for a nonconforming product already made); the specificity of the reason (the regulator's 2021 finding is what a generic reason looks like at scale); the test (a result, not a tick); and the reader (someone other than the author, whose views the author could not predict — section 5 says why that condition matters). Version 1's one-line gloss, "only the record separates expedient from earned", should read: *the record, written before, specific, tested and read, is what separates them; a record written after records an expedient decision for what it is, which is better than not recording it and is not the same as earning it.* *(Evidence; Standard (method); Reading.)*

### Twelve regimes that let a rule be broken on the record

The chapter read twelve regimes in which a rule may be departed from without the departure being a breach, chosen because each publishes its mechanism and several have measured its use. They are set out briefly, in the regime's own words, with the mechanism in one line each — who decides, who checks, what is written, whether it expires, whether it is published — and then compared.

**Comply or explain (UK corporate governance).** The 2024 Code: "The Code does not set out a rigid set of rules; instead, it offers flexibility through 'comply or explain' reporting against the Provisions." (read once); "Explanations should set out the background, provide a clear rationale for the action the company is taking and explain the impact that the action has had."; "Where a departure from a Provision is intended to be limited in time, the explanation should indicate when the company expects to conform to the Provision."; and "Explanations should be a positive opportunity to communicate, not an onerous obligation." The regulator's 2012 paper, which set the template, proposed that an explanation "should set the context and historical background, should give a convincing rationale for the action it was taking, and describe mitigating action to address any additional risk and to maintain conformity with the relevant principle", and "should indicate whether the deviation from the Code's provisions was limited in time and when the company intended to return to conformity" (two passes); and it placed the reader: "The FRC believes strongly that explanations are directed to shareholders and it is up to them to decide whether to accept or reject explanations." The regulator's news page in 2012 said "a large majority of companies who do not comply with one or more provisions of the Code provide a full explanation of their reasons." Nine years later, the regulator scored a random sample of 100 companies: "Our sample identified 74 cases of non-compliance with the Code, but we found only four explanations that we considered high quality and offered an insight into the companies' approach to good governance."; "The majority of explanations were inadequate, and in one instance, not given at all."; "We found that 'tick-box compliance' continues to be preferred over high quality reporting of good governance practice." (read once) "Such a formulaic approach leads to boilerplate language, and ineffective reporting that lacks substance and information about governance outcomes."; and some companies "that claimed full compliance with the Code, on further investigation had not acknowledged departure from one or more Provisions" (read once). Earlier academic counts agree: in 2006 statements, "almost 25% of UK deviations, and almost 50% of German deviations were not properly justified" (Seidl, Sanderson & Roberts, working paper; the published article, abstract only, reports "some 715 records of deviation" from 257 companies †); across 245 UK companies in 1998–2004, "Almost one in five instances of non-compliances (on average 17%) are not explained" and "the frequency of good explanations is roughly about 20%" (Arcot, Bruno & Faure-Grimaud, discussion paper; read once for the second); and a legal review notes "There is no provision in the UK for any statements by companies to be assessed by any regulatory body." (Keay 2014 †). The regulator's 2025 review counts companies in its 100 disclosing any departure falling from 73 in 2022 to 25 in 2025 (three passes) and gives no proportion judged adequate (two passes); set beside the 2021 finding of undisclosed departures, a falling count of declared deviations is ambiguous. *Mechanism: the board decides; shareholders are the checker and mostly do not check; the record is the annual report, with four elements and a time limit; published.* *(Standard (regulator); Evidence; Evidence †.)*

**Disproportionate burden (public-sector accessibility).** The regulation: "Regulation 6 does not require a public sector body to comply with the accessibility requirement if doing so would impose a disproportionate burden on the public sector body." (read once); "A public sector body must perform an assessment of the extent to which compliance with the accessibility requirement imposes a disproportionate burden."; the assessment must weigh "the size, resources and nature of the public sector body" and "the estimated costs and benefits for the public sector body in relation to the estimated benefits for persons with disabilities, taking into account the frequency and duration of use of the specific website or mobile application"; and if the body so determines it "must— (a) explain in its accessibility statement the parts of the accessibility requirement that could not be complied with" (SI 2018/952, reg. 7; verified 6 October 2026; the regulation shows no outstanding effects). Regulation 8 requires the statement and that the body "keep that statement under regular review" (read once). The guidance adds the review condition — "If your circumstances change, you'll need to review whether something's still a disproportionate burden." — and the model statement's wording for it: "We will make another assessment when the supplier contract is up for renewal, likely to be in [rough timing]." The monitoring body's 2022–24 report, on 1,203 websites and 21 apps: the share "with a disproportionate burden claim at closure" was 17 per cent of simply tested sites, 37 per cent of fully audited sites and 57 per cent of apps; "The understanding of disproportionate burden varies greatly and has often caused confusion and questions. Common issues include:" first, "organisations claim disproportionate burden without having carried out an assessment beforehand"; statements compliant with the model rose from 8 to 65 per cent over monitoring (simplified tests); 55.3 per cent of issues found were fixed during monitoring; and "many accessibility statements are now out-of-date and have not been reviewed in the last 12 months." *Mechanism: the body decides after a mandatory assessment; nobody approves in advance; the record is the public statement, with a review condition; monitoring and enforcement come afterwards, and the first listed failure is the claim without the assessment.* *(Standard (law); Standard (government guidance); Evidence.)*

**Partial conformance (WCAG).** The W3C has no deviation tier; what it has is a statement whose first words are the admission. "Conformance claims are not required. Authors can conform to WCAG 2.2 without making a claim."; where third-party content cannot be controlled, "A 'statement of partial conformance' may be made that the page does not conform, but could conform if certain parts were removed.", and "The form of that statement would be, 'This page does not conform, but would conform to WCAG 2.2 at level X if the following parts from uncontrolled sources were removed.'"; the excluded parts must be identifiable — "they cannot be described as 'all parts that we do not control' unless they are clearly marked as such." — and "No conformance claim can be made if it is not possible to monitor or correct non-conforming content" (verified in the editors' source file; check against the dated Recommendation). The Understanding document: "It is important to recognize that this is a statement of non-conformance". *Mechanism: self-declared, in a fixed sentence that says "does not conform" first, with the exclusions identified; no approver; no review date.* *(Standard (W3C).)*

**Service assessments (UK government digital services).** "'Amber' means that the standard has not yet been met, but the issues are not critical. The service can continue to the next phase of delivery while you work on the amber issues." (read once); "You need to fix amber issues within a reasonable period of time, usually up to 3 months."; "You must record your progress in a live 'tracking amber evidence' document which will be visible to the assessments team." (read once); red means "The service will stay in its current phase of delivery."; the panel is independent for larger services ("more than 100,000 transactions per year" or used by "civil servants in more than one organisation") and departmental otherwise; and the outcome is published (read once). No exemption from the standard exists; only the scope rule. *Mechanism: an independent peer panel decides; a failed point blocks the next phase; a non-critical failure is a time-boxed, tracked, visible non-conformance; published.* *(Standard (government guidance).)*

**The Code of Practice for Statistics (voluntary application and exemptions).** Two mechanisms. For producers outside official statistics, "Where an organisation chooses to adopt and apply TQV for all or some of its statistics or functions, we encourage it to publish a TQV statement."; "It is helpful to your users to be clear if there are aspects of the Code that are not applied"; the regulator adds the organisation to a public register when the statement is published (read once), "will conduct a short annual review to check in with organisations on the Register", and intervenes only on a concern, "on an informal and advisory basis". For official statistics, "Exemptions are rare." "They have mainly been agreed where it is not possible to give contact information for the responsible statistician for security reasons."; the route, shown on the page as Code text with the edition not stated, is that the head of profession "should request an exemption from that practice of the Code from the Director General for Regulation and publish details of the exemption"; and the regulator keeps two public tables, permanent and temporary, the second with a "Duration" column (read once). Edition 3.0's practices carry the deviation duty for methods in plain words: "Base methods on national or international good practice, scientific principles or professional consensus. … Use recognised standards, classifications and definitions. Explain reasons for deviations from these standards and any related implications for use." (practice 6.7; read once). *Mechanism: a named professional head requests; an independent regulator grants; the producer publishes; the list is public and the temporary ones carry a duration; for voluntary adopters, a self-declared statement, a public register and an annual check-in.* *(Standard (regulator); Standard (official statistics).)*

**Waivers (financial regulation).** The statutory test is quoted above; the regulator's own pages add the procedure: "You will need to explain how: Complying with the rule would be unduly burdensome or not achieve its purpose. Granting a waiver will not adversely affect our 3 objectives" (two passes); "Most waivers and modifications are granted for a set period and then cease to apply."; "A waiver or modification will not apply retrospectively."; "We publish all approved waivers and permission notices unless we consider it inappropriate or unnecessary."; and the handbook: "A waiver may be given for a specified period of time only, after which time it will cease to apply." (SUP 8.3.6). *Mechanism: the rule-subject applies with reasons and evidence; the regulator decides against a two-limb test, with other factors; time-limited by default; never retrospective; published by default.* *(Standard (law); Standard (regulator).)*

**Concession and deviation permit (quality management).** The vocabulary standard separates permission after the fact from permission before it. A *concession* is "Permission to use or release a product or service that does not conform to specified requirements", and "is generally limited to the delivery of products and services that have nonconforming characteristics within specified limits and is generally given for a limited quantity of products and services or period of time, and for a specific use."; a *deviation permit* is "Permission to depart from the originally specified requirements of a product or service prior to its realization" (read once), with the same limits (ISO 9000:2015 definitions, read in a railway agency's terminology collection that reproduces them; the standard itself is not open). The management-system standard's clause on nonconformity could not be read: ISO withdrew the 2015 edition on 16 September 2026 and the 2026 edition is not open; the only wording returned identically by two secondary sources is that the organisation shall "retain documented information as evidence of: a) the nature of the nonconformities and any subsequent actions taken; b) the results of any corrective action." (secondary; withdrawn edition). *Mechanism: a named authority grants; before or after is a different word; limited in quantity, time and use.* *(Standard (method); secondary.)*

**Exception reporting (the family-doctor contract).** The best-measured override mechanism found. A clinician may exclude a patient from a quality target for a listed reason; since April 2019 the mechanism is a "Personalised Care Adjustment", for reasons including "Intervention described in the indicator is clinically unsuitable." and "The patient has chosen not to receive the intervention described in the indicator.", and "the decision to apply a PCA should be based on clinical judgement, informed by patient preferences, and underpinned by shared decision-making principles." (read once). Across 8,229 practices in 2008–09: "The median rate of exception reporting was 2.7% (interquartile range 1.9-3.9%) overall and 0.44% (0.14-1.1%) for informed dissent, but variation in rates was wide between practices and across indicators."; "Common reasons for exception reporting were logistical (40.6% of exceptions), clinical contraindication (18.7%), and patient informed dissent (30.1%)."; the cost was £0.58 per patient; and "our findings raise the possibility of gaming" (Doran et al. 2012; read once for the last). A co-author's later summary: "The overall rate of exception reporting has been relatively low, with an initial median rate of 5.3%, which fell to 4.5% in 2008/2009."; "However, 1% of practices initially excluded more than 15% of their patients."; and "we still do not know whether the ability of doctors to exclude patients from the P4P scheme has been in the interests of patients or not." (P4P: pay for performance) (Roland 2016). A cohort of 2.46 million patients found that "Exempted patients had a higher adjusted risk of death in the following year than non-exempted patients, regardless of whether this exemption was for contraindication (hazard ratio 1.37, 99% CI 1.33 to 1.40) or for informed dissent (1.20, 99% CI 1.17 to 1.24)." (Kontopantelis et al. 2016). The 2024–25 official statistics show adjustment rates from 1.8 per cent (smoking) to 29.9 per cent (COPD) by indicator group. *Mechanism: the person at the point of decision decides, against a closed list of reasons; the record is a coded reason; the commissioner may audit; the rates are published nationally; no time limit; and the override's use rose after a missed target and its subjects did worse.* *(Standard (regulator); Evidence.)*

**Ministerial directions (public money).** "If the minister decides it is nevertheless appropriate to continue with a course that does not meet the Managing Public Money standards, the accounting officer should ask for a formal written direction to proceed." and "Since 2011, there has been a presumption that such directions are published, unless the matter must be kept confidential." (HM Treasury). The route runs to the auditor and the committee: the accounting officer tells the Treasury, which tells the Comptroller and Auditor General, who tells the Public Accounts Committee (Institute for Government, two passes), and 107 are known since 1990 (read once). *Mechanism: the person who must break the rule asks the person who wants it broken for the order in writing; the auditor is told; published by presumption.* *(Standard (government guidance); Practice.)*

**Departures and relaxations (road design).** The manual's verbs were quoted in section 3; the procedure is that "a departure application shall be submitted in accordance with the procedures required by the relevant Overseeing Organisation and approved: 1) before the design is finalised; and, 2) prior to their incorporation into the works."; "Adoption of a relaxation and its reasoning shall be recorded by the design organisation." (read once); and for a recommendation not followed, "A justification shall be developed", "Justifications to support all design decisions shall be recorded by the design organisation as part of the project record." and "Any deviation from a recommendation along with the supporting justification shall be available to the Overseeing Organisation as required." (read once). *Mechanism: four tiers; the top cannot be varied; the second only by prior external approval; the third by recorded justification and risk assessment; the fourth freely; the record is part of the project record.* *(Standard (method).)*

**Deviations (the C coding standard).** A deviation is "A violation which has been formally accepted and approved"; its record holds the guideline violated, "A concise description of the circumstances in which a violation is acceptable", "The reason why the deviation is required", the background, and "A set of requirements to include any risk assessment procedures and precautions which must be observed" (two passes, wording stable); the two forbidden grounds are quoted above; and re-categorisation "may only move a guideline to a stricter category, except that Advisory may be disapplied" (read once). *Mechanism: formal acceptance by someone other than the developer; a record with circumstances, reason, background and precautions; two reasons never accepted; rules may be tightened but not loosened.* *(Standard (method).)*

**Waivers nobody re-read (the shuttle).** The negative case, secondary because the board's report could not be reached: "Of the 4,222 Criticality 1/1R Items, '3,233 have waivers (and) 36 percent of these waivers have not been reviewed in 10 years.'" (the Columbia Accident Investigation Board, as excerpted on a third-party site; secondary), with the board's recommendation, in a tertiary report, that "an independent group rather than shuttle program managers be given sole authority to grant waivers." (read once). *Mechanism: the body that wanted the waivers granted them, and nobody was paid to re-read them.* *(Practice; secondary.)*

### What the twelve share

Set side by side, the regimes agree on more than they differ.

| Regime | Who decides | Who checks | What is written | Expires or reviewed | Published |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Comply or explain | the board | shareholders (in practice rarely) | context, rationale, impact, timescale | if time-limited, a date | yes, annual report |
| Disproportionate burden | the body, after assessment | monitoring body, afterwards | the parts not met and why; alternatives | "under regular review"; re-assess on change | yes, the statement |
| WCAG partial conformance | the author | none | "This page does not conform, but would…", exclusions identified | none | yes, if a claim is made |
| Service assessment | independent panel | the panel, at reassessment | the report; a live amber-tracking document | amber ≤ about 3 months | yes |
| Code of Practice | head of profession; voluntary adopter | Director General; annual check-in | the exemption's details; the TQV statement | Duration column; "review regularly" | yes, a register |
| FCA waiver | regulator, on application | the regulator | the direction, with conditions | set period; never retrospective | yes, by default |
| ISO concession / permit | a named authority | — | the permission, its limits and use | limited quantity, time, use | internal |
| Exception reporting | the clinician | the commissioner, by audit | a coded reason | none | rates, nationally |
| Ministerial direction | the minister, in writing | auditor; committee | the direction | — | by presumption |
| DMRB departure | Overseeing Organisation, before design is final | the Overseeing Organisation | the project record | — | internal |
| MISRA deviation | formal approval | the approver | guideline; circumstances; reason; background; precautions | — | internal |
| Shuttle waiver | programme managers | nobody, for a decade | the waiver | none, in practice | no |

Four things recur, and the chapter takes them as the test of a regime. First, a *reason with a shape* — context, rationale, risk, time — and the measured finding that without a reader the shape fills with boilerplate. Second, *a reader other than the author* — a panel, a regulator, an auditor, a Director General — and where that reader is nominal (shareholders; nobody) the record decays. Third, *a time limit or a review condition*, near-universal: the Code's "when the company expects to conform", the statement's "another assessment when", the waiver's "set period", the concession's "period of time", the exemption's "Duration", the amber's three months; the regimes without one in the text read are the partial-conformance statement, exception reporting — the one whose subjects did worse — and the three engineering records whose review is the project's. Fourth, *publication by default*, with the one regime whose private waivers were counted being the one that had not re-read a third of them in ten years. What none of the regimes shows, in the evidence read, is a controlled test that publishing or checking changes behaviour; what three show is a direction: statements compliant with the model rose from 8 to 65 per cent under monitoring, the exception median fell from 5.3 to 4.5 per cent under commissioner audit, and declared departures from the governance code fell while undisclosed ones were found. *(Reading; Evidence.)*

Two things the regimes add that the Decision Book's four lines lack: the *who* — who decided and who checked, which the government's analysis-quality guidance has in both its logs ("explain who made the decision and who signed it off"; read once) — and the *when next* — a review date, which the house's template has nowhere. Section 5 builds the entry. *(Reading.)*

## 5 · The record

### What the house's log asks for, in how many versions

The Decision Book's override log is printed five times and is three different things. The sentence this chapter was briefed on — "Trigger · rule and tier · alternative chosen · evidence. Four lines." — is at one line of the skill file. The rule in the register, BRK-08, is "Log every override: trigger, rule, alternative, evidence" — "rule" without "and tier", "alternative" without "chosen". The checklist, printed in two files, is "Override logged: trigger, rule and tier, alternative, evidence". And the template file, under the words "Four lines per departure", is a table with five columns: Date, then the four. This book inherited the drift: Chapter 0 gives the skill file's four; Chapters 2 to 5, in their break tests, give a different four — "(what rule, why, for whom, what proved it)" — of which "for whom" is in none of the house's printings and "alternative chosen" is in none of the chapters'; Chapters 1, 6 and 7 name the record without fields; Chapters 8 to 11 do not mention logging a break; Chapters 12 and 13 say a break "is logged" without saying where; and Chapters 14 and 15 log to records of their own. It is what Chapter 15 found of every rule system it read, the rule changing under the same name, and the Decision Book's own eleventh rule is the cure: "State every value in the design system once; other mentions point to it", because "A value stated twice has already begun to diverge". The entry below is stated once, in section 11, and this chapter's rules point to it. *(Standard (design system); Reading; the compilation.)*

### What other fields put in the record

Seven records of decisions were read for their fields, and the fields are set out here because the entry in section 11 is their intersection.

**The architecture decision record.** The founding post names its purpose — "One of the hardest things to track during the life of a project is the motivation behind certain decisions." — and the two bad choices a newcomer otherwise has, to "Blindly accept the decision." or "Blindly change it." (read once); its form is Title, Context ("the forces at play … These forces are probably in tension, and should be called out as such."), Decision ("stated in full sentences, with active voice"), Status ("proposed" or "accepted"; later "deprecated" or "superseded" "with a reference to its replacement") and Consequences ("All consequences should be listed here, not just the "positive" ones."); "The whole document should be one or two pages long."; "ADRs will be numbered sequentially and monotonically. Numbers will not be reused."; "If a decision is reversed, we will keep the old one around, but mark it as superseded." The immutability the house may have assumed is not in that post — the word is absent and a control string asserting it was rejected — and lives in later guidance: "When the team accepts an ADR, it becomes immutable." and "When the team accepts the new ADR, it supersedes the previous ADR." (AWS). The government's standard adds the location and the reason for it: "Architecture decisions should be stored in version control so there is a record of what was changed, who by, and when."; "If a decision has been superseded by another decision, the old ADR must be clearly marked as 'superseded'." (the GDS Way, reviewed 5 March 2026); and a cross-government framework published on 4 November 2025 fixes the fields as title, date, status, context, decision, consequences, stakeholders consulted and links to supporting documents (two passes). The measured life of the form: adoption "is still low", "About 50% of all repositories with ADRs contain just one to five ADRs" (Buchgeher et al. 2023 †); 63 per cent of 5,800 records born "accepted" (Santana Junior et al. 2026 †); and in one controlled comparison, the short founding template beat the longer one on an overall score, 0.8 against 0.7, with 33 students (Nogueira, Silva & Conte 2026; read once for the scores). *(Practice; Standard (government guidance); Evidence †.)*

**The preregistration deviation.** The definition: "any discrepancy between what the authors said they would do in the preregistration and what the authors actually did in the final article." The table proposed for reporting it has columns for the number, the details (type, reason and timing), the original wording, the deviation's description and the reader impact (two passes; headings not checked letter by letter); the reasons offered are a typo or oversight, a data or documentation error, a plan "not possible or inappropriate because of data", new knowledge, a reviewer's suggestion and miscommunication (read once); and the timing is the field the others lack — "It is important to transparently report the timing of when the deviation was recognized or occurred because data-dependent deviations increase risk of bias." The authors ask that the table sit in the paper and that "researchers should also include the results of the preregistered analyses alongside the deviated analyses". The base rate it answers is the audit's: "Two out of 27 preregistered studies contained no deviations from the preregistration plan."; "One study (4%) reported all the deviations."; "In the remaining 24 of 27 (89%) studies, there was at least one item for which a discrepancy between the preregistration plan and the journal article was not fully disclosed."; and the reason disclosure matters is the reader's default — "Few readers will take the time to meticulously compare the published paper with the preregistered plan. Instead, they will assume that, unless noted otherwise, the preregistered plan was followed" (read once). The repository's own procedure fixes the before-and-after split: before analysis, an amendment with a mandatory justification ("You will be prompted to enter a justification for the update." "This field must have information entered in order for the update to be submitted."); after, "Start a Transparent Changes document now" and keep the original standing. *(Standard (method); Evidence; Practice.)*

**The analysis-quality logs.** The government's own guidance for analysis has two registers with the same spine. "The assumptions log is a register of assumptions … that have been risk assessed and signed off by an appropriate governance group or stakeholder." and its entries "describe each assumption, quantify its effect and reliability, set out when it was made, explain why it was made, explain who made the assumption and who signed it off"; "The decisions log is a register of decisions" whose entries "describe each decision, set out when it was made, explain why it was made, explain who made the decision and who signed it off." (read once). *(Standard (government guidance).)*

**The appraisal record.** The Treasury's appraisal guidance, rewritten in February 2026, uses "must" for the reason: "Practitioners must record the reason for each rating in a brief paragraph, explaining the underlying evidence, assumptions and sources."; "Practitioners should explain transparently what options have been considered as well as the reasons they have or have not been progressed to shortlist appraisal."; and the purpose: "This provides a clear sequence of reasoning for reviewers and decision makers, and supports future evaluation." *(Standard (government guidance).)*

**The changelog.** The lightest of the conventions and the only one whose first principle is the reader: "Changelogs are for humans, not machines."; "There should be an entry for every single version."; "The release date of each version is displayed."; "Keep an Unreleased section at the top to track upcoming changes."; "If you do nothing else, list deprecations, removals, and any breaking changes in your changelog."; and the warning this book's records should carry on their face: "A changelog which only mentions some of the changes can be as dangerous as not having a changelog." The versioning convention beside it gives the never-edit rule in full: "Once a versioned package has been released, the contents of that version MUST NOT be modified. Any modifications MUST be released as a new version." and, for when the rule itself is broken, "Even under this circumstance, it is unacceptable to modify versioned releases." (Semantic Versioning 2.0.0). *(Practice; Standard (community).)*

**The datasheet and the model card.** The one-document conventions, read for section 7 and for their maintenance sections. The datasheet's questions are grouped by "motivation, composition, collection process, preprocessing/cleaning/labeling, uses, distribution, and maintenance" (read once), the last asking "Who will be supporting/hosting/maintaining the dataset?", "Is there an erratum?", "Will older versions of the dataset continue to be supported/hosted/maintained?" and "If so, please describe how its obsolescence will be communicated to dataset consumers."; and the authors insist the document is written, not generated: "We emphasize that the process of creating a datasheet is not intended to be automated." ### When a written reason helps, and when it is noise

The belief that requiring a reason improves the decision has been tested, and the result is narrower than the belief. The experimental core is a 1989 paper whose abstract states the model and reports that the experiment supported it: "(a) when people know the views of the audience and are unconstrained by past commitments, they will rely on the low-effort acceptability heuristic and simply shift their views toward those of the prospective audience,"; "(b) when people do not know the views of the audience and are unconstrained by past commitments, they will be motivated to think in relatively flexible, multidimensional ways (preemptive self-criticism),"; "(c) when people are accountable for positions to which they feel committed, they will devote the majority of their mental effort to justifying those positions (defensive bolstering)."; "The experiment yielded results supportive of these 3 predictions." (Tetlock, Skitka & Boettger 1989 †; authorship confirmed through Crossref, *JPSP* 57(4):632–640). The review that made the finding famous could not be read beyond its abstract, which asks "Under what conditions will accountability attenuate, have no effect on, or amplify cognitive biases?" and "Does accountability alter how people think or merely what people say they think?" (Lerner & Tetlock 1999 †); the words "unknown views" and "defensive bolstering" are the 1989 paper's and are not placed in the 1999 review's mouth here. A later summary states the condition in a sentence: "Accountability in front of an audience with unknown views generally results in more cognitive effort." (Vieider 2008, secondary). *(Evidence †; secondary.)*

Against it, two experiments found nothing. In 144 and then 110 participants asked to justify their judgements on randomly chosen trials, "Process accountability neither changed how accurately people made a judgment, nor the judgment strategies." and "Justifying the judgment process only decreased confidence in trials involving a justification." (Hoffmann, Gaissmaier & von Helversen 2017). And a forecasting tournament of more than a thousand people over ten months found that accountability for outcomes "boosted forecasting accuracy beyond holding them accountable for process", while "Process accountability promoted more effective knowledge sharing, improving accuracy among observers." (Chang et al. 2017 †). The audit evidence is of the same shape: impact assessments "are being used once a policy decision has been taken" and "A predetermined policy agenda can have a far greater influence on government action than the outcome or findings of the impact assessment." (NAO 2007); two years later, "only 20 per cent presented the results of an evaluation of a range of options", "60 per cent of IAs included £0 for either the costs or benefits" and "approximately one fifth provided no justification for this assessment" (NAO 2009), with the mechanism named — "the new requirements have led policy officials to provide only the information specifically requested rather than present an analysis of a range of regulatory proposals." And a reason-giving duty was repealed in England in 2013 because the reasons were being written twice: the requirement on planning authorities "to provide a summary of reasons for approval" was said by the local government planning community to be "both burdensome and unnecessary because it duplicates material documented elsewhere", since officer reports "typically provide far more detail on the logic and reasoning behind a particular decision than a decision notice" (Explanatory Memorandum to SI 2013/1238; the memorandum says "duplicates", not "boilerplate"). *(Evidence; Evidence †; Practice; Standard (law).)*

Read together: a written reason improves the *decision* when it is owed before the decision, to a reader whose view the writer cannot guess; it improves the *next reader's* decision in most conditions, which is the architecture-record argument and the forecasting tournament's finding; it does nothing, or lowers confidence, when demanded after the fact as a form; and it should be written once, where the reasoning is done, and pointed to from everywhere else. For a book of 486 rules this says four things about its deviation entry: it is written before the departure ships; it names a reader who is not the author and whose view is not known in advance; it is kept where the work is kept, in the repository beside the build, as the government standard and the Technology Radar entry both say ("stored in version control"; "storing these details in source control, instead of a wiki or website, as then they can provide a record that remains in sync with the code itself"); and it is one entry, not an entry here and a summary there. *(Reading.)*

### How records decay, and who reads them

Nobody has measured the readership of decision records directly; the nearest are the founding post's "six to ten developers" and the mining study's contributor counts, which give no readership. What has been measured is decay. Software engineers "often do not maintain documentation"; "When SEs do update documents, they usually do so several weeks after changes are made to the code."; forty-four per cent of forty-five respondents somewhat agreed and 24 per cent strongly agreed that "Documentation is always outdated relative to the current state of a software system."; and the one condition under which they kept it current is the one this book's records should be built on — "SEs were likely to update documentation continually only when it was attached to the process for completing a change request" (Lethbridge, Singer & Forward 2003). Architects defer the record and "at that point, they've often forgotten many decisions and the rationale behind them" (Harrison, Avgeriou & Zdun 2007). Outward-pointing records rot: "one out of five STM articles suffering from reference rot", and "seven out of ten" of those with web references (Klein et al. 2014) — which bears on every record in this book that cites a URL, including this chapter's. Institutional memory leaves with people: "Several departments, including the Treasury and the Cabinet Office, lose a quarter of their staff each year" (Institute for Government 2019). And a record-keeping rule that depends on each person deciding, as a separate act, what counts as a record is not followed: "Existing systems which require individual users to identify documents that should constitute official records, and then to save them into an EDRMS [an electronic records system] or corporate file plan, have not worked well. The processes have been burdensome and compliance poor." (Allan 2015). *(Evidence; Practice.)*

The design consequence is the same one Chapter 8 drew for the dashboard's freshness and Chapter 15 drew for its retirement: the record is kept current only where updating it is part of the step that makes the change. For the deviation log that means the entry is written in the same change that ships the departure, and the build refuses the change without it — section 8's check. For the record of the dashboard it means the record's review line carries a date and the build reports the records whose date has passed, as the accessibility statement's "last reviewed" line and the datasheet's maintenance section both intend, and as the monitoring body found many statements had not been. *(Reading.)*

### Does disclosing a deviation cost trust?

No experiment on it was found. The nearest evidence is a preregistered survey of 34 journal editors (of 170 emailed), who rated a disclosed deviation with an explanation as neutral to slightly positive for their view of a manuscript — "M = 3.3, SD = 1.3" on a scale from 1 to 5 — and an undisclosed deviation found in review as strongly negative, "M = 1.3, SD = 0.5" (Willroth & Atherton 2024; the article's own sentence for the second scenario prints "reported" where the scenario is the unreported one, so the numbers are quoted and the sentence is not). The same editors said they checked adherence to the plan "65% of the time, on average". A registered report testing whether the preregistered *label* raises trust was inconclusive — "we report ambiguous Bayes factors and conclude that we do not have strong evidence towards answering our research questions." (Field et al. 2020) — and is not about deviations. So, on a small sample of gatekeepers' stated attitudes: the penalty attaches to concealment, not to the deviation. That is the only evidence this book has on the question Chapter 15 raised of whether printing a dashboard's departures costs it, and it points the same way as Chapter 15's producers, who disclosed errors as they happened. `[Validate with users]` *(Evidence; Reading.)*

From the regimes' agreement (section 4), the records' fields and the evidence on when a reason helps, the deviation entry has these lines, and section 11 states them once as the chapter's record: the rule and its tier, in the register's sense; the trigger — what made the departure necessary, specific to the case; the alternative — what was done instead, and the alternative that was declined; what it buys, from the Decision Book's list, and what it costs; the test — which of the six forms, and its result; who decided and who checked, the second not the first; the date; the review date or the condition on which it lapses; and a status that moves — proposed, accepted, superseded, lapsed — with the old entry kept. ## 6 · Overrides in practice: what a rule set that fires too often does to the people under it

### The measured rates

In the one field that logs every override, the rates are known. The 2006 review of seventeen papers: "Drug safety alerts are overridden by clinicians in 49% to 96% of cases." — and in the same abstract, the finding that keeps this from being a story about careless people: "Alert overriding may often be justified and adverse drug events due to overridden alerts are not always preventable."; the fault is placed in "error-producing conditions like low specificity, low sensitivity, unclear information content, unnecessary workflow disruptions" (van der Sijs et al. 2006 †). A 2020 review of 23 articles: "The range of average override alerts was 46.2%-96.2%." and "An average of 29.4%-100% of the overrides alerts were classified as appropriate" (Poly et al. 2020). A 2024 meta-analysis of fifteen studies, for drug-interaction alerts only: "the overall prevalence of alert override by physicians was 90% (CI95% 85–95%)" (Felisberto et al. 2024). In one system, 157,483 alerts on two million orders: "82 889 (52.6%) of alerts were overridden."; by chart review of 600 overrides, "An average of 53% of overrides were classified as appropriate" ranging "from 12% for renal recommendations to 92% for patient allergies" (Nanji et al. 2014 †). The last range is the point: whether an override is right depends on the rule, from one in eight to nine in ten, so a high override rate indicts the rule as often as the person. *(Evidence; Evidence †.)*

### The dose–response

What drives the rate is measured in one cohort of 112 primary-care clinicians over three and a half years, 1.27 million advisories and 326,203 drug alerts: "Likelihood of reminder acceptance dropped by 30% for each additional reminder received per encounter, and by 10% for each five percentage point increase in proportion of repeated reminders."; "Clinicians became less likely to accept alerts as they received more of them, particularly more repeated alerts."; and, as important for a rule set, "There was no evidence of an effect of workload per se, or of desensitization over time for a newly deployed alert." (Ancker et al. 2017). What cuts acceptance is neither busyness nor habituation but how many rules fire at once and how often the same one repeats. The patient-safety primer states the end state: clinicians "ignore both the bothersome, clinically meaningless alarms and the critical alerts that warn of impending serious patient harm". *(Evidence; Practice.)*

### Tiering by consequence, and hard stops

Two studies measure the remedy the fields' three-tier vocabularies imply. Where interaction alerts were tiered by severity at one centre and not at another, across 71,350 alerts, "Compliance with DDI alerts [drug–drug interaction alerts] was significantly higher at the site with tiered DDI alerts compared to the non-tiered site (29% vs. 10%, p < 0.001)." and "At the tiered site, 100% of the most severe alerts were accepted, vs. only 34% at the non-tiered site" — with the caveat that the tiered site used hard stops for the severest alerts, so the hundred reflects in part that the top tier could not be overridden (Paterno et al. 2009 †; the caveat read once). Where most alerts were made non-interruptive and only a minority interrupted, the interrupting ones were accepted 67 per cent of the time (Shah et al. 2006; secondary, no control group). And a systematic review of 32 studies of hard stops — rules that cannot be overridden — found "Seventy-nine percent showed improvement in health outcomes and 88% in process outcomes.", "Hard stops were superior to soft stops in 3 of 4 studies.", and the cost: "Eleven studies reported on unintended consequences including avoidance of hard-stopped workflow, increased alert frequency, and delay to care." (Powers et al. 2018 †). A rule that cannot be broken works better than one that can, and people route around it. *(Evidence; Evidence †; secondary.)*

### Checklists, used and ticked

The book has fifteen checklists, one per chapter, and the evidence on checklists is the evidence on the difference between a list that is used and a list that is ticked. The eight-hospital study: "The rate of death was 1.5% before the checklist was introduced and declined to 0.8% afterward (P=0.003)." and complications from 11.0 to 7.0 per cent, with the study's own hypothesis sentence saying what the checklist was for — "a 19-item surgical safety checklist designed to improve team communication and consistency of care" (Haynes et al. 2009 †). The 101-hospital study of a mandated checklist, 109,341 procedures before and 106,370 after: "Implementation of surgical safety checklists in Ontario, Canada, was not associated with significant reductions in operative mortality or complications." (Urbach et al. 2014 †). The accompanying editorial, read only as quoted on a third party's page: "it is not the act of ticking off a checklist that reduces complications, but performance of the actions it calls for"; "In the absence of direct monitoring by observation, true compliance is unknown."; "Compliance was undoubtedly much lower than the reported 98%."; and "If a checklist is required, the person responsible for documentation will ensure that all boxes are ticked." (Leape 2014, secondary; read once for the last). Observed directly in five English hospitals, 565 time-outs and 309 sign-outs: "On average, two-thirds of the items were checked, team members were absent in more than 40% of cases, and they failed to pause or focus on the checks in more than 70% of cases."; "Surgical safety checklist performance was better when surgeons led and when all team members were present and paused." (Russ et al. 2015 †). The pooled estimate across seven studies is a risk ratio of 0.59 for any complication and "a strong correlation between a significant decrease in postoperative complications and adherence to aspects of care embedded in the checklist" (Bergs et al. 2014 †); the sceptical review of 25 studies notes that "Postoperative mortality rates were not significantly decreased in any studies in developed nations" and that "many of the positive changes … were due to temporal changes, confounding factors and publication bias" (De Jager et al. 2016; the journal attribution unverified). The flight-deck literature had said it first, qualitatively: "a long and detailed checklist is no guarantee of absolute safety. Indeed, it carries the risk that some pilots might choose not to use the checklist or conduct it poorly because of its length." (Degani & Wiener 1990). *(Evidence; Evidence †; secondary.)*

### What this means for a book with 433 Fixed rules and fifteen checklists

A rule set in which nearly everything carries the strongest word, and in which the strongest word is enforced by a person reading a list, is the condition under which, in the one field that measures it, between half and nineteen-twentieths of firings are dismissed — nine in ten for interaction alerts — and the dismissal reaches the ones that matter. The remedy the fields use is also measured: fewer rules in the top tier; the top tier set apart and, where the consequence warrants it, not overridable; everything else logged rather than enforced. The remedy the checklist evidence adds is that a list works where it changes what a team does together and is observed to be used, and not where it is a document someone is responsible for ticking.

So the register in section 8 is built in three parts and the book's fifteen checklists become two things. The *Fixed register* is short — the requirements the build can test, which fail the build; the sequences, which the build checks by the order of records and dates — and is the only list a reviewer is asked to hold. The *Adaptable register* is long, and it is a list of house defaults with their grounds rather than a checklist — the list a departure is logged against. And the *checks* are the build's: the fifteen chapters' lists are folded into the register's test column and run by the build or by a named reviewer, with the result written, as Chapter 13's scan and Chapter 12's crop test already are; what is left of each chapter's checklist is the handful of items that are a conversation and not a check, which stay as the chapter's "what proves a break works". Section 8 gives the counts and the form. The evidence read here does not say how many rules a person can hold; the field's own folklore figures were not verifiable at source and are not printed. What it does say is that every extra rule that fires in the same encounter costs a measured share of the attention paid to all of them. *(Reading; Nightlife Culture.)*

## 9 · The failures, read for the machinery

Carly's eight sentences, read against a register and a log rather than a display.

**"A dashboard fails if it looks the same every month."** The machinery's version is a register that looks the same every year: rules nobody re-reads, waivers nobody reviews. The review date on every entry and the stale-record report are the answer, and the regimes' near-universal time limit is the precedent. *(Reading.)*

**"It fails if its just a fancy spreadsheet with some charts that tell you lots of big numbers, but not what actually happens."** The register's equivalent is a log that records conclusions — 63 per cent of records born accepted; a reason field with a space in it — and not decisions. The entry written before, with the alternative declined and the test's result, is what records what actually happened. *(Reading.)*

**"It fails if a viewer reads it and asks 'what am I looking at'."** A rule set in which 89 per cent of rules carry the strongest word, in fifty-three wordings, 182 kinds and six names for the documents beneath it, is one a reviewer reads and asks that of. Section 3's three senses, section 7's one record and one log, and Chapter 0's revision are the fix; the Decision Book's test is the pass — name the tier, or the register has failed. *(Reading.)*

**"For nightlife, it fails if they do not recognise their place within the front-end interface."** The machinery's reader is the scene's own governance under Chapter 10's record, and the one line that touches recognition in the log is *who checked*. A deviation that changes how a place is shown is checked by someone from the place, which is what Chapter 12's review line already says. *(Reading.)*

**"It fails if people dont use it."** The one field that logs every override found that people stop using rules that fire too often, by a measured 30 per cent per extra rule, and that a short top tier set apart is obeyed. A register with two hundred Fixed rules enforced by the build and three hundred defaults logged is the design that is used; fifteen checklists ticked by a person is the one that is not. **"It fails if breaks or isnt reliable."** A Fixed requirement enforced by a check that fails the build is the only kind of rule that cannot be broken by accident; everything else can, and is logged when it is. ## 11 · What holds true regardless

Seven things this chapter can state for any rule system beneath a dashboard, each with its kind and its grounds.

**1. A tier is a statement about whether a right answer exists, and the fields that draw the same three lines add a top tier that cannot be varied at all, a named approver who is not the designer for departures from the tier below it, and a list of reasons that are never earned.** The house's split is verified at source; the IETF's, ISO's, W3C's, the road manual's and the coding standard's are set beside it; and the instruction that comes with every one of them is to use the strongest word sparingly. *Standard (method); Fixed as a statement of the record.* Grounds: *Navigating Ambiguity* (verified in the publisher's guide); RFC 2119 §6; ISO/IEC Directives Part 2 (read once); DMRB GG 101; MISRA Compliance 2020 (two passes).

**2. A rule set in which nearly everything carries the strongest word is read as noise, and the cost is measured: acceptance falls by a step with each extra rule that fires, and the severe rules are obeyed only where they are set apart.** *Evidence; Fixed as a statement of the record.* `[Further research]` for the full texts behind the daggers. Grounds: Ancker et al. 2017 (30 per cent per additional reminder; 112 clinicians; 1.27 million advisories); van der Sijs 2006 (49–96 per cent †); Felisberto 2024 (90 per cent pooled); Nanji 2014 (53 per cent of overrides appropriate, 12–92 by rule †); Paterno 2009 (29 against 10 per cent; 100 per cent of the severest at the tiered site, with hard stops †); Powers 2018 (hard stops better in 3 of 4; routed around †).

**3. A written reason improves a decision when it is owed before the decision to a reader whose view is unknown, improves the next reader's decision in most conditions, and is noise when demanded after the fact as a form.** *Evidence; Fixed as a statement of the record.* `[Further research]` for the full texts behind the daggers. Grounds: Tetlock, Skitka & Boettger 1989 (†); Hoffmann et al. 2017 (null on accuracy; lowered confidence); Chang et al. 2017 (observers' accuracy †); the FRC's 4 of 74; the NAO's 20 and 60 per cent; Wright et al. 2019 ("a space or random characters").

**4. A deviation is earned by its timing, its specificity, its test and its reader, and the record is where the four are visible; a record written after the fact records an expedient decision for what it is and does not earn it.** The entry has nine lines — rule and tier; trigger; alternative, and the one declined; what it buys and what it costs; test and result; who decided and who checked; date; review date or lapse condition; status, with the old entry kept — stated once, below. *Reading; Fixed as a sequence* (before the departure ships) *and as a requirement* (the lines are filled). Grounds: the twelve regimes of section 4; the eight records of section 5; Santana Junior et al. 2026 (63 per cent born accepted †); Claesen et al. 2021 (1 of 27 disclosed all).

**5. A record is kept current only where updating it is part of the step that makes the change, and a record nobody is paid to re-read decays; so every entry carries a review date and the build lists the entries whose date has passed.** *Evidence; Fixed as a requirement.* Grounds: Lethbridge, Singer & Forward 2003; GDS 2024 ("many accessibility statements are now out-of-date"); the Columbia board's 36 per cent (secondary); Klein et al. 2014 (reference rot); the near-universal time limit of section 4.

**6. Many records that write the same concept under different names are one record written once per concept and drawn into views, and the view the public sees begins with what is not met.** *Standard (official statistics); Reading; Fixed as a requirement* (each concept once) *with the publication of the producer's view the house's.* Grounds: Eurostat's SIMS ("each concept is only reported upon once"); the ONS and Analysis Function's two tiers; Nygard against the hand-maintained large document; the compilation's matrix (a date in twelve records under twelve names; three lines the chapters call one field); WCAG's partial-conformance sentence and the accessibility statement's model.

**7. A checklist works where it changes what a team does together and its use is observed, and not where it is a document someone is responsible for ticking; a book's rules are therefore enforced by the build where they can be checked and logged where they cannot, and the list a person holds is short.** *Evidence; Fixed as a statement of the record.* `[Further research]` for the full texts behind the daggers. Grounds: Haynes 2009 (1.5 to 0.8 per cent, eight hospitals †); Urbach 2014 (101 hospitals, no significant change †); Leape 2014 (98 per cent reported; secondary); Russ 2015 (two-thirds of items; 70 per cent no pause †); Bergs 2014 (effect tracks adherence †); Degani & Wiener 1990.

Three things the chapter will not state as truths. That disclosing a dashboard's departures costs it nothing (thirty-four editors' stated attitude is the only evidence, and it is about concealment). That a register of two hundred Fixed rules is short enough (the fields' folklore figures for how many rules a person can hold were not verifiable at source; the build's first year is where the count is tested). And that the one record will be read (nobody has measured the readership of such records; what is measured is that they are not kept, and the one record is designed for the condition under which they are).

## What proves a break works

The test for a departure from one of this chapter's rules is the Decision Book's own, with one line added. **Take any decision currently being argued about in a project and name its tier from the register.** If it is a requirement, the argument is over and the build's check decides; if it is a sequence, the record's dates decide; if it is Adaptable, the argument is a design conversation to be had properly and its outcome is an entry; if it is Decorative, there was never anything to argue about. **If the tier cannot be found, the register has failed, not the argument** — and that is a gap in the register, logged in four fields with an interim rule, and closed only when a rule exists that would have caught it. Then find the entry: a departure from this chapter's own rules — a Fixed register that grew long; an entry written after; a record edited rather than superseded; a review date left blank — is proved to work the way every other one is, by the entry that says so, read by someone who did not write it, with a date on which it is read again. If no such entry exists, it was not a break; it was the undocumented expedient decision, and the Decision Book has already said what that is indistinguishable from.

## Checklist

- [ ] The tier found in the register before the argument, in one of its five wordings
- [ ] The departure classified — ignorant (fix the register), lazy (a breach), expedient (logged, cheap to reverse) or earned (nine lines)
- [ ] Neither forbidden reason: not convenience; no reasonable alternative untaken
- [ ] The entry written before the change ships, in the same change, type named
- [ ] A checker named who is not the author; from the place, where the place is what changes
- [ ] The test's form matched to the tier's sense, and its result written, not ticked
- [ ] A review date or lapse condition on the entry, and on every record section touched
- [ ] Nothing accepted edited; superseded and kept
- [ ] The stale-record and register reports read at this build
- [ ] If the register had no rule: the gap logged with an interim rule, and closed only by a rule that would have caught it
