## The question

**What does a dashboard do, why does it matter, what is risked when it is done poorly, what is gained when it is done well — and how is any of that proved?**

---

## The 60-second version

> The word "dashboard" is older than the car. In 1846 it meant the board at the front of a carriage that stopped the horses' hooves throwing mud into your lap. Only in 1990 did a dictionary record it meaning a screen. The job never changed: a dashboard keeps the mud out. The mud is everything that will not fit — the uncleaned data, the things nobody thought to count, the knowledge that lives in people rather than in fields — and every chapter of this book is about what gets screened out and what that costs.
>
> The field that builds dashboards cannot agree what one is. Its most-quoted definition — a single screen, monitored at a glance — describes about a fifth of the dashboards actually made. The only survey of managers who use them found they are used mainly to *talk* with, not to watch. And after twenty years of claims, the evidence that dashboards change decisions is thin everywhere and mixed where it is best. In hospitals, most studies are before-and-after designs at serious or moderate risk of bias. In schools, dashboards move the things that are easy to count and leave the things they were built for untouched. In government, the one meta-analysis finds a small average effect that depends on what people do around the display.
>
> What *is* well evidenced is uncomfortable. The most reliable reason a dashboard fails is that nobody opens it. The most reliable thing a published measure does is change the behaviour it measures. Trust attaches to the people who produce a number and how they conduct themselves, not to the chart: in Britain, 85% of people trust the national statistics office's figures and 32% trust the government to present them honestly. And the arithmetic of aggregation is not an opinion. The same numbers, regrouped, can produce any correlation you like; the smallest units sit at both ends of every ranking; and official statistics are designed to blank out exactly the small cells where the interesting case lives.
>
> So this book's working definition is deliberately not about screens. **A dashboard is a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader.** It is an argument, not a window. Done poorly, it is a confident picture of the wrong thing, trusted by people who cannot check it, that quietly reshapes what it claims to measure. Done well, it is a shared object that several communities can each read in their own way; it shows its own uncertainty and its own age; it is also a data release and a conversation; and it leaves a door open to the mud.

## 1 · Start with the mud

Shannon Mattern, a historian of urban media, traced the word. "The term dashboard, first used in 1846, originally referred to the board or leather apron on the front of a vehicle that kept horse hooves and wheels from splashing mud into the interior." The instrument panel of the Model T inherited the name because it sat where the splash-board had been; the aircraft cockpit, the mission-control room, the Bloomberg terminal and the city operations centre inherited the idea. "Only in 1990, according to the Oxford English Dictionary, did the term come to denote a 'screen giving a graphical summary of various types of information…'"

The first meaning explains the second. A dashboard is a surface designed to keep most of the world out so that a few things can be seen clearly. Mattern again: "The acknowledged partiality of the dashboard's rendering might make us wonder what is bracketed out. Why, all the mud of course! All the dirty (un-'cleaned') data, the variables that have nothing to do with key performance (however it's defined), the parts that don't lend themselves to quantification and visualization."

### What the field says a dashboard is

The definition the field quotes is Stephen Few's, from 2004: "A dashboard is a visual display of the most important information needed to achieve one or more objectives; consolidated and arranged on a single screen so the information can be monitored at a glance." Few — a practitioner and teacher, not a researcher — wrote it in irritation at the software industry. His article opened, "Everyone in the business intelligence (BI) space is wild about dashboards, but few proponents are talking about the same thing," and he insisted that "a dashboard is a type of display or style of presentation, not a specific type of information or technology." For Few the whole point was *monitoring*: a dashboard tells you whether something needs your attention now. Anything else — analysis, exploration, a report with charts — was a different object wearing the name. He was equally blunt about the state of the art: "most dashboards say too little, and what they do say requires far too much effort to discern."

Wayne Eckerson, writing from inside the business-intelligence industry, defined the opposite thing with the same word: "a layered information delivery system that parcels out information, insights, and alerts to users on demand so they can measure, monitor, and manage business performance more effectively." Not a screen but a system: a top layer of graphical measures, a middle layer you can slice, a bottom layer of detail, and three jobs — monitoring, analysis and management — mapped onto three kinds of user: operational, tactical, strategic. Few's dashboard is one page. Eckerson's is a building.

Twenty years on, the academic field admitted it could not choose. Alper Sarikaya and colleagues titled their 2019 paper "What Do We Talk About When We Talk About Dashboards?" and found that "even the definition of a dashboard is in flux." Their way out was to split the word: "We distinguish between the visual genre of dashboards (a visual data representation structured as a tiled layout of simple charts and/or large numbers…) and the functional genre (an interactive display that enables real-time monitoring of dynamically updating data)." Something counts as a dashboard if it looks like one *or* works like one. When they sorted 83 real examples, seven clusters emerged, and one of them — fifteen dashboards they called "Dashboards Evolved" — contained things that "visually appeared as dashboards, but may not fit the strictest definition of dashboard functionality." Their study is a peer-reviewed analysis of a convenience sample, not a user study; but it is the field's own admission.

The pattern holds at scale. Benjamin Bach and colleagues reviewed 144 dashboards for a 2023 paper and found that the kind Few described — static, single-screen — was 21% of the corpus; 73% were interactive and analytic. A census of 25,620 dashboards published on Tableau Public found a median of eight panels, cross-filtering in three quarters, and a bar chart in six out of ten. Few's definition is still quoted at the top of nearly every paper, and then classified around.

### The job the users say it does

The only survey this chapter could find of managers who actually use dashboards — 145 Finnish sales managers, of whom 36 had one — reported that "dashboards are most intensively used for communication and consistency purposes, even more than for self-monitoring. This highlights the fact that dashboards are to be seen as collaborative tools rather than individual decision support systems…" It is a small survey in one country, and should be read as a prompt rather than a finding. But Sarikaya's corpus had a "Communication" cluster of thirteen, and Bach's genres include "magazine" and "infographic". In practice, people use dashboards to show each other things and to agree what is true.

### The definition this book uses

This book will not define a dashboard by its shape. **A dashboard is a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader.** Three parts carry the weight. *Data*, because a dashboard is downstream of measurement and inherits every choice made in it. *A decision*, because a display that changes nothing is decoration, and the test of a dashboard is what someone does after reading it. *A named reader*, because — as the rest of this chapter shows — the same numbers mean different things to different people, and a display built for nobody in particular serves nobody at all.

Under that definition Few's single screen, Eckerson's layered system, a public health tracker, a council's performance report and the Vibe Map are all dashboards, differing in reader and decision. Power BI, the Microsoft product that has become shorthand for a certain kind of corporate dashboard, is one species of the genus, not the genus. A club flyer is not a dashboard, but it is a display that turns information into a decision for a named reader faster than most dashboards manage, and Chapter 14 returns to what that teaches.

### Where it came from, and the argument buried in the metaphor

Three lineages meet in the modern dashboard, and they do not agree.

The oldest is the French *tableau de bord*, a board of measures used by process engineers from the early twentieth century. The second is the executive information system of the 1980s — a computer screen giving a handful of senior people "easy access to internal and external information that is relevant to their critical success factors," in the words of a 1991 survey of fifty firms. Eckerson's history records what happened to it: it "never gained traction," because the systems "were geared to so few people in each company and were built on mainframes or minicomputers that made them costly to customize and maintain." The dashboard's direct ancestor failed once already, for reasons that had nothing to do with charts.

The third lineage is the Balanced Scorecard. In 1992 Robert Kaplan and David Norton asked managers to "think of the balanced scorecard as the dials and indicators in an airplane cockpit. For the complex task of navigating and flying an airplane, pilots need detailed information about many aspects of the flight … Reliance on one instrument can be fatal." That passage is widely reproduced; the original article is paywalled and the wording has not been checked against it here. Notice what the cockpit argues: *many instruments, or you die.* The car dashboard that Few and the software vendors reached for a decade later argues the opposite: *one glance, a few things, nothing you do not need.* Breadth against focus — most arguments about what to put on a dashboard are that collision replayed. Bach's workshop participants named the practical form of it ("can a dashboard ever contain too much information?") and the authors' answer was a four-way trade: reduce screen space, abstraction, the number of pages or the amount of interaction, and one of the others has to grow.

## 2 · Why people believe what they believe

A dashboard carries four beliefs with it, so widely held that they are rarely stated. Each has a respectable origin. Each is, on the evidence, only partly true. A reader who knows where each belief comes from can tell when it is being leaned on too hard.

### Belief one: you can understand a situation at a glance

The cognitive theory behind "monitored at a glance" is Mica Endsley's model of *situation awareness*, from aviation and air-traffic control. Few cited it himself: a dashboard's "effectiveness should be judged on the basis of its ability to help people monitor what's going on—that is, to maintain situation awareness." Endsley defined situation awareness as "the perception of the elements in the environment within a volume of time and space, the comprehension of their meaning, and the projection of their status in the near future." Three levels: *perceive* the elements, *comprehend* what they mean together, *project* what happens next. The 1995 paper is one of the most cited in human-factors research and also contested as a construct; the definition here is taken from Endsley's own later restatement, since the original is paywalled.

Read the three levels against a typical dashboard and a gap opens. A screen of current values delivers the first level. Comprehension — "the decision maker forms a holistic picture of the environment" — and projection are things a reader does, with a mental model the display cannot supply. The theory the field uses to justify the glance sets a bar the glance cannot meet on its own. This is where "what am I looking at?" comes from: a perception-level display shown to someone without the model to turn it into understanding.

### Belief two: what gets measured gets managed

The performance-management tradition — scorecards, targets, league tables, the city "stat" meetings that began in Baltimore — rests on the belief that making a thing visible makes it improve. The best evidence says it does, a little, and only when people do something with it. Ed Gerrish's 2016 meta-analysis of 49 studies found that "performance management has a small average effect. However, the effect is substantially larger when indicators of best practices in high-quality studies are included, indicating that management practices have an important impact." Robert Behn, who chronicled Baltimore's CitiStat programme more closely than anyone, wrote that "it is impossible to prove that CitiStat, by itself, produced all (or even most) of the improvements that Baltimore achieved," and located whatever worked in "an ongoing series of regular, frequent, periodic, integrated meetings" — "meeting, after meeting, after meeting." The display was the agenda. The meeting was the intervention.

The belief has a shadow, and it is the most reliably reproduced finding in this chapter. In 1997 the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern, writing about audit in British universities, put it in the form everyone now quotes: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." She credited the label — Goodhart's law — to a colleague, and the economist Charles Goodhart's own 1975 wording was about monetary statistics collapsing "once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes." Donald Campbell had said the social version first, in 1979: "The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor." Sociologists call the general phenomenon *reactivity*: "Measures are reactive; they cause people to think and act differently." A concrete case: after New York State began publishing surgeons' mortality rates, "reported congestive heart failure rose from 1.7% in 1989 to 7.6% in 1991" — a sicker-looking caseload makes a mortality rate look better. A dashboard does not observe a system from outside. It is inside the system, and the system learns it.

### Belief three: transparency produces trust

Public dashboards are built on the belief that showing people the numbers will make them trust the institution. The philosopher Onora O'Neill took this apart in her 2002 Reith Lectures, the BBC's annual public lecture series. "Transparency certainly destroys secrecy: but it may not limit the deception and deliberate misinformation that undermine relations of trust." Worse: "Transparency can encourage people to be less honest, so increasing deception and reducing reasons for trust." What builds trust, she argued, is being able to check: "Where we can check the information we receive, and when we can go back to those who put it into circulation, we may gain confidence about placing or refusing trust." Her phrase for the alternative is *intelligent accountability* — giving an account to "others who have sufficient time and experience to assess the evidence." This is philosophy, not measurement, but the UK's statistics regulator built its Code of Practice on it, and section 5 shows the survey evidence lining up behind it. For a dashboard the lesson is exact: a screen full of numbers nobody can check is transparency without trust. The data behind it, the method, and a route back to the people who made it are what turn one into the other.

### Belief four: the numbers are objective

The deepest belief is that a number is a neutral fact and a dashboard is a window onto it. The historian Theodore Porter's *Trust in Numbers* argues nearly the reverse: quantification is "a technology of distance," adopted where personal trust cannot reach, and "the regime of calculation was imposed not by all-powerful experts, but by relatively weak and divided ones." Numbers are what you reach for when you are not trusted. (Porter is quoted here from a published excerpt collection; the book itself is on the reading list for Chapter 5.) The geographer Rob Kitchin and colleagues made the same point about city dashboards directly, in the most-cited critical paper on the subject: indicators "do not simply act as a camera reflecting the world as it is, but rather act as an engine shaping the world in diverse ways," and the data in them "are never raw, but always already cooked." Their recommendation is one this book adopts as a rule: dashboard projects should "openly recognize their situatedness as the product of a complex data assemblage … signal their positionality and politics … fully document their lineage, data provenance, metadata, and levels of error and uncertainty."

Put the four beliefs together and a dashboard turns out to be a stranger object than a window. It shows perception-level facts to a reader who must supply the understanding; it changes the thing it shows; it may reduce trust while increasing transparency; and it is an argument made of numbers that someone chose. None of that is a reason not to build one. It is the reason to build one deliberately.

## 3 · Does it work? The evidence, sector by sector

The honest answer is: nobody knows very much, and what is known points in one direction.

### Hospitals — the best evidence there is

Clinical dashboards have been studied more carefully than any other kind. Dawn Dowding and colleagues' 2015 systematic review screened 122 papers and kept eleven, of which "one study was rated as being of high quality, four as being fair-good quality and five as low quality." Its clearest finding is about *access*, not design: "in contexts where dashboards were easily accessible to clinicians (such as in the form of a screen saver) their use was associated with improved care processes and patient outcomes." Where clinicians had to go and open the dashboard, "there was variability in whether or not clinicians used the information," and two studies "indicated no difference with dashboard use, because of the proportion of individuals who opted not to use them." The authors concluded that "it remains unclear exactly what characteristics of dashboards are related to improved outcomes."

Enrico Coiera and colleagues' 2025 review of hospital dashboards found seventy studies — sixty-one of them quasi-experimental, mostly before-and-after designs, six randomised trials, three observational — and graded most as at serious or moderate risk of bias. The pattern of results is instructive. Length of stay, 43 findings: "28 reporting a reduction, an increase in five, and ten reporting no change." Mortality, twenty findings: "five reported a decrease, whilst the majority (n = 15) found no significant change." Harms, 21 findings: "increases were observed in six, decreases in four, and no change in 11." Their summary: dashboards "appear associated with either no change or a reduction in mortality, reduced costs, reduced LOS [length of stay], and improved patient and caregiver satisfaction with care," while "association with harms was equivocal" and "actual impacts … will likely be dependent on multiple local factors such as workflow integration."

Two things to take from the best-studied field. The evidence base is mostly weaker designs reporting benefit — a shape that usually signals publication bias — so the fair summary is "probably modestly helpful in the right conditions, with harms unresolved," not "proven." And the one robust lesson is that *use* is the hinge: a dashboard that is unavoidable can help; a dashboard that must be opened often is not.

### Schools — the evidence that dashboards move the wrong thing

The learning-analytics field's own overview of its dashboards, by Katrien Verbert and colleagues in 2014, stated the promise: to "promote awareness, reflection, and sense-making, and to enable learners to define goals and track progress toward these goals." Four years later Ioana Jivet and colleagues reviewed the 26 dashboards that had actually been evaluated and found that "very few dashboard evaluations take into account the educational concepts that were used as a theoretical foundation for their design." Fourteen of the 26 were evaluated on whether people liked them. "Only one paper directly evaluated improvement in self-regulated learning although 13 papers listed supporting self-regulated learning as one of their goals." The dashboards were built to change learning and tested for satisfaction.

Rogers Kaliisa and colleagues' 2024 review of 38 studies then asked whether they had changed anything. Achievement: "most studies reported negligible or small effects"; "there is no evidence to support the conclusion that LADs [learning-analytics dashboards] have lived up to the promise of improving academic achievement." Participation — logins, posts, clicks — showed medium to large effects. That finding rests on nine of the 38 studies, five with medium or large effects against four small or negligible, and the authors say the larger effects were in almost all cases confounded with another intervention or uncontrolled; read with that caveat, it is the clearest signal in any field of a pattern that recurs through this chapter: **a dashboard moves the countable proxy before it moves the goal.** And one finding belongs beside it. Jivet's group had found earlier that "current designs foster competition between learners rather than knowledge mastery, offering misguided frames of reference for comparison" — the peer league table built in without anyone deciding to build it.

### Government — necessary, not sufficient

Ricardo Matheus, Marijn Janssen and Devender Maheshwari's study of two public open-data dashboards in Rio de Janeiro concluded that "dashboards can improve transparency and accountability, however, realizing these benefits was cumbersome and encountered various risks and challenges. Challenges include insufficient data quality, lack of understanding of data, poor analysis, wrong interpretation, confusion about the outcomes, and imposing a pre-defined view. These challenges can easily result in misconceptions, wrong decision-making, creating a blurred picture resulting in less transparency and accountability, and ultimately in even less trust in the government." Their remedy: "Dashboards need to be complemented by mechanisms supporting citizens' engagement, data interpretation, governance and institutional arrangements." Two qualitative cases, but the only careful ones found.

Targets with teeth produce harms of their own; Gwyn Bevan and Christopher Hood's account of the English health service in the 2000s — "a system of governance of public services that combined targets with an element of terror" — catalogues the gaming that followed. Only its abstract has been read for this chapter; the typology belongs in Chapter 7.

### Business — the graveyard nobody has counted

Everyone in business intelligence believes most dashboards go unused. This chapter looked for the number and could not find one that was independent. A 2023 analyst-firm survey found that "less than half of data and analytics (D&A) leaders (44%) reported that their team is effective in providing value" — self-report by the people whose budgets depend on the answer. The widely repeated "only 15% adoption" could not be traced to its supposed source. The 2012 review from the accounting field said what is still true: "despite dashboards' popularity, little is known about the extent of their effectiveness." A 2025 paper by Francesca Rossi and colleagues in implementation science put the general point best: "The dashboard's impact is dependent on its use by the individuals for whom it was designed. Yet, few studies measure dashboard use, and of those that do, their utility is limited." Of the studies they reviewed, "only one study examined dashboard use and indicated that few individuals (28%) used the dashboard at least once." The dashboard graveyard is universally believed and nowhere counted. **This book will not print a percentage for it.**

### What the evidence, taken together, says

Three findings recur. Two of them hold across every field examined; the third is demonstrated in one and echoed in the others.

First, **the evidence that dashboards change decisions or outcomes is thin, and where it exists it is mostly weaker designs reporting benefit.** Claims that dashboards are proven to work go beyond anything in the literature.

Second, **non-use is the dominant failure mode, and it is rarely measured.** Dashboards built for people who were never asked what they needed go unopened. The best remedy on record is a screen saver — being unavoidable — and the next best is being woven into a routine, a meeting, a workflow.

Third, **the countable proxy moves before the goal.** Shown directly in learning analytics (participation up, achievement flat), consistent with the small average effect in public performance management, and explained by the reactivity literature. A dashboard that is not watching for this will report success while nothing improves.

## 4 · The year everyone used one

In 2020 the whole world looked at the same kind of screen every day, and the episode is the richest recent evidence on what makes a public dashboard trusted and used.

The Johns Hopkins University dashboard reached a scale no public data display had reached before: "over 226 billion feature layer requests and 3·6 billion page views" in thirty months, with a single-day peak of "more than 4·6 billion requests and over 69 million views." Its builders' account of what held it together is about data curation, not charts: "the anomaly detection service is crucial to the data curation process," with "mandatory manual reviews" when a figure jumped; open data on GitHub; public corrections. And one detail that should give every dashboard builder pause: their scrapers had to handle "more than 400 sources," some of which published their numbers "in inaccessible formats, such as infographics and business intelligence reporting frontends." Other people's dashboards were an obstacle to data. **A dashboard that is not also a data release is, to anyone downstream, a wall.**

Damir Ivanković and a European team appraised 158 public COVID dashboards for *actionability* — fitness for the decisions their readers faced. "In total, 20/158 dashboards (12.7%) were appraised as highly actionable." One in eight, in the one year when dashboards had everyone's attention and resources. The seven features the twenty shared are the nearest thing the field has to a validated checklist: they know their audience and information needs; manage the type, volume and flow of displayed information; report data sources and methods clearly; link time trends to policy decisions; provide data that are "close to home"; break down the population into relevant subgroups; and use storytelling and visual cues. Two of the seven are about provenance and context, not display. The study measured features, not decisions taken.

Erica Barbazza's study of 33 national dashboard teams found something that explains the other seven in eight: "Above all, the dashboards targeted the general public, though no dashboard team described having direct or formal contact with the public in the early development stage," and "monitoring user analytics was not pursued for the common reason: 'there wasn't time for deep analysis of user behaviour.'" Built for the public; built without meeting the public; never checked for use.

The UK's dashboard is the counter-example, and its team wrote up what they did. They asked: "We recently posted a survey on the dashboard asking for feedback, and within 48 hours we had 17,000 responses." They were used: "over 300,000 unique users every day," "around 10,000 people online refreshing the page" at four o'clock, "a record 76.5 million hits in just 24 hours" at the peak of the third lockdown, and "about 19 million page views across the site, many of them postcode searches on the interactive map." They were trusted: "an outstandingly high level of user trust in the dashboard data at 90.3%, up from … 73.4%" — a self-selected user survey, so strong as a signal and weak as a population estimate. And look at *what they credit*. A fixed daily time. Simultaneous release: "the latest data are always available to members of the public at the same time they are seen in Downing Street." Everything downloadable through an interface that the Office for Statistics Regulation called "an exemplar for the provision of open data across government." Postcode-level data — "close to home." Relentless user surveying. A later national survey found the dashboard's followers "varied less between groups categorised by age, qualification and economic activity" than users of statistics usually do. The team's blogs barely mention the charts. The trust came from conduct.

## 5 · What makes a number trusted

The United Kingdom has, unusually, a written answer to this question, and it is built on O'Neill. The Code of Practice for Statistics rests on three pillars: **Trustworthiness** — "confidence in the people and organisations that produce statistics and data"; **Quality** — "data and methods that produce assured statistics"; **Value** — "statistics that support society's needs for information." The Code's own gloss: "Trustworthiness is a product of the people, systems and processes within organisations that enable and support the production of statistics and data." It binds official statistics; anyone else adopts it voluntarily, and this book does. The regulator's staff say the same in plainer words: "you can't force someone to trust you. You can only show through the way you behave … that you are honest, reliable, and competent."

The survey evidence is consistent across a decade. In the 2023 Public Confidence in Official Statistics survey, run by the National Centre for Social Research for the UK Statistics Authority, 85% of people who expressed a view said they trusted the statistics the Office for National Statistics (ONS) produces, and 72% agreed they are "free from political interference" — while only 32% agreed that "the government presents official statistics honestly." In the 2014 wave the like-for-like figures were 81% trusting ONS statistics and 28% trusting the government's presentation of them. The same numbers, trusted when the producer holds them and distrusted when a presenter does. **Trust attaches to the producer and its conduct, and detaches from the presenter.** For a dashboard this means the question "do people trust it?" is really "do people trust *us*, and can they check?"

One more finding undoes a widespread fear. Builders often hide uncertainty on the grounds that ranges and caveats make people trust the number less. Anne Marthe van der Bles, David Spiegelhalter and colleagues ran five experiments with 5,780 people — including a preregistered replication and a field study on the BBC News website — and found "only a small decrease in trust in numbers and trustworthiness of the source, and mostly for verbal" uncertainty. Numeric ranges barely dented trust; vague verbal hedges did. Show the range as a number. Do not write "around" and hope.

The Open Data Institute adds a hierarchy worth keeping: privacy and security at the base; ethics and transparency above it; engagement and accountability above that; equity and fairness at the top — "gaining trust requires organisations to go above and beyond good data governance practices." Each layer is a thing an organisation does, not a thing a screen shows.

## 6 · The arithmetic of the biggest

The eighth of the brief's failures, listed in section 8 — "an aggregation of the biggest" — is the one that most needs to be stated as mathematics rather than as a grievance, because it is one. Nothing in this section is contested among statisticians. Most dashboards are built as if none of it were true.

### The same numbers can say anything

In 1984 the geographer Stan Openshaw took one dataset — the percentage voting Republican and the percentage of the population over sixty in each of Iowa's 99 counties — and regrouped the counties into zones. At the county level the correlation between the two was 0.34. When he grouped the 99 counties into six zones in different ways, "the range of possible correlations is between -.99 and +.99." Same 99 numbers; any answer you like, depending on where you draw the lines. His name for this is the *modifiable areal unit problem*, and his verdict was blunt: "There are no rules for areal aggregation, no standards, and no international conventions to guide the spatial aggregation process. Quite simply, the areal units (zonal objects) used in many geographical studies are arbitrary, modifiable, and subject to the whims and fancies of whoever is doing, or did, the aggregating." It has two parts — the *scale* effect (how big the units are) and the *zoning* effect (where the borders fall at a given size) — and a 2025 paper in political science still finds that results "change in wildly unpredictable ways" with the choice of unit. Openshaw's own wry remark is the one to remember: "The simplest solution to the MAUP is to pretend it does not exist…" — and that is still the default. The consequence for any place-based dashboard is a rule, not a caution: **the unit of aggregation is a decision, it changes the answer, and it must be shown and justified.** A ward map, a local-authority map and a postcode-district map of the same thing are three different arguments.

### The area is not the person — and the person is not the area

In 1950 the sociologist William Robinson showed that across the nine US census divisions, the correlation between being foreign-born and being illiterate was –0.62 — divisions with more immigrants had *less* illiteracy — while at the level of individuals it was +0.12: immigrants were somewhat more likely to be illiterate, but they had settled where the native-born were more literate. His conclusion about using area correlations to stand in for individual ones: "They cannot." This is the *ecological fallacy*, and every dashboard that colours an area and lets the reader infer something about the people in it is standing on it.

But the modern reading of Robinson is the one this book needs. When S. V. Subramanian and colleagues re-analysed his data in 2009, they found that the area was not noise around the individuals: states with legalised segregation had higher illiteracy among both Black and white residents, with a stronger effect on Black residents. The context had a real effect of its own. Their conclusion: "perils are posed by not only ecological fallacy but also individualistic fallacy. Multilevel thinking, grounded in historical and spatiotemporal context, is thus a necessity, not an option." The mirror-image error — inferring that a place has no quality of its own because its people vary — has a name too: the *atomistic fallacy*. **A place-level figure can be information about the place, not just an average of the people in it.** For anything that wants to measure what a place is like rather than who lives there, that is the sentence to build on.

### A ranking can reverse when you look inside it

Simpson's paradox is the third fact, and the one that most directly threatens league tables. The classic case: at Berkeley in 1973, "about 44 percent of the males and about 35 percent of the females were admitted" — apparent bias against women — but within departments there was "a small but statistically significant bias in favor of women." The reason: "the proportion of women applicants tends to be high in departments that are hard to get into and low in those that are easy to get into." The aggregate reversed the truth because the mix differed. A medical example makes the same point: one kidney-stone treatment looked better overall (83% against 78%) and was worse for small stones *and* for large ones, because it had been used disproportionately on the easier cases. For a dashboard: **any ordered comparison of units with different mixes — of venue size, of genre, of audience — can reverse when the mix is controlled, and the display must either control it or say it has not.**

### What the index-builders' own handbook admits

The OECD and the European Commission's Joint Research Centre wrote the standard handbook on composite indicators in 2008 — the method behind most "indexes" that rank places. It opens with the warning that "composite indicators can send misleading policy messages if they are poorly constructed or misinterpreted," and then concedes, in print, what this chapter needs. On size: "To have an objective comparison across small and large countries, scaling of variables by an appropriate size measure, e.g. population, income, trade volume, and populated land area, etc. is required." Unscaled sums are won by the big. On the choices that follow: ranking "is not affected by outliers" but "information on levels is lost"; with standard scores "indicators with extreme values thus have a greater effect on the composite indicator." Every way of putting things on a common scale treats the outlier differently. And the sentence that should be pinned above every index: "In both linear and geometric aggregations, weights express trade-offs between indicators. This implies an inconsistency between how weights are conceived (usually measuring the importance of the associated variable) and the actual meaning…" The weights published as "importance" are, mathematically, exchange rates. Chapter 5 builds on all of this; here the point is only that the mechanism by which the biggest wins is documented by the people who build the indexes.

### Small numbers sit at both ends of every ranking

Howard Wainer called the standard error of the mean — de Moivre's equation, the rule that variability shrinks with the square root of sample size — "the most dangerous equation," because ignorance of it has cost billions. The rate in a small place swings far more than the rate in a large one, for no reason but size. His examples: US counties in the lowest tenth for kidney-cancer deaths "tend to be very rural, Midwestern, Southern and Western" — and so do the counties in the *highest* tenth. "A county with, say, 100 inhabitants that has no cancer deaths would be in the lowest category. But if it has 1 cancer death it would be among the highest." In Pennsylvania's schools, "of the top-scoring 50 schools (the top 3 percent) six were among the smallest 3 percent of the schools" — and of the fifty lowest-scoring, "nine of these (18 percent) were among the 50 smallest schools." Across all schools "the regression line … is essentially flat." Small schools were over-represented at the top because they were small, and a foundation that had "given grants to education projects totaling approximately $1.7 billion" on the theory that small schools are better had, in Wainer's words, spent "more than a billion dollars on a theory based on ignorance of de Moivre's equation."

The obvious fix has its own trap. Statisticians "shrink" small-area estimates toward the overall average to tame the noise, and Andrew Gelman and Phillip Price showed what that does: after shrinkage, "the highest adjusted rates tend to be found disproportionately in well-sampled areas." Raw maps put the extremes in the small places; smoothed maps move them to the big ones. Their title is literal: "All maps of parameter estimates are misleading." There is no setting of the dial that makes a ranking by rate honest. **The honest display shows the uncertainty, not a point.**

The Royal Statistical Society's own verdict on league tables, in Harvey Goldstein and David Spiegelhalter's 1996 discussion paper that is still the reference, is the rule this chapter adopts: in the school example they analysed, "about two-thirds of all possible comparisons do not allow separation," and so "we can use such rankings as screening instruments, but not as definitive judgments on individual institutions." A league table may tell you where to look. It cannot tell you what you will find.

### Official statistics are designed to blank the small cells

The last fact bites hardest for anyone trying to see a small scene. Statistical offices suppress or disturb small counts, for two reasons given in one breath: reliability and confidentiality. The US Centers for Disease Control's cancer registry does not show "statistics based on counts below 16." The ONS's standard rule for published tables is the "10-5 rule" — suppress below ten, round to the nearest five above. The 2021 Census swapped rare records between areas and added noise to "around 14% of cell counts," so that "where there is a cell count of one in a published output, there is a high chance that it could be a swapped record, a perturbed count or an imputed record." And the technique used to fill the gaps, small-area estimation, "will tend to shrink estimates towards the average level … so estimates at each end of the scales tend to be over- or under-estimated." The design of official statistics removes, by rule, precisely the small cells where a local reader would look for the interesting case — not from malice but from method. Any dashboard built only from official sources will be blind in exactly the places Carly's brief cares about, and must say so rather than show a blank. This is the statistical root of a problem that Chapters 4, 6 and 10 each inherit a piece of: a disclosure threshold decides, before any screen is drawn, how many people a scene must contain before it can be seen.

### Why an outlier can be the point

Put the facts together and the outlier changes character. The textbook definition — an observation "which deviates so much from other observations as to arouse suspicions that it was generated by a different mechanism" — is, read carefully, an invitation: *a different mechanism* is a different story, and a story is what a reader came for. John Tukey's founding attitude for exploratory data analysis was that "the greatest value of a picture is when it forces us to notice what we never expected to see." The discipline of anomaly detection exists because "anomalies in data translate to significant (and often critical) actionable information" — in fraud, in intrusion, in machine failure. And there is a method that makes the favourable outlier its object of study: *positive deviance*, which "enables communities to discover the wisdom they already have, and then to act on it." In its founding case, in Vietnamese villages in 1990, the families whose children were well-nourished on the same resources as everyone else turned out to be adding sweet-potato greens and paddy crabs to meals, feeding children "three to four times a day, rather than the customary two," and washing hands first; the practitioners report that malnutrition fell by 85% in the pilot villages — their own figure, uncontrolled, and to be quoted as such. The analytical move is the one Carly describes in words: the small venue that holds a scene together is not noise in the distribution of venues; it is a different mechanism, and the question is what it is doing right. A positive-deviance analysis on the Vibe Map's own data is one of the clearest early experiments available.

## 7 · What a dashboard screens out

The critical scholarship on quantification is not hostile to measurement; it is precise about what measurement does to the thing measured, and a builder who knows it can design against it.

James C. Scott, writing about states rather than dashboards, described the state's project as making a society "legible" — simplified so it can be taxed, counted and governed — and his description of the resulting documents is a description of a dashboard written before the word meant a screen: "These state simplifications … were, I began to realize, rather like abridged maps. They did not successfully represent the actual activity of the society they depicted, nor were they intended to; they represented only that slice of it that interested the official observer." And then: "they were maps that, when allied with state power, would enable much of the reality they depicted to be remade." The map reshapes the territory. Against the abridged map Scott sets *mētis* — "a wide array of practical skills and acquired intelligence in responding to a constantly changing natural and human environment" — the knowledge that "resists simplification into deductive principles." His conclusion is not that the map is useless but that "the formal scheme was parasitic on informal processes that, alone, it could not create or maintain." A dashboard lives on the knowledge it cannot hold.

Wendy Espeland and Mitchell Stevens named the operation at the centre of every index: *commensuration*, "the valuation or measuring of different objects with a common metric," which "transforms all difference into quantity." To put two scenes on one axis you must first decide they are the same kind of thing. Sally Engle Merry added two mechanisms that explain what ends up on a screen. *Data inertia*: "existing data determine what an indicator can measure." *Expertise inertia*: "insiders with skills and experience have a greater say in developing measurement systems than those without." A dashboard built from what already exists as data, by the people who already hold data, will measure what those people already measured. That is not a conspiracy; it is gravity, and climbing out of it takes deliberate work.

Sarikaya's paper cites a case, from an earlier project by O'Connell and colleagues, that shows the gravity working. A community-safety dashboard, in moving to a data-driven representation, "refocused the conversation" from "an asset-based model of neighborhood health (social cohesion, resilience)" to "a deficit-based model (crime, code violations)" — because the deficits were what the data held. Nobody decided to describe the neighbourhood by its problems. The available columns decided.

Catherine D'Ignazio and Lauren Klein's *Data Feminism* supplies the principle and the warning. The principle, from the geographer Joni Seager: "what gets counted counts" — "what is counted … often becomes the basis for policymaking and resource allocation." The warning is "the paradox of exposure: the double bind that places those who stand to significantly gain from being counted in the most danger from that same counting (or classifying) act," and its consequence: "sometimes the most ethical thing to do is to help people be obscure, hidden, and invisible." D'Ignazio's later work names the alternative to being counted by someone else: *counterdata* — "data production, analysis, visualization and circulation of data that happens outside of – and often in opposition to – mainstream counting institutions." A dashboard that a community builds about itself is a different object from one built about it, and the absence of a number is itself a datum about who was counting.

The culture sector has been through this argument already and left a record. The Arts and Humanities Research Council's Cultural Value Project concluded that "the instrumental arguments for the arts were mostly about economic or social objectives that could be achieved by other means" and that "instead of 'measuring' it is more appropriate to speak of 'evaluation', of which measurement is but one component." Eleonora Belfiore's account of the sector's "impact" numbers borrows the economist John Kay's line — "Bad economics here, as so often, involves inventing bogus numbers to answer badly formulated questions" — and asks for "a more open, honest and indeed humble debate around value." This is Porter's thesis in miniature: a sector that feels distrusted produces numbers for advocacy, and numbers for advocacy are not the same as numbers for understanding. A nightlife evidence base that wants to be trusted by the committee room has to be built as the second kind, even when it is used as the first.

### The idea that makes several readers possible

One more concept, and it is the most constructive in the chapter. In 1989 Susan Leigh Star and James Griesemer studied how amateurs and professionals managed to work together on a natural-history museum without agreeing about what they were doing, and named the things that made it possible *boundary objects*: "objects which are both plastic enough to adapt to local needs and the constraints of the several parties employing them, yet robust enough to maintain a common identity across sites. They are weakly structured in common use, and become strongly structured in individual-site use. … They have different meanings in different social worlds but their structure is common enough to more than one world to make them recognizable, a means of translation." One of their four types is the *ideal type*: "an object such as a diagram, atlas or other description which in fact does not accurately describe the details of any one locality or thing. It is abstracted from all domains, and may be fairly vague. However, it is adaptable to a local site precisely because it is fairly vague." Vagueness, in this theory, is a feature — which runs directly against the dashboard ideal of precision, and is worth sitting with.

A dashboard that must serve a council officer, a promoter, a funder and a scene at once is attempting to be a boundary object, and the theory says two things about that. First, it does not require everyone to agree what the numbers mean; it requires a structure each can read in their own way — which is why Chapter 12's insistence that a scene recognises its place is not sentiment but a condition of the object working. Second, from Esther Turnhout's 2009 study of ecological indicators: the property is not designed in. Their effectiveness as boundary objects, "rather than a characteristic of the indicator itself, … is a social achievement, which depends on a common culture of shared values and preferences." The shared display is downstream of the shared community. You cannot build the common ground with the dashboard; you build the dashboard on common ground that already exists — the strongest argument in this book for the project's insistence on convening and co-design before screens.

## 9 · What holds true regardless

The candidate canonical truths this chapter contributes, each with its kind and firmness. Later chapters test them; the closing chapter confirms or demotes them.

1. **A dashboard is an argument, not a window.** It translates; it does not mirror; through reactivity it reshapes what it shows. *Reading of strong scholarship (Kitchin; Espeland & Stevens; Scott). Fixed (requirement) in its design consequence — document provenance, positionality and uncertainty on the face.*
2. **Purpose and reader come before pixels.** A display built for nobody serves nobody. *Evidence (Ivanković; Jivet; Barbazza). Fixed (sequence).*
3. **The evidence that dashboards work is thin, and depends on use and context.** *Evidence (four systematic reviews across sectors; one meta-analysis). Fixed (statement of the record).*
4. **Non-use is the dominant failure mode.** Use must be designed and measured. *Evidence (Dowding; Rossi; Behn). Fixed (requirement).*
5. **The countable proxy moves before the goal.** *Evidence (Jivet; Kaliisa; Gerrish) and the reactivity literature. Fixed (sequence).*
6. **A published measure changes the behaviour it measures.** *Evidence and reading (Strathern; Campbell; Goldstein & Spiegelhalter). Fixed (sequence).*
7. **Trust attaches to the producer and its conduct, not to the chart.** Transparency without checkability does not produce trust. *Standard (the Code) and evidence (the confidence surveys; O'Neill). Fixed (requirement).*
8. **Shown as a number, uncertainty does not cost trust.** Vague hedging does. *Evidence (van der Bles; preregistered and replicated). Fixed (requirement).*
9. **The unit of aggregation is a decision that changes the answer.** *Evidence (Openshaw; Lee et al.). Fixed (requirement).*
10. **A place-level figure can be information about the place, not just an average of its people.** Both the ecological and the atomistic fallacy are real. *Evidence (Robinson; Subramanian et al.). Fixed (statement of the record).*
11. **Small numbers sit at both ends of every ranking, and no smoothing makes a rate ranking honest.** Show uncertainty; treat rankings as screens. *Evidence (Wainer; Gelman & Price; Goldstein & Spiegelhalter). Fixed (requirement).*
12. **Official statistics blank out small cells by design.** A dashboard built on them is blind where small scenes live, and must say so. *Standard (ONS, CDC practice). Fixed (requirement).*
13. **What gets counted counts; what exists as data gets measured.** *Reading (Seager via D'Ignazio & Klein; Merry). Fixed as a design risk to be worked against.*
14. **An outlier is a candidate mechanism, not an error.** *Reading (Hawkins; Tukey; positive deviance). Adaptable: a default stance, applied with judgement.*
15. **A display that serves several communities does so as a boundary object — by shared structure, not shared meaning — and that property is a social achievement, not a design feature.** *Reading (Star & Griesemer; Turnhout). Adaptable in application; Fixed (sequence) as the reason co-design precedes screens.*
16. **A dashboard that is not also a data release is a wall.** *Evidence (Dong et al.) and standard (the regulator on the UK interface). Fixed (requirement) for any public-interest dashboard.*

## 10 · Where the choice is yours

Everything above constrains; none of it decides. The choices this chapter leaves open, with their trade-offs:

**Which job.** Monitoring, analysis or communication — Few's, Eckerson's or the Finnish managers' dashboard. The evidence says most real dashboards are used to communicate and align, and that monitoring displays nobody is obliged to watch go unwatched. A public-interest dashboard is realistically a communication and analysis object with a monitoring heartbeat; that is a position, not a law. *Adaptable.*

**One screen or many layers.** The cockpit says breadth; the car says focus; Bach says you pay somewhere. The choice depends on the reader and the decision, and Chapter 11 returns to it with evidence about first screens. *Adaptable.*

**How much to frame.** Every dashboard frames; the question is whether to author openly — a sentence, a story, a stated position — or to present a configurable surface and let the reader frame. The 2020 panel's "storytelling and visual cues" and Kitchin's "signal their positionality" both argue for authored framing, honestly labelled. *Adaptable.*

**Which failures to prevent first.** All eight cannot be prevented at once with equal force. The evidence ranks them: non-use and distrust kill a dashboard outright; aggregation and non-recognition kill its truth; the others degrade it. *Adaptable — and the order is a Nightlife Culture decision.*

**How much mud to let in.** A dashboard that admits uncertainty, gaps, uncounted things and the knowledge it cannot hold is less tidy and more honest. Where to set that dial is a judgement about the reader. *Adaptable; the floor — never a blank where there is a reason — is Fixed.*

## 11 · What this means for a nightlife dashboard

*An illustration, not a specification.*

The Vibe Map's funding deck describes it as "a living, interactive map of the people, activity, value and support systems that sustain a place's nightlife culture," to be "recognisable to the scene," "designed to invite understanding," and "shared without flattening difference." It names three groups who will test it — policymakers and politicians; advocates and trade bodies; funders and investors — with the artists, DJs, promoters, crews and venues who make the night as co-developers. Read those three phrases through this chapter.

*Recognisable to the scene* is the boundary-object condition. A display that must serve an officer, a promoter, a funder and the dancefloor works, if it works, through a structure each can read in their own way, built on common ground that exists before the screen does. The project's months of convening are not a preamble to the dashboard; on this chapter's evidence they are what makes one possible.

*Designed to invite understanding* is Endsley's second and third levels — comprehension and projection — which a display of current values cannot supply alone. Whatever the Vibe Map shows first will need the sentence, the story and the comparison that turn perception into understanding.

*Shared without flattening difference* is commensuration, named and resisted. Every time two scenes are placed on one axis, difference becomes quantity; the rule is that the axis is chosen openly, the unit of aggregation is shown, and a scene's identity is never the thing ranked. The suburban hub that holds a music community together is, in this chapter's language, an outlier generated by a different mechanism — and the method that studies it rather than trimming it already has a name.

Three warnings travel with the illustration. The paradox of exposure: a scene that stands to gain from being counted is also put at risk by it, and the choice to be visible must be the scene's own (Chapter 10). Data inertia: the datasets already in hand will pull the map toward what they already measure, and the value column will stay empty unless its emptiness is designed as a finding (Chapter 4). And trust: a studio's dashboard earns trust by conduct — fixed release, open data, visible method, a route back to the makers — not by the beauty of its charts. The club flyer can do what it does on top of that, not instead of it.

## What proves a break works

If you must build a dashboard that breaks one of the Fixed rules above — a ranking without intervals, a display without a data release, a measure that is not scaled — the test is the same in every case: **find the person the rule protects and show them.** Give the ranking to someone at the bottom of it and ask if it is fair. Give the display to someone who needs the data underneath and ask them to get it. Give the unscaled measure to the smallest unit in it and ask whether they can see themselves. If the break survives that, log it in the deviation entry Chapter 16 describes. If it does not, it was not a break; it was a mistake.

## What this chapter could not settle

- No independent measurement exists of how many dashboards go unused. Interim position: refuse to print a percentage; state the gap as a rule.
- No study found on staleness, what happens when a dashboard stops changing. Interim position: inferred from monitoring theory and the non-use literature.

## Checklist

- [ ] The reader is named and the decision they will make is written down
- [ ] The job — monitor, analyse, communicate — is stated and the design matches it
- [ ] Provenance, method, positionality and uncertainty are on the face
- [ ] The data is released with the display, through a documented route
- [ ] Uncertainty is shown as numbers
- [ ] Use is measured from the first day
- [ ] The goal is measured, or the proxy's drift from it is watched
- [ ] The unit of aggregation is shown, justified and sensitivity-tested
- [ ] Size-dependent measures are scaled and the denominator named
- [ ] No ranking by rate without intervals; none presented as a verdict
- [ ] Suppressed or perturbed cells say so; no blanks
- [ ] Outliers investigated before trimmed
- [ ] Trust mechanisms are conduct: release time, open data, visible method, a route back
- [ ] The communities who will read it were met before it was designed, and recognition tested
