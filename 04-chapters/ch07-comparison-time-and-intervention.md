## The question

**When a dashboard sets one thing beside another — this place beside that one, this month beside last, the year before a change beside the year after, what is beside what might be — what does each comparison show, what does it hide, and what may be said from it?**

---

## The 60-second version

> Every comparison is a claim that two things are alike in everything but the thing compared. Chapter 4 asked for that claim to be declared; this chapter is about the four comparisons a dashboard makes — and a fifth discipline, reading the borrowed indices of a market — and what each one's declaration must contain.
>
> **Place against place.** A ranking is a measure that changes what it measures — rankings "do not simply reflect an underlying social hierarchy but in fact play a crucial role in creating this hierarchy" — and a noisy one: in the standard analysis "about two thirds of all possible comparisons do not allow separation", and a clinic's rank can move substantially between years "even though their live birth rate did not change significantly". The World Bank's own review of its *Doing Business* index recommended "Remove the aggregate index and country rankings", and a UK publisher of a standardised mortality ratio tells users "It is inappropriate to rank trusts according to their SHMI." The alternative has a name and a shape: the funnel plot, which "avoid[s] spurious ranking of institutions into 'league tables'" † by plotting each place against its own precision. Comparing like with like needs standardisation — a ratio of observed to expected — and peers chosen by a published method, because in the one field where self-chosen comparators have been measured, executive pay, "peer groups are constructed in a manner that biases compensation upward". †
>
> **This period against last.** A series has a trend, a season and a remainder, and every "underlying trend" line is a smoothing someone chose. Seasonal adjustment is a model whose factors "are estimates based on present and past experience", revised when "just one additional observation" arrives; a series under three years "cannot be seasonally adjusted"; and comparing this month with the same month last year "does not completely remove all seasonal effects" and is slow at turning points. A year-on-year rate can move because of what happened twelve months ago. Regression to the mean "can make natural variation in repeated data look like real change" and "should always be considered as a possible cause of an observed change". † An index is change relative to a reference date the producer calls "arbitrary", and the textbook rules of thumb for how many observations make a trend readable "are misleading and unsubstantiated in theory or practice".
>
> **Before against after.** The one-group before-and-after comparison was introduced in 1963 "as a 'bad example'", with eight named rivals to the explanation that the intervention did it. The designs that do better each add a comparison: a pre-trend projected forward (interrupted time series); a comparison group whose trend is assumed parallel — an assumption that "cannot be tested because it involves a non-existent counterfactual that cannot be observed" — whose pooled estimate misleads when places adopt at different times and effects change over time; a weighted blend of other places (synthetic control), which "preclude[s] extrapolation" and makes "explicit the contribution of each comparison unit"; a cut-off; a match on what was recorded. Each has stated requirements and a rung on a published ladder on which plain before/after is the bottom and the floor for counting as evidence is a comparison group; the stronger ones have a placebo test. Each rung has its verb: *after* for a before/after; *departed from its trend* for an interrupted series; *contributed to* for a comparison-group design with a tested theory of change; *caused* only for the designs that earn it. Spillovers and displacement are the ripples, and "Seeing evidence of growth in the zone itself … tells us very little if that activity is simply displaced from elsewhere". A small effect in a noisy series from few places cannot be seen at all.
>
> **What is against what might be.** The UK's appraisal rules say how such a claim is stated: adjust for optimism bias — "the demonstrated systematic tendency for practitioners to be over-optimistic" — by the organisation's own record of forecast against outturn; give "a central estimate and a range"; calculate the switching value at which the conclusion flips; run scenarios, and (the cross-government uncertainty toolkit adds) an even number of them. The best-known uncertainty display in British official life, the Bank of England's fan chart, was built "without suggesting a degree of precision that would be spurious"; the Office for Budget Responsibility's version carries a note that its width "does not take into account the current level of uncertainty"; and the Bank's was recommended for removal in April 2024 as having "outlived their usefulness" and had gone from its reports by April 2026, replaced by named scenarios. Communicating a numerical range costs little trust; vague words cost more, and people hear "very likely" as very different numbers even with the key in front of them. "Projections are not forecasts." Nowcasting predicts the present, not the future, and its best-known failure overshot by more than double. "Leading" is a word earned by a past record of turning points, hedged "Historically" and "generally".
>
> So the canonical truths: a comparison declares what it holds equal, and who chose the comparators; a rank is a noisy summary and position is shown within a distribution with its interval; like is compared with like by standardisation and by peers chosen by a published method; change is read against the series' own season and history, with the adjustment named as a model; after is not because, and the counterfactual earns the verb on a graded ladder; a claim about the future is a range with its assumptions, its track record and its switching value; and a market movement is an index whose base, adjustment and coverage are declared. Beneath every comparison sits a *comparison record*, and the line a dashboard cannot fill is written as "not done", visibly.

## 1 · What a comparison is

A number on its own says almost nothing; Chapter 4 showed that it is undefined until its unit, period and base are stated. A comparison is where a dashboard begins to say something — *more than*, *less than*, *up*, *down*, *better*, *worse*, *because* — and every one of those words rests on a claim the reader does not usually see: that the two things set side by side are alike in everything except the thing being compared. Chapter 4 called the statement of that claim the comparability declaration and gave it ten lines. This chapter is about the four comparisons a dashboard makes and what each one's declaration must contain to be true.

The four are these. *Place against place*: this district beside that one, this venue beside its peers, this city in a table of cities. *This period against last*: this month against the month before, or the same month a year ago, or the trend. *Before against after*: the years before a change — a licence, a fund, a festival, a road — beside the years after it, which is the comparison that most wants to say "because". And *what is against what might be*: the projection, the scenario, the "if this policy then that benefit", which compares a measured present with an imagined future. Each has a literature, and the literatures agree on one thing before they disagree on anything: the comparison is only as good as the thing held equal, and the thing held equal is almost never equal.

A fifth kind runs through all four — reading market movement, the indices and prices and counts that dashboards borrow from the economy's own instruments — and section 10 treats it as a discipline of its own, because an index carries a base and an adjustment that are the comparison's hidden terms.

The chapter does not decide which comparisons the Vibe Map should make. It sets out what each one may claim and what it must declare, so that when the studio and its designers choose, they choose with the method in view.

## 2 · Why people believe what they believe

Eight beliefs sit under most comparisons on most dashboards. Each has a reason to exist and a literature that tests it; the sections that follow give the evidence, and this section only names the beliefs so that the reader can see which ones they hold.

### Belief one: a ranking is information

A list of places from best to worst looks like the most informative display there is: every place has a position, and the positions can be read at a glance. The literature on rankings finds two things against this. The ranked change to fit the rank, so that the ranking becomes partly a measure of itself; and at small differences a rank is mostly noise, so that most of the positions in the list cannot be told apart. Section 3.

### Belief two: the top of the table is the best

If a ranking is information, its top is where the best place is. Chapter 6 found the first problem: small places sit at both tails because their rates swing most. This chapter finds the second: in a relative ranking a place can improve and fall, if others improved faster, and a place can rise while nothing about it changed, if the method changed. Section 3.

### Belief three: like is being compared with like

Two districts, two numbers, one is higher. The comparison assumes the districts are alike in whatever drives the number apart from the thing being compared — age, size, wealth, the mix of what is there. The public-health literature has a method for this, standardisation, and a rule that goes with it: a figure standardised against a national expectation is compared with the expectation, not with another standardised figure. The official methods for choosing similar areas are published, and the one commercial model most used in local government says similarity "can be a subjective process". Section 4.

### Belief four: last month is the baseline

This month against last is the simplest comparison a dashboard can make, and Chapter 6's finding was that it "tells you nothing about trends and how much the data varies naturally". This chapter adds the seasonal problem: last month may be a different season, this month against the same month last year carries last year's weather and holidays, and the adjusted series that fixes both is a model that is revised when new data arrive. Section 6.

### Belief five: the trend line is the truth under the noise

A smoothed line looks like the real thing with the mess removed. It is one smoothing among several, its smoothness is a parameter someone set, it is least reliable at its most recent point, and the two official traditions disagree about whether to read turning points from it. Section 6.

### Belief six: after means because

The change happened; the number moved; the change did it. The 1963 text that founded the study of quasi-experiments called this design a "bad example" and listed eight other things that could have moved the number. The designs that do better all add a comparison, and each comparison carries an assumption that cannot be tested from the data alone. Section 7 and 8.

### Belief seven: a forecast is a number

"Spend will rise 12%." The official forecasters who are held to account for their numbers publish them as ranges and distributions, evaluate them against outturn every year, and in one case have abandoned their most famous uncertainty display for named scenarios. Section 9.

### Belief eight: an index measures the thing it is named for

A footfall index, a price index, a leading indicator. Each is change relative to a base period someone chose, adjusted or not by a method someone chose, built from the coverage Chapter 4 warned about, and "leading" is a description of a past record. Section 10.

## 3 · What a ranking does

### The ranked change to fit the rank

The sociological account of rankings begins with a word borrowed from methodology. Espeland and Sauder's 2007 study of law-school rankings defined reactivity as "the idea that people change their behavior in reaction to being evaluated, observed, or measured", and proposed "a framework for investigating the consequences, both intended and unintended, of public measures". Their article "first identifies two mechanisms, self‐fulfilling prophecy and commensuration, that induce reactivity". † (The dagger marks a source read at the authors' abstract or summary only, not its full text.) The body of that article was not reached; their later book, of which the first chapter is open, states the finding plainly. In the case of law-school rankings, "reactivity" refers to the fact that "the measures do not simply reflect an underlying social hierarchy but in fact play a crucial role in creating this hierarchy". "Because people are reflective and reflexive, they tend to react to being measured in unanticipated ways." "People scrutinize rankings, worry over them, invest in them, and act differently because of them, and these behaviors change the institutions that rankings evaluate." And the reason rankings in particular do this: "One reason that rankings generate such extensive change is that they override the strategies organizations normally use to manage external pressures." Commensuration, the second mechanism, is defined in Espeland and Stevens's earlier review as "the comparison of different entities according to a common metric" † — which is what any single score across places does.

Sauder and Espeland's 2009 paper, reached at its abstract, describes the mechanism from the inside: "Members' tendency to internalize these pressures, to become self-disciplining, is also salient", and "Internalization is fostered by the anxiety that rankings produce, by their allure for the administrators who try to manipulate them, and by the resistance they provoke." † Chapter 5 found, through Campbell and through Bevan and Hood, that a measure used for decisions changes what it measures. A ranking is the strongest case, because it adds position to measurement, and position is zero-sum.

### A rank can fall while the place improves

The arithmetic of a relative rank is stated most plainly by the publisher of the English Indices of Deprivation, in its guidance on comparing editions. "The IMD2019 is a relative measure of deprivation. This means it can tell you if one area is more deprived than another but not by how much." "For example, a small area with a rank of 1,000 is not half as deprived as a place with a rank of 500." And on change over time: "it would not necessarily be correct to state that the level of deprivation in the area has increased on some absolute scale … as it may be the case that all areas had improved, but that this area had improved more slowly than other areas and so had been 'overtaken' by those areas." A rank is a position in a field that moves. The reader who sees a place fall three positions does not know whether the place got worse.

The field's largest recent case is the World Bank's *Doing Business* index, discontinued on 16 September 2021 after "data irregularities on Doing Business 2018 and 2020 were reported internally in June 2020". The external panel that reviewed it (the External Panel Review, or EPR) — six economists, chaired by Mauricio Cárdenas — made eleven recommendations, of which the one this chapter needs is a heading: "Remove the aggregate index and country rankings". Their reasons: "Doing Business covers a diverse array of indicators that often have little meaning when aggregated with arbitrary weights" (Chapter 5's finding, in an audit), and "Publishing the indicators to enable further analysis elevates the level of global policy dialog; aggregating them and ranking countries lowers it, and invites the type of external interference and conflicts of interest witnessed in recent years". Summarising two earlier reviews of the index, from 2008 and 2013, the panel also records the arithmetic of a rank among bunched scores: "Small changes in actual regulation can produce large swings in rankings, given bunching of scores in the middle of the distribution." A 2018 blog by two authors, one of them a later panel member, had shown a country's rank moving "all due to methodological tinkering and almost no underlying changes in Chile's actual laws or policies". The successor, Business Ready, "does not present an aggregate score by economy"; its FAQ never uses the word "rank"; and it presents economies "into five groups of equal size (quintiles), from highest to lowest performers" within each pillar. The league table went; the ordering, in equal-sized bands, stayed. A display can drop the number and keep the hierarchy, and a quintile band is a rank with fewer rows. The distinction this chapter will draw in section 5 is between bands of that kind — an ordering cut into equal shares — and categories set by statistical separation from an expectation ("higher", "lower", "similar"; "alert", "alarm"), which say how far a place is from what would be expected of it and nothing about its position among the others.

### A rank is a noisy summary

The statistical case was made to the Royal Statistical Society in 1996, by Goldstein and Spiegelhalter, in a paper on league tables of schools and hospitals whose typescript is open. Its argument is for intervals: "although such ranks are particularly sensitive to sampling variability, there has been no straightforward way to place interval estimates around those ranks". When intervals are placed, the result in their worked example of 325 schools is that "about two thirds of all possible comparisons do not allow separation". Their conclusion goes further than most dashboards would like: "An overinterpretation of a set of rankings where there are large uncertainty intervals, as in the examples we have given, can lead both to unfairness and inefficiency and unwarranted conclusions about changes in ranks." And: "current official support for output 'league tables', even adjusted ones, is misplaced and Government should be concerned that potential users are properly informed of their shortcomings." "If such tables continue to be produced then they need an accompanying health warning about their use." They also name the reason comparison of institutions is hard at all: many of the outcomes "are influenced more by factors extrinsic to the institutions than by those for which institutions might be held to be accountable."

The empirical demonstration followed two years later. Marshall and Spiegelhalter ranked fertility clinics by live-birth rate, as the regulator's league table did, and found: "There was great uncertainty, however, concerning the true ranks, particularly for the smaller clinics." "Only one clinic could be confidently ranked in the bottom quarter according to this measure of performance." "Many centres had substantial changes in rank between years, even though their live birth rate did not change significantly." Their conclusion is a rule: "Even when there are substantial differences between institutions, ranks are extremely unreliable statistical summaries of performance and change in performance, particularly for smaller institutions." "Any performance indicator should always be associated with a measure of sampling variability." Chapter 6's small place is at the top and bottom of the table by arithmetic. A later working paper by Leckie and Goldstein added the time dimension: a school league table describes a past cohort while the parent's decision concerns a future one, and "incorporating this uncertainty leads to a situation where only a handful of schools' future performances can be separated from both the overall mean and from one another with an acceptable degree of precision" — "school league tables, including value-added ones, have very little to offer as guides to school choice." The modern econometric treatment, by Mogstad and colleagues, builds confidence sets for ranks and finds, in their abstract, that conclusions about which countries do best at reading, maths and science "are fairly robust to accounting for uncertainty", while the sets "become much less informative" for neighbourhoods at finer grain. † That is the honest shape of the evidence: ranks of a few large units with large differences between them can survive their intervals; ranks of many small places do not; and a dashboard of districts and venues is the second case.

The sector's own exemplar says the same thing in its own audit. The EU's Cultural and Creative Cities Monitor reports, of its 2019 edition, that "such confidence intervals are fairly narrow for the majority of the cities (less than ±3 positions for around 80% of them)", and its 2017 statistical annex that "the C3 rank is close to the median rank (fewer than two positions away) for 81% of the cities" under two thousand simulations — a thousand sets of weights perturbed by ±25%, each run under two normalisation methods. Read the other way, as an inference from those interval widths: for about one city in five the rank interval spans three places or more under modelling choices alone, and the test varied only weights and normalisation. Version 1 of this book paraphrased this as "for roughly one city in five, the rank reflects modelling choices rather than data"; the actual wording is narrower and is given here.

### What the professional body says

The Royal Statistical Society's working party on performance indicators, in 2005, put the position that this chapter adopts: "PM done well is broadly productive for those concerned. Done badly, it can be very costly and not merely ineffective but harmful and indeed destructive." "Because of their usually tentative nature, PIs should be seen as 'screening devices' and not overinterpreted." "Procedures for data collection, analysis, presentation of uncertainty and adjustment for context, together with dissemination rules, should be explicitly defined and reflect good statistical practice." The NHS's publisher of the Summary Hospital-level Mortality Indicator attaches the rule to a live number: "The SHMI is not a direct measure of quality of care." "The SHMI cannot be used to directly compare mortality outcomes between trusts." "It is inappropriate to rank trusts according to their SHMI." And a minute of the UK Statistics Authority's board, in October 2025, records a member's point that "the comparability of official statistics should not be reduced to league tables" — a discussion point, not guidance, and cited here as no more than that.

One official voice pulls the other way, and the tension should be seen rather than hidden. The Government Analysis Function's chart standard says: "Always rank bars by value, unless there is a natural order, for example, age or time." It is a readability rule, and it is right as one; a bar chart of twenty places sorted by value is easier to read than one sorted alphabetically. It is also, by construction, a league table. The chart standard points to separate guidance on showing uncertainty but does not itself ask for it; the statistical guidance quoted above says that ranks carry intervals too wide to separate most pairs. A dashboard designer inherits both, and section 5 gives the form that satisfies both: a sorted display in which the sorting is visibly not the finding.

## 4 · Comparing like with like

### Standardisation, and the ratio that must not be ranked

Two areas have the same crude death rate. One is young and one is old. "Given that mortality rates increase with age, one would expect the older population in area B to experience more deaths" — so equal rates mean the younger area is doing worse. The public-health method for this is standardisation, and the Association of Public Health Observatories' technical briefing gives both forms in a sentence each. Direct: "Directly age-standardised rates express an indicator in terms of the overall rate that would occur in a standard population age-structure if it experienced the age-specific rates of the observed population." Indirect: "The age-specific rates of a chosen standard population (usually the relevant national or regional population) are applied to the age structure of the subject population to give an expected number of events", and "The observed number of events is then compared to that expected and is usually expressed as a ratio (observed/expected)." The ONS's one-sentence reason: age-standardised rates "allow for differences in the age structure of populations and therefore allow valid comparisons to be made between geographic areas, over time and between sexes" — with the caveat that changing the standard breaks the series: rates "based on the 1976 ESP are not comparable with those based on the 2013 ESP".

The method generalises beyond age: whatever drives the number apart from the thing compared — population size, venue count, income, the mix of what is there — can be standardised for, if it is measured, and the result is an *expected* figure against which the *observed* one is read. That ratio is what the NHS's hospital mortality indicator is: "the ratio between the actual number of patients who die following hospitalisation at the trust and the number that would be expected to die on the basis of average England figures, given the characteristics of the patients treated there." And it comes with a rule that is easy to miss and that most dashboards break. "Indirectly standardised ratios for areas A and B may be compared to the standard but should only be directly compared to each other if the age structures of areas A and B are similar to the standard, or if the ratio of their age-specific mortality rates to that of the standard is consistent across the age groups." An observed-to-expected ratio tells you how a place compares with the expectation; it tells you how two places compare with each other only under the two conditions the briefing names. The SHMI publisher's "It is inappropriate to rank trusts according to their SHMI" is given without a reason on its page; this chapter's reading is that the arithmetic above is one. A dashboard that standardises each district against a national or city expectation and then sorts the districts by their ratios has made a comparison the method does not license.

Two further rules from the same briefing: direct standardisation "is the preferred method for comparing a number of different populations against each other" (read once), and indirect standardisation "is more stable as it minimises the variance … It is therefore more appropriate when dealing with the statistical significance of small populations" (read once). Public Health England's technical guide adds a floor: "Directly standardised rates should not be calculated when the total count across all age groups is less than 10" (read once). Chapter 6's small place meets the method's limit again.

### Who is like whom: the official peer methods

"Like" also means which places to compare at all. Three UK methods are published, and their candour varies. The most used, the Chartered Institute of Public Finance and Accountancy's Nearest Neighbours Model, says on its public page that "measuring similarities between local authorities can be a subjective process", that "the output returned by these calculations is a simplistic way of presenting a fairly complex underlying idea", and that "the outcome ultimately relies on the indicators and mathematical procedures used" — forty metrics, with the distance measure and number of neighbours not stated on its public page. The Office for Local Government adopted it: "we use Chartered Institute of Public Finance and Accounting (CIPFA)'s nearest neighbour model in order to try to compare authorities who are similar in terms of demographics and size, amongst other things."

The ONS's method is fuller. Its clustering of local authorities uses k-means on 34 metrics; "The results should not be viewed as being a judgement about the performance of a local authority"; the clusters "can be used by subnational policy makers to identify other local authorities that may be facing similar challenges, and to create control groups for evaluating the impact of interventions"; and the method's own weakness is stated: "K-means clustering is particularly sensitive to skewedness and extreme values in the data." In February 2025 the ONS added statistical nearest neighbours, and in March 2026 split the lists by tier of authority: "an ordered list of the 20 most similar areas" by "Euclidean distance, a measure of the straight-line distance between two points in a multidimensional space", "more suitable for projects that are looking to identify a small number of similar areas for direct comparison" — "An example of this is finding an area with a similar profile to use as a control to track the impact of a policy intervention over time." Two warnings go with it: "the nearest neighbours for an area may not all be in the same cluster", so two official definitions of "similar" can disagree about the same place; and the output "is designed as a snapshot, and should not be viewed as comparable over time with previous versions of the output". In its public service, Explore Local Statistics, the ONS then describes an area against its peers in three words — "higher", "lower" or "similar" — using the median absolute deviation ("The text says 'similar' if the values are within 1 median absolute deviations of each other"), shows no rank, and suppresses the comparison when "there are fewer than 10 areas with data for the same geographic level and period as the selected area".

The Department for Education's statistical neighbours model for children's services is the fullest open statement. "Each local authority receives a set of 10 statistical neighbours (SN)." The distance is "a weighted Euclidean distance on the standardised background variables", with the variables chosen by regression for how well they predict the outcomes of interest and then weighted. Its assumption is written down: "It was assumed that local authorities with similar characteristics would perform or progress at a similar rate." And each neighbour carries a label for how close it is, from "Extremely Close" to "Not Close", on stated distance bands (read once). That is what a peer method should look like on a dashboard: a fixed number, a stated distance, a stated assumption, and a label for how like the neighbour is.

### When the compared choose their comparators

Version 1 of this book proposed that each place choose its own peers, and argued that the choice would itself be scene knowledge worth collecting. The evidence on self-chosen comparators that this chapter could reach comes from one field, executive pay, where companies choose the peer group against which their chief executive's pay is set, and one of its two papers is read here at the authors' abstract. Faulkender and Yang, after controlling for industry, size and other factors: "we find that firms appear to select highly paid peers to justify their CEO compensation and this effect is stronger in firms where the compensation peer group is smaller". Bizjak, Lemmon and Nguyen: "Companies can potentially use compensation peer groups to inflate pay by choosing peers that are larger, choosing a high target pay percentile, or choosing peer firms with high pay", and — in a sentence whose qualifications matter — "Although peers are largely selected based on characteristics that reflect the labor market for managerial talent, we find that peer groups are constructed in a manner that biases compensation upward, particularly in firms outside the Standard & Poor's (S&P) 500." Their hopeful line is hedged: "Preliminary evidence suggests that increased disclosure has reduced the biases in peer group choice." † The analogy is from companies, not places; the peers were mostly chosen for defensible reasons and the bias was at the margin; and the remedy is preliminary. What it supports is a caution rather than a verdict: when the compared choose, the choice tends to flatter, and disclosure is the known check. No public-sector rule requiring comparators to be fixed in advance was found; what the official methods offer instead is a fixed and published procedure.

### When places are not comparable at all

A last method for place against place refuses to force the comparison. In partial-order analysis, two places are compared on several indicators at once; if one exceeds the other on all, it dominates; if each exceeds the other on some, they are *incomparable*, and the analysis says so instead of weighting the indicators into a tie-break. Bruggemann and Carlsen: "Any decision support system mapping the set of indicators into one ranking index will lose this specific information" — the information being that "the appearance of incomparabilities, say x || y, indicate that there are conflicts in data" (read once). Carlsen and Bruggemann: "The evidence of conflicts is smashed out by aggregation methods to obtain a single quantity, which allows a ranking." Fattore's work on multidimensional deprivation, reached at its abstract, puts evaluation "in terms of multidimensional comparisons among achievement profiles, rather than through attribute score aggregations". † The method's own limit is stated by its advocates: "When the number of incomparabilities overwhelms comparabilities, the situation becomes uncomfortable from a partial order point of view" (read once). For a dashboard, incomparability is a display state: *these two places cannot be ordered on these measures*, which Chapter 5's price list showed is the truthful answer whenever the parts trade off. No usability research on whether lay readers can read "incomparable" was found.

## 5 · The funnel: the comparison that is not a league table

Chapters 5 and 6 both promised this chapter the funnel plot, and it is the form in which the public-health world replaced the league table. Spiegelhalter's 2005 paper, reached at its abstract, defines it: "'Funnel plots' are recommended as a graphical aid for institutional comparisons, in which an estimate of an underlying quantity is plotted against an interpretable measure of its precision." "'Control limits' form a funnel around the target outcome, in a close analogy to standard Shewhart control charts." His conclusion: "funnel plots are flexible, attractively simple, and avoid spurious ranking of institutions into 'league tables'." † Public Health England's technical guide gives the mechanics: "A funnel plot is a type of chart in which an indicator is plotted on the y-axis against the denominator (or sample size) of that indicator on the x-axis." "The control limits or 'funnels' represent the bounds of statistical confidence around the average value." "As the sample size increases, the control limits narrow, giving a characteristic funnel shape." And the warning Chapter 6 gave for control charts: "2 out of every 1,000 observations are likely to lie outside the 3 standard deviation funnel" — a correct funnel, on places that are all alike, still puts some outside.

The reasoning behind it is Chapter 6's common and special cause applied to places instead of time, and the Association of Public Health Observatories' briefing states it in plain English. "Methods based on ranking, such as league tables or percentiles, have a number of flaws." "The main problem with ranking is the implicit assumption that apparent differences between organisations are the results of better or poorer performance." "Simply because institutions may produce different values for an indicator, and we naturally tend to rank these values, does not mean that we are observing variation in performance." "All systems within which institutions operate, no matter how stable, will produce variable outcomes." The funnel asks a different question of each place — is this place outside the range that a stable system of places like it would produce, given how many cases it has? — and the briefing claims a practical result: statistical process control (SPC) methods "have also been shown to improve targeting of performance management compared with the use of league tables." The current national audit guidance builds the funnel into a rule: "Funnel plots can be used to take into account the size (volume of activity) of a healthcare provider meaning that larger providers, with larger numbers of patients, have narrower confidence limits"; a difference "of more than three standard deviations is defined as an 'alarm'", and between two and three "an 'alert'" (read once); and "Comparison of healthcare providers must take account of the differences in the mix of patients by adjusting for known, measurable patient characteristics that are associated with the performance indicator."

Two things the funnel does that a league table cannot. It places the small unit where Chapter 6 said it belongs: inside wide limits, so that its extreme value is read as expected variation unless it is extreme even for its size. And it is two-tailed by construction — a point below the lower limit is as much a signal as one above — which is the positive-deviance search Chapter 4 opened and Chapter 6 could not source: the place doing better than its size and conditions predict is the one below the funnel's floor on a harm measure, or above its ceiling on a good one.

One thing it does not do by itself. When the places differ in ways the denominator does not capture — case mix, deprivation, the kind of scene — more points fall outside the funnel than chance allows. Spiegelhalter named the problem the same year: "A problem can arise when a performance indicator shows substantially more variability than would be expected by chance alone, since ignoring such 'over-dispersion' could lead to a large number of institutions being inappropriately classified as 'abnormal'." † The briefing: "Such an abundance of points outside the control limits is sometimes known as overdispersion." "It arises when there are large numbers of events, and case-mix or other risk factors (e.g. deprivation) are not accounted for." "In this case it is usual to adjust the control limits or the data to eliminate potential sources of variation, such as case-mix and demography." The remedy is section 4's standardisation, or wider limits that admit the unmeasured difference — and either is a choice that the display declares.

The funnel resolves section 3's tension with the chart standard. A funnel's points can be sorted by anything — size, usually — and the sorting is visibly not the finding; the finding is distance from the limits. "Always rank bars by value" and "it is inappropriate to rank" are reconciled by a display in which order is a reading aid and position inside or outside the funnel is the claim. It also settles what counts as a band. The funnel's "alert" and "alarm", and the ONS's "higher", "lower" and "similar", are categories of statistical separation from an expectation: each says how far this place is from what would be expected of a place like it, and a place can move between them without any other place moving. A quintile is a category of position: it says where this place stands among the others, and a place moves between quintiles when the others move. The first is what this chapter means by comparison without a league table; the second is a league table in bands. Both, cropped, say one word about a place; only the first says something the place can act on alone. Whether a first-time reader reads a funnel that way is not settled by the literature.

## 6 · This period against last

### A series has parts, and every trend line is a choice

The standard open textbook on forecasting gives the vocabulary. A series is a trend-cycle, a seasonal component and a remainder, added or multiplied: "The additive decomposition is the most appropriate if the magnitude of the seasonal fluctuations, or the variation around the trend-cycle, does not vary with the level of the time series", and "When the variation in the seasonal pattern, or the variation around the trend-cycle, appears to be proportional to the level of the time series, then a multiplicative decomposition is more appropriate." Removing the season leaves the adjusted series: "If the seasonal component is removed from the original data, the resulting values are the 'seasonally adjusted' data." But "Seasonally adjusted series contain the remainder component as well as the trend-cycle", so "they are not 'smooth', and 'downturns' or 'upturns' can be misleading", and "If the purpose is to look for turning points in a series, and interpret any changes in direction, then it is better to use the trend-cycle component rather than the seasonally adjusted data."

The trend-cycle is itself an estimate, made by smoothing, and the smoothing is a choice. The oldest method, the moving average, "eliminates some of the randomness in the data, leaving a smooth trend-cycle component", and "In general, a larger order means a smoother curve"; classical decomposition built on it "originated in the 1920s", is "not recommended, as there are now several much better methods", leaves no trend estimate "for the first few and last few observations", "tends to over-smooth rapid rises and falls in the data", and assumes "that the seasonal component repeats from year to year". The modern method, STL, lets "The seasonal component … change over time, and the rate of change can be controlled by the user"; "The smoothness of the trend-cycle can also be controlled by the user"; its two parameters "control how rapidly the trend-cycle and seasonal components can change", and "Smaller values allow for more rapid changes." The software's default seasonal window of eleven "usually gives a good balance between overfitting the seasonality and allowing it to slowly change over time" — "But, as with any automated procedure, the default settings will need adjusting for some time series" (read once). Every "underlying trend" line is the product of a window someone set, and a different window gives a different line; the line is shown with its method named, or the reader will take it for data.

### Seasonal adjustment is a model, and it is revised

Official statistics adjust for season so that this month can be compared with last. The ONS: "The purpose of seasonal adjustment is to remove systematic calendar-related variation associated with the time of the year, that is, seasonal effects." "This facilitates comparisons between consecutive time periods." The effects removed are two kinds: seasonal effects — weather patterns, the school year, "retail sales increasing in the run up to Christmas", the lengths of months (read once) — and calendar effects, "trading day effects, which are caused by months having differing numbers of each day of the week from year to year" and "moving holidays, which may fall in different months from year to year: for example Easter, which can occur in either March or April". What is left is the trend and the irregular, and the ONS files three things under irregular that a dashboard reader might expect to be adjusted away: "sampling error, non-sampling error, unseasonable weather, natural disasters, or strikes". Adjustment removes the usual winter, not this year's unusual one; the ONS's retail bulletin handles weather as commentary — "which they attributed to promotions, the hot weather, and the World Cup" (read once) — and no standard weather caveat was found.

Three properties of the adjusted series matter for a display. First, it is an estimate. The US Census Bureau: "It is important to note that seasonal factors are estimates based on present and past experience, and that future data may show a different pattern of seasonal effects." The ONS's retail index QMI (its quality and methodology information report): "Seasonal adjustment is performed each month and reviewed each year", and "Revisions to all published seasonally adjusted estimates are not constrained or restricted as they are for other short-term indicators and the national accounts." Eurostat's guidelines: revisions happen "because of a better estimate/identification of the seasonal pattern due to new information provided by new unadjusted data", and "in seasonal adjustment it may be the case that just one additional observation results in revisions of the seasonally adjusted data for several years, which sometimes confuses users." A dashboard's adjusted history can change when a new month arrives, and the reader who remembers last quarter's chart will see a different one. Second, it has a minimum length. Eurostat: "Very short series (less than 3 years) cannot be seasonally adjusted using either moving average or model based methods"; series of three to seven years "are long enough to run moving average or model based methods but remain quite short" and need more frequent review. A dashboard eighteen months old cannot show a seasonally adjusted series of its own data, whatever its software offers. Third, it can manufacture what it claims to remove. The Australian Bureau of Statistics: "When a time series is dominated by the trend or irregular components, it is nearly impossible to identify and remove what little seasonality is present. Hence seasonally adjusting a non-seasonal series is impractical and will often introduce an artificial seasonal element." Eurostat's rule: "seasonal adjustment should be applied only when seasonal and/or calendar effects can be properly explained, identified and estimated." And the method is not one method: "seasonality cannot be precisely defined and different approaches … may result in different outcomes" (read once).

### The same month last year

The commonest dashboard comparison is this month against the same month a year ago, on the reasoning that it needs no adjustment because the season is the same. The Australian Bureau of Statistics answers that reasoning directly. "A comparison of original data from the same period in each year does not completely remove all seasonal effects." Moving holidays "fall in different periods in each year, hence they will distort observations" (read once); "year to year values will be biased by any changes in seasonal patterns that occur over time"; trading-day effects are ignored (read once). And the larger fault: "the major disadvantage of comparing year to year original data, is lack of precision and time delays in the identification of turning points in a series." "If we compare year apart data in the original series, we may miss turning points occurring during the year" — a series that rose until September and has fallen since can still show March above last March. Eurostat's rule for which growth rate goes with which series: "Period-on-period growth rates and changes in level should be computed on seasonally adjusted data"; "Year-on-year comparisons should be computed on calendar adjusted or, in the case of absence of calendar effects, on unadjusted data."

Two official traditions then disagree about the one thing a dashboard reader most wants, the turning point, and the disagreement should be shown rather than settled. The textbook says read turning points from the trend-cycle, not the adjusted series. Eurostat says the trend-cycle is least reliable exactly where the reader looks: "When presenting trend-cycle estimates, the most recent values should not be shown because of the end-point problem or they should be accompanied by warnings", and "Particular care is required at turning points, where it is often months before the new correct direction of development appears." The ONS's own retail bulletin takes the practical line: "Monthly growth rates can be volatile", and "Monthly growth should therefore be used with caution and alongside other measures, such as the three-month growth rate." A dashboard that shows the latest point of a smoothed line as a turn has shown the least reliable estimate in the series as its headline.

### What happened a year ago moves this year's rate

A year-on-year rate has two ends, and a change in it can come from either. The European Central Bank defines the base effect as "the contribution to the change in the year-on-year inflation rate in a particular month that stems from a deviation of the month-on-month rate of change in the base month (i.e. the same month one year earlier) from the usual seasonal pattern". In plain terms: if last March was unusually high, this March's year-on-year growth is pulled down whatever happened this March. "The change in the year-on-year inflation rate from one month to the next reflects the impact of both recent price changes and price movements in the base month, i.e. 12 months earlier." A dashboard that shows "footfall down 8% on last year" and lets the reader infer that this year is weak has not checked whether last year was strong. The reader needs both ends.

Two smaller disciplines sit beside this. Percentage points and per cent: "A percentage point is the difference between percentages. A value of 10% falling by 1 percentage point becomes 9%", and "A fall of 1% would result in a value of 9.9%." And the word "significant": the ONS's style guide says "This term has a specific meaning in statistics. Do not use it in a statistical context unless you have a particular point to make about the statistical significance of an estimate" — which is Chapter 6's badge rule stated as house style. No official rule was found on the percentage change from a small base ("from two to four is a hundred per cent"); the nearest is the Analysis Function's "Describe the direction of change, its absolute size and its relative size over time", which, followed, makes the small base visible.

### Indices, bases and real terms

Much of what a dashboard shows as a line is an index, and an index has a hidden term. Eurostat's explainer: "An index shows the development of a number over time. It is not expressed in tons, euro or any similar common unit; it only shows the change of a figure from one point in time to another", with the reference value "usually set to 100". The ONS, of its consumer price indices: the index "measures price changes, not price levels"; the reference date "is arbitrary, providing simply a convenient benchmark for comparison"; "The choice of date has no material effect on the measurement of price changes between one month and another." Rebasing changes the level and not the movement: "The change to the reference year is a simple calculation and does not impact on the movements of the series." But a change in index value is not a percentage change, and the US Bureau of Labor Statistics gives the reason: "Movements of an index from one month to another are usually expressed as percent changes rather than as changes in the index value, because index value changes are affected by the level of the index in relation to its base period, while percent changes are not." An index that has climbed to 140 moves 1.4 points for every one per cent; a dashboard that reads points as percentages is wrong by the level.

A price index is also a basket, and the basket is a choice refreshed on a schedule: the international manual says "At a minimum, weights should be updated every five years" and "the longer the period between weight updates, the less relevant and representative the CPI becomes", which is why series are chain-linked — the ONS's indices "must be chain-linked twice every year" — and why a long index is a chain of short ones joined. And a money series is two series: "Value estimates of retail sales reflect both price and volume changes", so "To remove the direct effects of price changes, value data are deflated to produce RSI volume measures." The Treasury's guide: "Current price figures measure value of transactions in the prices relating to the period being measured"; "Constant price figures express value using the average prices of a selected year"; the deflated series "can be used to express a given time series or data set in real terms, i.e. by removing price changes." A dashboard showing spend rising over five years without saying whether in cash or real terms has shown inflation as growth.

### The commonest false change

Regression to the mean is the explanation that should be tried before any other for an improvement in a place selected because it was doing badly, or a decline in one selected because it was doing well. Barnett, van der Pols and Dobson, at their abstract: it "is a statistical phenomenon that can make natural variation in repeated data look like real change. It happens when unusually large or small measurements tend to be followed by measurements that are closer to the mean." "The effect of RTM in a sample becomes more noticeable with increasing measurement error and when follow-up measurements are only examined on a sub-sample selected using a baseline value." "RTM is a ubiquitous phenomenon in repeated data and should always be considered as a possible cause of an observed change." † Bland and Altman's one-page note, open in full, gives the mechanism and the remedy: "regression towards the mean occurs whenever we select an extreme group based on one variable and then measure another variable for that group"; "The second group mean will be closer to the mean for all subjects than is the first, and the weaker the correlation between the two variables the bigger the effect will be"; "Even if subjects are not treated the mean blood pressure will go down, owing to regression towards the mean"; and "We need to separate any genuine reductions due to treatment from the effect of regression towards the mean. This is best done by using a randomised control group, but it can be estimated directly." Morton and Torgerson: "The more extreme the value from the population mean, the more room there is to regress to the mean."

Places on a dashboard are chosen for attention because their number is extreme — the district with the worst closures, the venue with the best attendance — and attention is followed by a measurement. The measurement will, on average, be less extreme, whether or not anything was done. Chapter 6's one-tailed search and Chapter 4's outlier-as-question both arrive here: the thing selected for being extreme is the thing most likely to look as if it changed.

### When a series is no longer one series

Chapter 4 asked that every figure carry its revision status; this chapter asks that every series carry its breaks. The Code of Practice for Statistics requires producers to "Give advance notice to users of planned changes to methods and sources that will impact the statistics" and to "Explain the nature and extent of the change, and provide a consistent back series where possible"; to "Release revisions and corrections of errors transparently … being clear about the nature and scale of change"; and to use data "consistent over time and comparable across and within geographies". The ONS's definition: "Revisions are updates to previously published statistics, analysis or data that improve quality by incorporating improved methods, additional data sources or statistics that were unavailable at the point of initial publication", and the trade-off is named: "in particular between timeliness and accuracy". Eurostat's: "Revisions are broadly defined as any change in a value of a statistic released to the public", and a vintages database "archives data revisions and inflow of new data extracted from primary sources"; the Philadelphia Fed's plain gloss is that vintages are "snapshots". The ONS's crime statistics show what a break does: "it is not possible to compare police recorded crime earlier than the year ending March 2003", because recording standards changed and "Both these changes resulted in an increase in the number of crimes recorded"; and when the survey moved to the telephone, a reworded question "had a far greater effect than was envisaged" — more than the change of mode. The Analysis Function's guidance for describing change gives the discipline in three lines: "Describe the direction of change, its absolute size and its relative size over time"; "Changes between periods can fall within the margins of survey error and random variation, especially for weekly, monthly or quarterly series"; "Emphasise that changes within the bounds of random variation are indicative, rather than definitive, and should be looked at in the context of the broader series" — and the warning against caveats that are not caveats: "Phrases like 'care must be taken' and 'exercise caution' are not sufficient by themselves, because they do not help the reader to understand what they can and cannot do with the numbers."

A change over time in a population has one more ambiguity, which the public-health primer names. "Age effects are variations linked to biological and social processes of aging specific to individuals"; "Period effects result from external factors that equally affect all age groups at a particular calendar time"; "Cohort effects are variations resulting from the unique experience/exposure of a group of subjects (cohort) as they move across time"; and "it is statistically impossible to estimate unique estimates for the three effects" because "Period – Age = Cohort". A scene's audience changing over a decade may be the same people ageing, everyone living through the same years, or a new generation replacing an old one, and the series alone cannot say which.

### How long before a trend may be read

The question every dashboard owner asks has no number for an answer, and the textbook says so. "As with almost all sample size questions, there is no easy answer." "The sample size required increases with the number of parameters to be estimated, and the amount of noise in the data." "Some textbooks provide rules-of-thumb giving minimum sample sizes for various time series models. These are misleading and unsubstantiated in theory or practice." "There is, for example, no justification for the magic number of 30 often given as a minimum for ARIMA modelling" (read once). The author's own paper on seasonal models gives the floor and the caveat: a seasonal model needs at least a few more observations than it has parameters — "6 observations for quarterly data and 14 observations for monthly data" for the simplest, "9 observations for quarterly data and 17 observations for monthly data" for Holt–Winters — and "For most realistic problems, substantially more data are required." The only official thresholds found are Eurostat's three and seven years for seasonal adjustment, and Chapter 6's NHS rule for a run on a control chart, which is a policy and says so. The answer a dashboard can give is the one it owes: how long its series is, how noisy, and what that length can and cannot support — a seasonal adjustment at three years, a trend at whatever length its noise allows, and until then, the points with their season beside them and no line through them.

## 7 · Before against after

### The design introduced as a bad example

The study of what may be concluded from a before-and-after comparison was founded by a 1963 chapter whose authors numbered the designs and graded them, and the comparison every dashboard makes is their Design 2. Campbell and Stanley: "Internal validity is the basic minimum without which any experiment is uninterpretable: Did in fact the experimental treatments make a difference in this specific experimental instance?" They list "eight different classes of extraneous variables" which, "if not controlled in the experimental design, might produce effects confounded with the effect of the experimental stimulus". The eight, in their words: "History, the specific events occurring between the first and second measurement in addition to the experimental variable." Maturation, "processes within the respondents operating as a function of the passage of time per se (not specific to the particular events)". "Testing, the effects of taking a test upon the scores of a second testing." Instrumentation, "changes in the calibration of a measuring instrument or changes in the observers or scorers used". "Statistical regression, operating where groups have been selected on the basis of their extreme scores." Selection, "Biases resulting in differential selection of respondents for the comparison groups". "Experimental mortality, or differential loss of respondents from the comparison groups." And eighth, the interactions among these, such as selection with maturation. Of the one-group before-and-after design, "while it is judged as enough better than Design 1 to be worth doing where nothing better can be done", "it is introduced here as a 'bad example' to illustrate several of the confounded extraneous variables that can jeopardize internal validity." Design 1, the single observation after the event, has "such a total absence of control as to be of almost no scientific value" (read once).

The eight are a checklist for any dashboard that shows a line crossing a vertical marker labelled "policy introduced". History: what else happened. Maturation: what was already changing. Testing: did measuring the place change it (Chapter 5's reactivity). Instrumentation: did the measurement change — Chapter 4's platform caveat and section 6's breaks. Regression: was the place chosen because it was extreme. Selection and mortality: who is in the after that was not in the before. Interaction: did the places selected also mature differently. A reader shown the line and the marker and nothing else is being asked to rule out all eight from the picture, which is not possible.

### Interrupted time series: the pre-trend as the counterfactual

The design that uses a before-and-after series properly is the interrupted time series, and the open tutorial by Bernal, Cummins and Gasparrini is the standard account. "In an ITS study, a time series of a particular outcome of interest is used to establish an underlying trend, which is 'interrupted' by an intervention at a known point in time." Its counterfactual is explicit: "The hypothetical scenario under which the intervention had not taken place and the trend continues unchanged (that is: the 'expected' trend, in the absence of the intervention, given the pre-existing trend) is referred to as the 'counterfactual'." It requires that the shape of the expected effect be chosen first — "the essential, yet often omitted, step of proposing the impact model a priori": "whether the change will be a gradual change in the gradient of the trend, a change in the level or both", and whether immediately or after a lag. "It is important that this decision is made a priori based on existing literature and knowledge of the intervention". That is Chapter 6's forking paths closed in advance: choose the shape before looking, or the data will choose it for you.

Its requirements and threats are stated. "ITS requires a clear differentiation of the pre-intervention period and the post-intervention period." "ITS works best with short-term outcomes that are expected to change either relatively quickly after an intervention is implemented or after a clearly defined lag." On how many points: "There are no fixed limits regarding the number of data points, as the power depends on various other factors including distribution of data points before and after the intervention, variability within the data, strength of effect, and the presence of confounding effects such as seasonality." The Cochrane group's floor for admitting such a study to a review is "at least three data points before and three after the intervention" — a floor for admissibility, not for power. The threats: co-interventions, "other events that occur around the same time as the intervention and that potentially influence the outcome"; seasonality, which "can cause two problems: first, if there is an uneven distribution of months before and after the intervention, such as a higher proportion of winter months, this could bias the results, especially in the analysis of short series"; and autocorrelation, that "consecutive observations tend to be more similar to one another than those that are further apart". The companion paper states the design's limit in one sentence: "the basic interrupted time series design cannot exclude confounding due to co-interventions or other events occurring around the time of the intervention." The remedy is a control — "adding a control group or control outcome which would not have been affected by the intervention (known as a controlled interrupted time series)" — and the controls can be a place, a group, a behaviour, a historical cohort, an outcome that should not have moved, or a time period (read once). The Magenta Book's annex puts the same design in official words: "ITSA does not require a control group. Without a control group, impacts are estimated by assuming that trends would continue in the absence of the intervention", and "The method therefore relies on the absence of other interventions or short-term time effects that might influence trends around the time of the intervention."

For a dashboard this is the first form in which "before and after" may be shown with a counterfactual attached — and the claim it supports is a precise one: that the series *departed from its own trend* after the change, by this much, in this shape. That is more than "after" and less than "because"; the departure may still be history, maturation or regression, which is why the co-interventions are listed and a control series shown where one exists. The display is the pre-trend drawn forward as the expectation, distinguished from the observed points; the after-points against it; the shape of the expected effect declared before the data arrived; the rivals listed. Without those, the vertical marker is a decoration on Design 2.

## 8 · The comparisons that make a counterfactual

Chapter 6 found that "because" needs a counterfactual — what would have happened otherwise — and that the Magenta Book's name for the thing that supplies it is a comparison group, "a proxy for what would have happened to the affected group in the absence of the intervention". This section is about the designs that build one from places and periods, what each assumes, and what each may say. They form a ladder of their own, and the UK's What Works centres have published the rungs.

### Two places, two periods: difference-in-differences

The canonical case is Card and Krueger's. "On April 1, 1992, New Jersey's minimum wage rose from $4.25 to $5.05 per hour." "To evaluate the impact of the law we surveyed 410 fast-food restaurants in New Jersey and eastern Pennsylvania before and after the rise." "Comparisons of employment growth at stores in New Jersey and Pennsylvania (where the minimum wage was constant) provide simple estimates of the effect of the higher minimum wage." The design: take the change in the treated place, subtract the change in a comparison place, and call the difference the effect. The World Bank's methods wiki: "The difference-in-differences method is a quasi-experimental approach that compares the changes in outcomes over time between a population enrolled in a program (the treatment group) and a population that is not (the comparison group)."

Everything rests on one assumption, and the open textbooks state it exactly. Huntington-Klein: "The parallel trends assumption says that, if no treatment had occurred, the difference between the treated group and the untreated group would have stayed the same in the post-treatment period as it was in the pre-treatment period." Cunningham: "The parallel trends assumption cannot be tested because it involves a non-existent counterfactual that cannot be observed." Huntington-Klein again: "In fact, no test of the data could possibly confirm or disprove the parallel trends assumption, since it's based on a counterfactual we can't see." The usual check — did the two places trend together before the change? — is a check and not a proof: "This test simply looks to see whether the treated and untreated groups were trending similarly before treatment." "These are not tests of whether parallel trends is true." "'Passing' these tests does not mean that parallel trends is true". Cunningham: "That is not the same thing as requiring pretrends to be parallel." Roth's 2022 paper adds that the check can make things worse: "conventional pre-trends tests may have low power", and "conditioning the analysis on the result of a pretest can distort estimation and inference, potentially exacerbating the bias of point estimates and under-coverage of confidence intervals" — "the bias caused by a violation of parallel trends can actually be worse conditional on passing the pretest". The World Bank's four checks — a placebo with a fake treatment group, a placebo with a fake outcome, repeated pre-period comparisons, and different comparison groups — are the standard, and "While this assumption cannot be proved, research teams can assess its validity" is the standard's own ceiling.

A dashboard will rarely have one treated place and one comparison; it will have several places that adopted something at different times. Goodman-Bacon showed what the usual pooled estimate then does. "Most DD applications, however, exploit variation across groups of units that receive treatment at different times." The two-way fixed-effects estimator "is a weighted average of all possible two-group/two-period DD estimators in the data", and in that average "The already-treated units in group k can serve as controls even though they are treated because treatment status does not change"; "when already-treated units act as controls, changes in their treatment effects over time get subtracted from the DD estimate"; "This negative weighting only arises when treatment effects vary over time, in which case it typically biases regression DD estimates away from the sign of the true treatment effect". His published abstract: "A causal interpretation of two-way fixed effects DD estimates requires both a parallel trends assumption and treatment effects that are constant over time." He is careful that "This does not imply a failure of the underlying design, but it does caution against the use of a single-coefficient two-way fixed effects specification to summarize time-varying effects" (read once). Callaway and Sant'Anna's answer is to estimate the effect for each adoption group at each time and then aggregate, with "different aggregation schemes that can be used to highlight treatment effect heterogeneity across different dimensions as well as to summarize the overall effect". For a dashboard the reading is simple: a single "effect of the policy across all the districts that adopted it" is, when districts adopted at different dates, an average whose weights the reader cannot see and some of which may be negative; the display is the effect by adoption group, or nothing.

### One place, a blend of others: synthetic control

When there is one treated place and no obvious twin, the method is the synthetic control, and it is the one the Magenta Book 2026 names for place-based work: "A solution to this problem is to use a synthetic control area, which is defined statistically, using available data, to mimic the characteristics of the treatment area". Abadie, Diamond and Hainmueller's founding application: "The idea behind the synthetic control approach is that a combination of units often provides a better comparison for the unit exposed to the intervention than any single unit alone." They "estimate that by the year 2000 annual per-capita cigarette sales in California were about 26 packs lower than what they would have been in the absence of Proposition 99." Two properties make it suited to a display. "Because the weights can be restricted to be positive and sum to one, the synthetic control method provides a safeguard against extrapolation." And it is transparent: "the synthetic control method makes explicit: (1) the relative contribution of each control unit to the counterfactual of interest". Abadie's 2021 guide, read in its author manuscript: "Synthetic control estimators preclude extrapolation, because synthetic control weights are non-negative and sum to one." "Synthetic controls make explicit the contribution of each comparison unit to the counterfactual of interest." The comparison is a named recipe — forty per cent this district, thirty-five that, twenty-five the other — and the recipe is on the face, which is Chapter 5's rule arriving in evaluation.

Its requirements are stated by its author as "contextual requirements, that is, the conditions on the context of the investigation under which synthetic controls are appropriate tools for policy evaluation", and they read as a checklist for any single-place comparison — six in his list, with a seventh, the pre-period, from the 2015 paper. Size of the effect: "small effects will be indistinguishable from other shocks to the outcome of the affected unit, especially if the outcome variable of interest is highly volatile"; "the impact of 'small' interventions with effects of a magnitude similar to the volatility of the outcome are difficult to detect". A comparison group: "inference based on these methods will be faulty in the absence of a suitable comparison group", and "Units that adopt an intervention similar to the one adopted by the unit of interest should not be included in the donor pool". No anticipation: estimators "may be biased if forward looking economic agents react in advance of the policy intervention"; "If there are signs of anticipation, it is advisable to backdate the intervention". No interference: "This is the stable unit treatment value assumption in Rubin (1980), which implies that there is no interference across units", and "Assuming that such spillover effects do not exist is a strong restriction that must often be enforced in the design of the study or accounted for in the analysis of the results." The convex hull: estimates "are predicated on the idea that a combination of unaffected units can approximate the pre-intervention characteristics of the affected unit". Time: "The effect of some interventions may take time to emerge or to be of sufficient magnitude to be quantitatively detected in the data." And the pre-period: "The applicability of the method requires a sizable number of preintervention periods"; "the credibility of a synthetic control depends upon how well it tracks the treated unit's characteristics and outcomes over an extended period of time prior to the treatment." The Magenta Book's annex: "The analysis is only viable where it is possible to establish a historical relationship between the behaviour of the treatment and control groups."

Its test is the placebo, in two directions, named by the same authors: "We refer to these falsification exercises as 'in-time placebos'" — "Our confidence about the validity of this result would dissipate if the synthetic control method also estimated large effects when applied to dates when the intervention did not occur" — and "in-space placebos": "Another way to conduct placebo studies is to reassign the intervention not in time, but to members of the donor pool", and confidence "would disappear if similar or larger estimates arose when the intervention is artificially reassigned to units not directly exposed to the intervention". The 2010 paper ran the method "to every other state in the donor pool" to see whether California's gap was large against the gaps that places with no intervention produce. That is Chapter 6's holdout, built into the design: the effect is a finding only if it is bigger than what the method finds where nothing happened.

### The cut-off, the match, and the ripples

Two further designs appear on the ladders. Regression discontinuity uses a threshold: treatment is assigned by "whether an observed 'assignment' variable … exceeds a known cutoff point", and because people just either side of a cut-off are alike, "the treatment is 'as good as' randomly assigned around the cutoff" — "RD designs can be analyzed – and tested – like randomized experiments", provided "individuals … are unable to precisely manipulate the assignment variable". The Magenta Book's annex gives its limit: "The method only allows estimation of the impact of the intervention for individuals close to the cut-off (local average treatment effect)." Lee and Lemieux, the standard guide, argue that reading is too narrow — the gap "can be interpreted as a weighted average treatment effect across all individuals", and the "only at the threshold" reading "may be an overly simplistic and pessimistic assessment" — and the two should be shown together: the estimate speaks most for those near the line. For a place, the cut-off is a funding threshold, a licensing boundary, a population band; where one exists, the places just inside and just outside are the comparison.

Matching builds the comparison from recorded characteristics, and its limit is the record. The Magenta Book's annex: "As matching is only based on observable characteristics, where treatment and outcomes are affected by unobservables, impact estimates will be biased, and it cannot be determined analytically that there are no such unobservable factors." The World Bank's wiki: "Ultimately, PSM estimation results are only as good as the characteristics used for matching", and the match must have common support — units "with the same covariate values have a positive probability of being both treated and untreated". A matched comparison is like-for-like on whatever someone happened to record, which for a scene is rarely the thing that matters.

The ripples are the assumption every design shares and most dashboards ignore. Cunningham on the no-interference assumption: "this implies that there are no externalities, because by definition, an externality spills over to other untreated units." "We are assuming away this kind of spillover." The Magenta Book 2026, in its section on place-based interventions, names the two: "Spillovers – wider outcomes, either positive or negative, that occur in neighbouring areas that are not targeted by the intervention." "Displacement – where an improved outcome in the treatment area may result in a corresponding deterioration somewhere else." "Understanding these spatial effects will improve the evaluation's assessment of impact and value for money." The What Works Centre for Local Economic Growth's plain version: "Displacement occurs when the increase in activity in one area comes at the expense of other areas", and the sentence any dashboard of a district should carry: "Seeing evidence of growth in the zone itself – the success stories that will always be highlighted by proponents – tells us very little if that activity is simply displaced from elsewhere" (read once). Chapter 6's economic displacement is this; the comparison place that the design needs is also the place the ripple reaches, and a neighbour used as a control has been contaminated by the thing it is controlling for. The Magenta Book is candid about why comparison places are hard to find at all: "Places are not selected for investment randomly but because of distinct characteristics – either a relative strength that interventions will seek to exploit or a relative weakness that they will seek to mitigate. Consequently, it may not always be possible to identify comparison areas that are sufficiently well matched to be used for counterfactual analysis" (read once). Its answer is the synthetic control, and its fallback Chapter 6's: "Where a robust counterfactual is not achievable, theory-based impact evaluation approaches may be used to examine the causal mechanisms that are expected to deliver outcomes."

### What a small place cannot show

Every design above has a size below which it shows nothing, and three traditions say so in the same terms. The trials literature: "Power is the probability of rejecting a false null hypothesis", and the minimum detectable effect is "the effect size below which we may not be able to distinguish that the effect is different from zero, even if it is"; "At best, an underpowered study is a waste of money"; "the addition of more clusters will in general increase power more than the addition of more units to existing clusters" — more places matter more than more people in each. The case-study literature: Abadie's "small effects will be indistinguishable from other shocks". The UK's central guidance: a counterfactual design needs that "The intervention effect is sufficiently large to be distinguished from expected 'noise' in the data", and is "Less feasible if" "Sample sizes are too small", "The effect builds up gradually over an extended time period", "Data is only available as coarsely aggregated totals", or "There is a simultaneous launch nationwide" (the last three read once). The practical consequence for a scene is the one Chapter 6 reached from the other side: a modest effect, in a noisy series, in one district, is undetectable by any of these designs, and a dashboard that shows no effect has not shown that there was none. The trials guidance names the policy risk: null results from an underpowered study "may be used to justify ending a program if they are conflated with the program itself being ineffective" (read once).

### The ladders

The UK's evidence centres have put the designs in order, and the order is by the credibility of the counterfactual. The What Works Centre for Local Economic Growth's version of the Maryland Scientific Methods Scale "is a five-point scale ranging from 1, for evaluations based on simple cross sectional correlations, to 5 for randomised control trials". Level 1: "Either (a) a cross-sectional comparison of treated groups with untreated groups, or (b) a before-and-after comparison of treated group, without an untreated comparison group", with "No use of control variables". Level 2: the same with "adequate control variables". Level 3: "Comparison of outcomes in treated group after an intervention, with outcomes in the treated group before the intervention, and a comparison group used to provide a counterfactual (e.g. difference in difference)", where "there are likely to be important unobserved differences remaining". Level 4: "Quasi-randomness in treatment is exploited, so that it can be credibly held that treatment and control groups differ only in their exposure to the random allocation of treatment" — a discontinuity or an instrument. Level 5: "explicit randomisation into treatment and control groups". And the floor: "We define SMS 3 methods to be the minimum standard for our reviews" — "These methods must involve a comparison of treated units against a control group before-and-after the treatment date." Difference-in-differences "achieves a maximum of SMS 3" because "it does not account for unobservable differences between the two that vary with time."

The Education Endowment Foundation's scale agrees on the order and differs on one placement — five padlocks for "Randomised design", four for "Design for comparison that considers some type of selection on unobservable characteristics (e.g. RDD, Diff-in-Diffs, Matched Diff-in-Diffs)", three for one "that considers selection on all relevant observable confounders", and zero for "No comparator"; "Methods that only attempt to control for observable characteristics (for example, matching/weighting) can only achieve 3 or less." Where the What Works scale caps difference-in-differences at three of five, the education scale gives it four of five; the two agree that it sits above matching and below randomisation, and disagree about how far below. Nesta's standards of evidence, written for innovators rather than researchers, put the lowest rungs in words a dashboard owner will recognise. Level 1: "You can give an account of impact." Level 2: "You are gathering data that shows some change amongst those receiving or using your intervention" — "At this stage, data can begin to show effect but it will not evidence direct causality." Level 3: "You can demonstrate that your intervention is causing the impact, by showing less impact amongst those who don't receive the product/service."

Set beside Chapter 6's three rungs, the ladders say where a dashboard's comparisons sit, and this chapter attaches a verb to each. A place beside another place, or a before beside an after, is Level 1 on the What Works scale — in Nesta's words for its own second level, data that "can begin to show effect but it will not evidence direct causality" — and its verbs are *alongside* and *after*. An interrupted series with its impact model declared adds a counterfactual and earns *departed from its trend*; it is still, on the What Works wording, a before-and-after without a comparison group, and the Cochrane group admits it to reviews on a floor of three points each side. A comparison group, chosen by a published method and trending in parallel as far as can be seen, reaches Level 3 — the floor at which the UK's evidence centres begin to count — and earns *contributed to*, where a theory of change has been tested and the placebo run. A cut-off is Level 4. The synthetic control postdates the 2016 scoring guide and has no level there; the Magenta Book 2026 treats it as the design for place-based work, and this chapter places it with the comparison-group designs, with its placebos as the test. "Caused" is reserved for a design that has earned it — a cut-off, a well-fitted synthetic control, or randomisation — with its placebo run and its ripples considered. Where the ladders differ, the chapter names the What Works scale as the one the UK applies to local growth and gives the other placement beside it; the claimant does not choose the kinder ladder.

## 9 · What is against what might be

The last comparison a dashboard makes is between the measured present and an imagined future — a projection, a scenario, a claim of the form "this policy gives that benefit at this risk". The rules for stating it are better developed than for any other comparison in this chapter, because the UK's forecasters and appraisers are held to account for theirs, and almost unused on dashboards.

### The appraiser's rules

The Green Book's February 2026 edition states them as duties. The first is about the appraiser, not the world: "Optimism bias is the demonstrated systematic tendency for practitioners to be over-optimistic about key assumptions in appraisal, such as social costs, social benefits or project duration." It means that "The capital costs and operating costs of interventions are typically higher in reality than practitioners originally anticipated", "The social benefits of an intervention are typically lower in reality than practitioners originally anticipated", and "The time taken to deliver an intervention is typically longer in reality than practitioners originally anticipated." The duty: "Practitioners must account for optimism bias by making explicit adjustments at the outset of an appraisal." And the method is the organisation's own record: "Practitioners should measure average historical forecast errors: the difference between the values that were anticipated in appraisal, and the actual outturn of those values." Only where a body has no record does it fall back on generic uplifts — the supplementary guidance's table, resting on a 2002 study, which puts the upper adjustment for capital cost at 24% for a standard building and 200% for equipment and development. That table covers costs and durations; for benefits the Green Book's instruction is to decrease the estimate by the body's own record, and where there is no record the size of the decrease is a declared judgement rather than a published number. A claim "x gives y" is expected to be discounted by how wrong similar claims by the same body turned out.

The second duty is the range and the flip. "Practitioners should try to give an indication of the uncertainty associated with social costs and social benefits. The simplest way to do this is with a central estimate and a range around that estimate." "Central estimate: The most likely value that a social cost or social benefit may take." "Range: The lowest and highest values that an impact might reasonably take." "Sensitivity analysis tests how changes in key assumptions might affect the results of a proposal." And: "A switching value is the value that a key variable would need to change to such that an option stops representing value for money." The switching value is the single most useful number a scenario can carry, because it tells the reader how wrong the assumption has to be before the conclusion changes — and "Practitioners should, at a minimum, conduct sensitivity analysis and calculate switching values for the preferred option" (read once). Scenarios are a named method: "Scenario analysis is a method of analysing uncertainty"; "Practitioners examine a range of plausible future outcomes, and then consider the value for money of the proposal under those different outcomes." The 2026 edition defines the two words loosely used on every dashboard: "Uncertainty: The assumptions that are made in an appraisal that are not fully known." "Risk: The effect of uncertainty on objectives."

### The forecaster's rules

The Office for Budget Responsibility publishes, every year, how its forecasts compared with what happened. "Our annual Forecast evaluation report (FER) examines how our forecasts compare to subsequent outturn data and identifies lessons for future forecasts." "These uncertainties mean differences between outturns and forecasts are inevitable." It separates kinds of miss: "Genuine errors, which would have been corrected before the forecast was finalised if we had spotted them", from differences "due to factors that could have been anticipated through improved modelling techniques", from shocks. Its record since 2010 is stated with a sign: "On average, our forecasts have underestimated annual real GDP growth at the one-year horizon by 0.4 percentage points but overestimated it by an average of 0.7 percentage points at the five-year horizon." And it names a structural cause: "the legal requirement that we condition our spending forecast on government plans", which were "subsequently significantly increased several times". A forecast conditioned on stated policy inherits the assumption that policy stays as stated; so does any "what might be" on a dashboard.

Its uncertainty display carries its own caveat. The OBR's fan chart "assumes that past forecast errors are representative of future errors, and does not take into account the current level of uncertainty", with "20 per cent probability bands", so that "there is a one-in-five chance that real GDP growth in 2026 could be higher than 3 per cent and a similar probability that it could be lower than -¾ per cent." The width of the fan is the past record, and the chart says so.

### The fan chart, and its abolition

The Bank of England invented the fan chart in 1996, and the paper that explained it stated its aim: "to convey to the reader a more accurate representation of the Bank's subjective assessment of medium-term inflationary pressures, without suggesting a degree of precision that would be spurious." "The fan chart portrays a probability distribution that approximates to the MPC's subjective assessment of inflationary pressures evolving through time, based on a central view and the risks surrounding it." Its bands were explicit about what they left out: "The distribution is truncated, so that there is an implicit ninth and final pair of bands, occupying the white space outside the 90% covered." The later chart note gave the plainest reading of a probability band in British official prose: "If economic circumstances identical to today's were to prevail on 100 occasions, the MPC's best collective judgement is that inflation in any particular quarter would lie within the darkest central band on only 30 of those occasions", and "on the remaining 10 out of 100 occasions inflation can fall anywhere outside the orange area of the fan chart."

In April 2024 the Bank's independent review, by Ben Bernanke, recommended its removal. "Despite their distinguished history, the fan charts as published in the MPR have weak conceptual foundations, convey little useful information over and above what could be communicated in other, more direct ways, and receive little attention from the public. They should be eliminated." Fan charts "suffer from significant analytical weaknesses and have outlived their usefulness"; their empirical basis "is the historical record of the Bank's forecast errors"; and in their place "the central forecast should be regularly augmented by alternative scenarios". The fan chart stayed in the Bank's reports through February 2026; the April 2026 report is the first without one, and by July 2026 the Monetary Policy Report carries no fan chart and no "best collective judgement" — it offers "a central projection that is judged to provide a reasonable baseline alongside two scenarios", which "are neither all-encompassing nor do they reflect the full distribution of risks". The lesson is not that uncertainty should go unshown. The most sophisticated probability display in British public life was judged to say less than a few named, reasoned alternatives, and the alternatives were introduced with a sentence saying what they do not cover. The Office for Budget Responsibility, meanwhile, still publishes its fan, with the caveat quoted above; the two official forecasters now show the future in two different forms.

### What communicating uncertainty costs

The fear that stating uncertainty costs trust has been tested. Van der Bles and colleagues' framework names what a caveat is about: three objects — "facts, numbers and science" — and "two levels of uncertainty: direct and indirect", the indirect being "uncertainty in terms of the quality of the underlying knowledge that forms a basis for any claims about the fact, number or hypothesis", "generally … communicated as a list of caveats about the underlying sources of evidence". Their review found that "While there is some evidence that communicating epistemic uncertainty does not necessarily affect audiences negatively, impact can vary between individuals and communication formats." Their experiments then measured it: five experiments, including "one field experiment on the BBC News website (total n = 5,780)", varying "formats (verbal vs. numeric), and magnitudes (high vs. low)". "Results show that whereas people do perceive greater uncertainty when it is communicated, we observed only a small decrease in trust in numbers and trustworthiness of the source, and mostly for verbal uncertainty communication." "These results could help reassure all communicators of facts and science that they can be more open and transparent about the limits of human knowledge." The licence is for the number: a range beside a figure costs little; a vague verbal hedge costs more. The finding is of stated trust in a number and its source, measured in experiments, and not of what readers then did.

The words themselves are the problem. The climate panel's guidance fixes a scale — "virtually certain" 99–100%, "very likely" 90–100%, "likely" 66–100%, "about as likely as not" 33 to 66%, "unlikely" 0–33%, "very unlikely" 0–10%, "exceptionally unlikely" 0–1% — and separates it from confidence, which is "expressed qualitatively" in five qualifiers from "very low" to "very high" and "should not be interpreted probabilistically" (read once); likelihood "provides calibrated language for describing quantified uncertainty." Readers do not hear the scale. Budescu and colleagues, in a reconstructed abstract: "The respondents' judgments deviated significantly from the IPCC guidelines, even when the respondents had access to these guidelines." † Wintle and colleagues, in an experiment with 924 participants: "People interpret verbal expressions of probabilities (e.g. 'very likely') in different ways, yet words are commonly preferred to numbers when communicating uncertainty"; "two people can, and often do, have very different numbers in mind when they hear or read words of estimative probability"; and the remedy that worked was the number in the sentence — correspondence with the standard "is substantially improved only when numerical guidelines are bracketed in text": "average correspondence was 66%, compared with 32% in the control." A key on another screen barely helps. The UK's own intelligence community uses a seven-band yardstick "informed by academic research" whose "scale is not continuous to avoid a false impression of accuracy" — the bands leave gaps where the climate panel's overlap.

### Calibration, sharpness and the score

A stated probability has two separable virtues, and a projection on a dashboard can be judged by both. Gneiting, Balabdaoui and Raftery, at their summary: † "Calibration refers to the statistical consistency between the distributional forecasts and the observations and is a joint property of the predictions and the events that materialize." "Sharpness refers to the concentration of the predictive distributions and is a property of the forecasts only." Their paradigm is "maximizing the sharpness of the predictive distributions subject to calibration". Calibration asks whether the things you called 70% likely happened about 70% of the time; sharpness asks whether you said anything — a band wide enough to be always right is perfectly calibrated and useless. The Brier score puts a number on a probability forecast's error: "The Brier score is the squared error of a probabilistic forecast", with a worked example from the forecasting tournament's own guide — a 70% forecast of an event that happened scores (1 − 0.7)² + (0 − 0.3)² = 0.18 (read once). The verification literature's caution: the score is "Sensitive to climatological frequency of the event: the more rare an event, the easier it is to get a good BS without having any real skill." A dashboard that makes projections can keep the score the forecasters keep — and the OBR's annual evaluation shows what keeping it looks like.

### Scenarios are not forecasts

The Government Office for Science draws the line the Bank of England moved toward. "Futures thinking contrasts with forecasting, and forecasting models." "Most forecasting models look at historical trends and, with various degrees of sophistication, attempt to project them forward", and their reliability "starts to break down over the longer term". "Scenarios are compelling stories about a range of different possible futures." "Scenarios are not predictions; they are neither good nor bad, wrong nor right." "No single future scenario will come true, but it is likely that parts of different futures will." The cross-government uncertainty toolkit adds three display rules in official words: "Presenting a single figure is best avoided as it can give a misleading impression of precision"; "Front load the important caveats and explain why they matter"; and "Try to include an even number of scenarios, to avoid having a middle option that may be misinterpreted as the 'most likely' scenario." The ONS's standing caveat on its projections is the label: "Projections are not forecasts and will differ from actual future outcomes to a greater or lesser extent." "These are based on assumptions of future levels of births, deaths, and migration." And the Code of Practice for Statistics makes the rule statutory for producers: they "must prominently explain the quality of the statistics, including strengths and limitations, and communicate the uncertainty in estimates", "by using qualifying words, numbers or graphics"; and, in its standards for public bodies, "Do not use statistics, data or wider analysis in a misleading way. This includes not cherry-picking figures, taking figures out of context or placing undue certainty on them."

A projection on a dashboard, then, has a known form. It is labelled a projection and not a forecast; its assumptions are stated first; it is a central estimate with a range, or an even number of scenarios with what they do not cover; its switching value is given; the body's own record of projection against outturn is kept and shown; the words for likelihood carry their numbers in the sentence; and it is adjusted, in advance, for the demonstrated tendency of the people who made it to be optimistic.

## 10 · Reading market movement

A dashboard that shows how a district's economy is moving borrows the economy's own instruments — indices, counts, prices, search interest, transactions — and each carries terms that the comparison depends on. This section collects the disciplines for reading them.

### Nowcasting predicts the present

Search and transaction data arrive before official statistics, and the method that uses them has a modest founding claim. Choi and Varian: "We are not claiming that Google Trends data can help in predicting the future. Rather we are claiming that Google Trends may help in predicting the present." "Predicting the present, in the sense described above, is a form of 'contemporaneous forecasting' or 'nowcasting'". Their result: simple seasonal models with search terms "tend to outperform models that exclude these predictors by 5% to 20%", and "It is well-known that it is difficult to identify 'turning points' in economic series" (read once). The best-known failure is Google Flu Trends, which — in Lazer and colleagues' account, itself reporting *Nature* — "was predicting more than double the proportion of doctor visits for influenza-like illness (ILI) than the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)", "despite the fact that GFT was built to predict CDC reports"; the authors' phrases for why ("big data hubris"; "algorithm dynamics") are not quoted here. The ONS's own fast-indicator programme states its boundary in its own words: the indicators "allow early identification of large economic changes" at "a level of timeliness and granularity not currently possible with official economic statistics", but "we are not attempting to forecast or predict gross domestic product (GDP) or other headline economic statistics here", "none should be used to predict GDP on their own", and "they should be considered early warning indicators providing timely insight into real activities in the economy, and their potential impact on headline GDP should be carefully interpreted." Chapter 6's footfall and card-spend caveats are the same producer saying the same thing of the same kind of data. A fast series on a dashboard is an early sighting of the present, with Chapter 4's coverage caveat, and never a forecast.

### "Leading" is a past record

Chapter 6 found that "leading" is measured, not declared, and the producer of the oldest leading index says how. The Conference Board's indexes "are summary statistics for the U.S. economy"; "Historically, the cyclical turning points in the leading index have occurred before those in aggregate economic activity", those of the coincident index "at about the same time", and those of the lagging index "generally … after". A series earns a place by conformity — "the series must conform well to the business cycle" — and consistent timing — "the series must exhibit a consistent timing pattern over time as a leading, coincident or lagging indicator" — and by smoothness and currency. Its own handbook warns against reading it the way dashboards read everything: "if economists took every one- or two-month decline in the index seriously, they would be forecasting a recession several times each year"; "never rely on individual data series or simple rules". And it admits that the published record flatters: although the present version of the leading index has a good historical record, "the index that actually existed at those points in time rarely provided adequate signals of the impending downturns" — a signed article within the handbook, so its author's words — and "peaks (or even troughs, for that matter) cannot always be recognized until months after they occur, especially during periods when the data are subject to significant revision" (read once). "Historically" and "generally" are the hedges in the definition itself. A series shown as leading a district's economy either has a measured record of turning points against a named reference series, or carries the word as a label.

### Indices, concentration and churn

Section 6 gave the index rules — change relative to an arbitrary base, points that are not percentages, cash that is not volume — and they apply doubly to borrowed indices, whose base, adjustment and coverage the dashboard did not set. Two further measures appear on dashboards of a local economy. Concentration: the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index "is defined as the sum of the squares of the market shares; it is small when there are many small firms and grows larger as the market becomes more concentrated, reaching 10,000 in a market with a single firm." The thresholds are American — "Markets with an HHI greater than 1,800 are highly concentrated, and a change of more than 100 points is a significant increase" — and the UK's competition authority applies none: "The CMA does not apply any thresholds to market share, number of remaining competitors or on any other measure to determine whether a loss of competition is substantial", while naming the index among possible measures. A "highly concentrated" label on a UK display has borrowed a foreign threshold, and should say so. Churn: no official UK definition was found; the ONS business demography bulletin uses births ("New business registrations … are referred to as business births"), deaths ("Businesses that have ceased to trade (identified through de-registration of the administrative units)"), and the rates of each "as a proportion of the active businesses", with Chapter 6's caveat that the latest two years are provisional. A churn figure is a construction from those, and its recipe is Chapter 5's.

### Two biases in a rising line

Survivorship bias, in Wikipedia's wording, marked as secondary: "a statistical error that results from concentrating on entities that passed a selection process while overlooking those that did not", which "often causes study results to skew higher because only companies that were successful enough to survive until the end of the period are included". A dashboard of venues' average attendance over five years, computed over the venues that still exist, has excluded every venue that closed, and the line rises for that reason among others. And the base effect of section 6, which for a market series read year-on-year is the commonest reason a rate moves when nothing has: last year's spike becomes this year's fall.

What reading market movement gives the chapter is a short list of terms every borrowed series must carry on the face: its base period and whether it has been rebased; whether it is adjusted, and by whom; whether it is cash or real; whose transactions or searches it counts; whether it is a nowcast of the present or a projection of the future; whether "leading" has a measured record; which firms or venues are still in it; and what happened in the base month. Each is a line in the comparison record that section 11 sets out.

## 11 · What holds true regardless

Seven truths this chapter will defend, each carrying what Chapter 0 calls its kind, and the record beneath them.

**One. A comparison is a claim about what is held equal, and the holding is declared — including who chose the comparators.** Place against place, period against period, before against after, present against possible: each asserts that two things are alike in all but the thing compared. Chapter 4 asked for the declaration; this chapter adds that the comparators' selection is part of it, because a comparison set chosen by the compared is a different claim from one chosen by a published method. *Kind: Standard (the official peer methods; the Code of Practice); the "who chose" line is a Nightlife Culture position, Fixed (sequence).*

**Two. A rank is a noisy summary that changes what it ranks; position is shown within a distribution, with its interval, and most pairs cannot be separated.** The ranked change to fit the rank; a rank can fall while the place improves; in the standard worked example two-thirds of pairwise comparisons cannot be told apart; small units sit at both ends; ranks of a few large units can survive their intervals and ranks of many small places do not; a successor index that dropped the rank kept the bands. The display that shows the finding without the ordering is the funnel, or the distribution with the place marked and its interval drawn, with categories of separation and not of position. *Kind: Evidence (Espeland & Sauder; Goldstein & Spiegelhalter; Marshall & Spiegelhalter; Mogstad et al.; the EPR) + Standard (SHMI; RSS); the extension of the ban to every measure on a dashboard of small places is a Reading, Fixed (requirement).*

**Three. Like is compared with like by standardisation and by peers chosen by a published method; an observed-to-expected ratio is compared with the expectation, not ranked.** Standardisation produces an expected figure for each place given what drives the number; the ratio to it answers "how does this place compare with what would be expected", and two such ratios are not, in general, comparable with each other. Peers come from a fixed number, a stated distance and a stated assumption, with a label for how like each is; self-chosen peers are shown beside them and kept as data. *Kind: Standard (APHO; ONS; DfE); Evidence (adjacent domain) for self-selection; Nightlife Culture for showing the chosen beside the published.*

**Four. Change over time is read against the series' own season and history, and the adjustment is named as a model that is revised.** A trend line is a smoothing someone chose; a seasonally adjusted figure is an estimate revised when the next month arrives, impossible under three years, and able to manufacture a season; the same month last year is slow at turning points and carries the base month; the latest smoothed point is the least reliable; an index is change from an arbitrary base and its points are not percentages; a money series is cash until deflated; regression to the mean is the first explanation for a change in a place selected for being extreme; and no number of observations makes a trend readable except the one the series' own noise allows. *Kind: Standard (ONS; Eurostat; Census Bureau; ABS; the Code of Practice) + Evidence (regression to the mean) + Practice (the forecasting textbook).*

**Five. After is not because; the counterfactual earns the verb, and its credibility is graded on a published ladder.** The one-group before-and-after is a "bad example" with eight named rivals. Each design that does better adds a comparison with an assumption that cannot be tested from the data: a pre-trend projected (interrupted series), a parallel trend (difference-in-differences), a weighted blend (synthetic control), a cut-off, a match. Each has stated requirements, a size below which it shows nothing, and a rung on a ladder on which plain before/after is the bottom and the floor for counting is a comparison group; each rung has its verb, from *after* through *departed from its trend* and *contributed to* to *caused*. Spillover and displacement are the assumption every design shares and the ripple a place most needs to see. *Kind: Evidence (Campbell & Stanley; Bernal; Abadie; Goodman-Bacon; Roth) + Standard (Magenta Book 2026; the SMS; EEF) + Practice (Nesta); the verbs are a Nightlife Culture reading of the ladders.*

**Six. A claim about the future is a range with its assumptions, its track record and its switching value; scenarios are not forecasts, and the words for likelihood carry their numbers.** Optimism bias is adjusted for in advance, by the maker's own record of projection against outturn; the central estimate has a range and the conclusion a switching value; scenarios come in an even number with what they do not cover; a projection is labelled one; the record of projection against outturn is kept and shown; and a numerical range costs little stated trust where a vague word costs more and is heard differently by every reader. *Kind: Standard (Green Book 2026; Code of Practice 3.0; the Futures Toolkit; the Uncertainty Toolkit) + Evidence (van der Bles; Wintle; the OBR's record; Bernanke's review).*

**Seven. A market movement is an index whose base, adjustment, coverage and survivors are declared, and "leading" is a past record.** A borrowed series carries terms the dashboard did not set; a nowcast predicts the present and not the future; a leading indicator is defined by a history of turning points, hedged "Historically" and "generally", that looked worse in real time than in the published record; a concentration threshold on a UK display is borrowed from abroad; and a rising average over survivors has excluded the fallen. *Kind: Standard (ONS; BLS; the Conference Board; the CMA) + Evidence (Choi & Varian; Lazer et al.) ; Reading for the survivorship line.*

### The record beneath them

Chapter 4 asked for a provenance set beneath every number, Chapter 5 for a recipe card beneath every measure, and Chapter 6 for a claim record beneath every statement of value. This chapter asks for a **comparison record** beneath every comparison — the lines that let a reader see what was held equal and by whom. Ten lines, as a floor; the first is Chapter 4's declaration and the rest are this chapter's.

1. **What is held equal** — Chapter 4's ten-line comparability declaration for the indicator: same definition, unit, period, base, source, coverage, revision status; and, for a borrowed series, cash or real, who is still in it, and any breaks or vintages.
2. **The comparators, who chose them, and why this place** — the places or periods compared; the published method, its distance and its fixed number; or "chosen by the place", labelled; and whether the place was selected for the comparison because its figure was extreme.
3. **The standardisation** — what the figure has been adjusted for (size, age, mix), against what expectation; or "crude", stated.
4. **The order on the display** — whether the display sorts the places, by what, and whether the sort is a reading aid or the finding.
5. **The interval** — the uncertainty on each place's figure, and whether the places can be separated; the funnel's limits, if a funnel.
6. **The season and the smoothing** — raw, seasonally adjusted (by what method, revised when, series length) or trend-cycle (what window); and whether the latest point is an end-point estimate.
7. **The base month, and the record** — for any year-on-year figure, what happened twelve months earlier; for any series called "leading", its measured record of turning points against a named reference series.
8. **The design, and its assumption** — for any before/after: none (Design 2, with the eight rivals listed), interrupted series (impact model chosen when), difference-in-differences (comparison group; pre-trend check; adoption timing), synthetic control (weights; placebos run), cut-off, match; and the rung on the ladder.
9. **The ripple** — spillover and displacement considered; the neighbour used as a control, and whether it was reached.
10. **The future's terms** — for any projection: the label; the assumptions; the central estimate and range; the switching value; the number of scenarios; the optimism-bias adjustment and its source; the record of past projections against outturn.

A filled record, for an imagined comparison, so that the shape is clear. The comparison: *District A's venue closures per hundred venues were 9 last year; the city average was 5.* (1) Closures and venue stock from the same register, same twelve months, same definition of "active"; the register's deaths are provisional for two years. (2) The city's twenty-three districts; the comparison is with all of them, not with chosen peers; the nearest-neighbour set by the ONS method would be five named districts, not yet used. (3) Crude — not standardised for venue size, age of stock or type; a district of late-licensed venues is compared with one of cafés. (4) The display sorts districts by rate; the sort is the finding as shown, which is the thing this chapter forbids; the funnel is not yet drawn. (5) District A has thirty-four venues; three closures; the exact binomial interval on 9 per hundred runs from about 2 to about 24, and overlaps the city average. (6) Raw annual count; no season; the series is two years long and cannot be adjusted. (7) Not applicable to an annual rate; the previous year's rate was 3, and the district was chosen for attention because this year's was the highest. (8) No design; no claim of cause is made; if one were, it would be Design 2 with history (a rent review), instrumentation (the register's revision) and regression to the mean (chosen because extreme) among its rivals. (9) Two of the three closures reopened under new names in the neighbouring district; displacement, not loss, is a live reading. (10) No projection. Seven of the ten lines change what the number means, and the comparison as first shown — a sorted bar chart with District A at the top — would have been read as a finding about District A.

## 12 · Where the choice is yours

The truths fix the questions. These are the answers the chapter leaves open, because the evidence does not settle them or because they belong to the people who will live with the display.

**Which peer method, and how many peers.** Twenty by Euclidean distance on standardised variables, as the ONS does; ten by weighted distance with closeness labels, as the DfE does; or the studio's own set of variables, documented to the same standard. The literature settles that the method is published and fixed, not which it is. The variables are a judgement about what makes two scenes alike, and that judgement is the studio's and the scene's.

**Whether, and against what, to standardise.** Standardisation needs the drivers measured; for a scene the drivers (size, mix, hours, tenure) are partly the thing being compared. The choice between a crude rate with a wide caveat and a standardised ratio against a declared expectation is a choice about which misreading is worse.

**How the display orders.** A funnel sorted by size; a distribution with the place marked; a peer set in closeness order; an unsorted small multiple. All satisfy the rule; which one a first-time reader reads correctly is a thing to test, and the partial-order "incomparable" state most of all.

**The smoothing window, and whether to show the latest smoothed point.** The textbook says the window is the user's; Eurostat says the end-point should be withheld or warned. Either, declared.

**Which growth rate to lead with.** Month-on-month on the adjusted series when there is one; year-on-year with the base month shown when there is not; three-month on three-month as the ONS's own compromise. Each answers a different question; what is fixed is that the display says which and shows both ends of a year-on-year figure; which leads is the choice.

**Which design, when a cause is wanted.** The ladder orders them; the place's circumstances — one treated place or several, adoption at once or staggered, a cut-off or none, a long pre-period or a short one — choose among them. The Magenta Book's own feasibility table is the decision aid.

**How much to adjust for optimism, and how many scenarios.** The generic uplifts for costs and durations until the studio has a record, a declared judgement for benefits; the record thereafter. The cross-government toolkit says an even number of scenarios and the Bank of England shows a baseline with two; this chapter takes the toolkit's rule as the default and says so; which futures matter to the reader is the judgement.

**Whether to show a projection at all.** A dashboard of evidence may decide that the future is the reader's to imagine from the past the dashboard shows, and that any line drawn forward is a claim it would rather not make. That is a legitimate answer to section 9, and it is the studio's.

## 13 · An illustration from nightlife — method only

The same district as Chapter 6's: a reputation, a council hearing both asset and nuisance, a landowner assembling sites. This time the questions are comparative. Is this district doing better or worse than others? Is it getting better or worse? Did the licensing change two years ago do anything? What happens if the council does the thing it is considering? What follows is the method's walk through those four questions, and nothing about the answers.

*Better or worse than others.* The dashboard does not rank the city's districts on anything. For each rate it shows every district against its size — a funnel or an equivalent — with limits the data set and this district marked; or it shows the distribution with this district's position and its interval, and says whether the district can be separated from the average. The peers it compares against come from the published method, in a number that fits a field of this size, each with a label for how near; if the district's own people would choose different peers, those are shown beside, marked as chosen, and the choice is kept. Where the measure is a rate that depends on what kind of venues the district has, the dashboard either standardises against a declared expectation and compares the district with the expectation, or shows the crude rate with the reason it is crude. Where each of two districts exceeds the other on different measures, the display says they cannot be ordered. Identity measures — what the district sounds like, when its week peaks, who comes — are never on an ordered axis.

*Better or worse than it was.* The district's series are shown raw, with the season visible, until they are long enough to adjust — the official floor is three years — and are adjusted only if they are seasonal, by a named method, with the note that the adjusted history may change when the next month arrives. The trend line, when drawn, names its window and does not reach the last point, or reaches it with a warning. Year-on-year figures show both ends: this month, and what happened in the same month last year. Index series name their base and are read in per cent, not points; money series say cash or real. When a measure has moved for the better in a district that was chosen for attention because it had moved for the worse, regression to the mean is named beside it as the first explanation. No line is called a trend until the series' own noise allows it, and the dashboard says how long that is.

*Whether the licensing change did anything.* The before-and-after series across the change is shown with its eight rivals listed beside it — what else happened in the district that year, what was already changing, whether the register changed how it counted, whether the district was chosen because it was extreme — and no verb stronger than "after". If the studio wants more, the display is an interrupted series: the pre-trend drawn forward as the expectation, the shape of the expected effect recorded before the after-data were looked at, the co-interventions named, and a control series — a district with no change, or an outcome the change should not have touched — beside it. If several districts changed their licensing at different dates, the effect is shown by adoption group, never as one pooled number. If the studio wants the strongest claim a single district can carry, it builds the synthetic district — a named blend of other districts, weights on the face — checks that the blend tracked this district for years before the change, runs the placebos in time and in space, and shows the gap against the gaps the method finds where nothing happened. In every case the display says where it sits on the ladder, whether the neighbouring districts were reached by the change, and whether the effect is large enough, in a series this noisy, to be seen at all.

*What happens if the council acts.* If the dashboard shows a projection, it is labelled a projection; its assumptions come before its numbers; it is a central estimate with a range, or an even number of scenarios with a line saying what they do not cover; the value at which the conclusion flips is stated; the words "likely" and "unlikely" carry their numbers in the sentence; and the figure has been adjusted, in advance, for the known tendency of projections to be optimistic — by a declared adjustment, because the studio has no record yet, and with a note that the record starts now. If the dashboard shows no projection, it says why: that it shows what has happened and leaves what might to the reader.

What the dashboard has not done, at the end of the walk, is say whether the district is better than its neighbours, improving, helped by the licensing change, or wise to do what the council proposes. It has shown each comparison with what it held equal, who chose the comparators, what the interval is, which season and smoothing were applied, which design and which assumption, which ripples were considered, and what the future's terms are. The judgement is the reader's. The method's job was to make the comparison one the reader could trust.

## What proves a break works

The test for breaking a comparison rule is the same as the earlier chapters': **give the comparison and what accompanies it to someone who was not in the room and ask four questions — what is being held equal, who chose the comparators, could these two be told apart, and what else could have moved the line?** If the answers come from the face and one tap, the break has cost nothing. For the ranking rule the test is adversarial: crop the display as a hostile reader would, and see whether what survives is a league table; if it is, the rule was carrying weight. For the projection rules the test is later and arithmetical: a year on, compare the projection with the outturn and see whether the range held and whether the switching value was crossed. Log the break in the deviation entry Chapter 16 describes.

## What this chapter could not settle

- The comparison literature's founding article (Espeland & Sauder 2007) and the funnel plot's founding paper (Spiegelhalter 2005) were read at abstract only; the chapter used the authors' open book chapter and the official technical briefings that implement the funnel, and marks the canon †.
- The Analysis Function's chart standard ("Always rank bars by value") and the statistical guidance ("inappropriate to rank") pull against each other with no reconciliation in either; reconciled here through the funnel: order as a reading aid, position against limits as the finding.
- The two official traditions disagree on reading turning points (textbook: from the trend-cycle; Eurostat: withhold the end-point); both are shown, with the rule that the end-point carries a warning.
- No official rule on percentage change from a small base, on minimum series length (ONS), or on pre-specifying comparator groups in the public sector; the chapter says so and uses the nearest official wording.
- The Decision Book has no rules on rankings, standardisation, peers, seasonal adjustment, regression to the mean, the designs, spillovers, power, the ladders, or projections; this chapter's rules table is the candidate chapter on comparison.
- Lee & Lemieux dispute the "only at the threshold" limitation that the Magenta Annex states as fact; both are shown.
