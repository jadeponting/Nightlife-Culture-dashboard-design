# Chapter 14 — Where evidence meets expression

*Dashboard Design · Part 4: The boundary and the practice*

---

*Reading this chapter.* This is one chapter of *Dashboard Design*, Nightlife Culture's research book on how evidence becomes a display a reader can trust, read and act on. The studio ("the house" in some chapters) is Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio; the brief is its founder Carly Heath's commission for the book, and her eight ways a dashboard fails, the failure register, run through it. The Vibe Map, the studio's prototype for showing a place's nightlife culture, appears only as an illustration, never as a specification. "Version 1" is the book's archived first draft, whose positions this version re-tests against the sources. Every rule carries a kind (where it comes from: Standard, Evidence, Reading, Practice or Nightlife Culture) and a tier (how firm it is: Fixed, Adaptable or Decorative); Chapter 0 explains both, and the three senses of Fixed: a requirement that can be checked, a sequence whose order is the hard line, and a statement of the record. A dagger (†) marks a source read at the authors' abstract or summary only; "secondary" a quotation reached through a named reproduction; "(read once)" a quotation returned on a single reading and not confirmed by a second. Chapter 0 holds the glossary of the words the chapters share; each chapter lists its own coinages before its sources.

---

## The question

Where is the line between the evidence layer and the expressive layer of a public dashboard — what must stay true under any creative treatment, and how is that tested; what does the measured evidence on embellishment, affect and persuasion license an expressive treatment to claim, and on what conditions; what do the conventions of "objective-looking" display themselves do to a reader, and what follows for a house whose evidence standard is written against strong design; what does the strongest expressive work in the field do when each piece is read for what it breaks of this book's rules, what substitutes, and whether it was ever evaluated; and what does a dashboard about sound owe a reader who cannot hear it?

---

## The 60-second version

**The boundary has a formal name, from 1986.** Mackinlay's criterion: "A set of facts is expressible in a language if it contains a sentence that (1) encodes all the facts in the set, (2) encodes only the facts in the set." The second clause is the removal test. The classic case of a sentence that says too much is his own: "Most people perceive the lengths of the bars as an encoding of an ordered or quantitative set" (read once), so a bar chart of unordered categories encodes an order that is not there. Expressiveness asks whether the picture says what the data says and nothing else; effectiveness — which Mackinlay calls "conjectural" and, beyond quantitative data, "not been empirically verified" — asks how well a person reads it. The house's three tiers sit inside this: a Fixed rule about a chart is, in the main, an expressiveness rule (or a floor rule from Chapter 13); an Adaptable one is an effectiveness rule; Decorative is what neither criterion can see. Munzner's nested model says where the line falls — inside the encoding level, between the encodings that carry the abstraction (what is counted and compared) and the choices around them that change no fact — and why no amount of care at that level repairs a wrong abstraction above it: "an upstream error inevitably cascades to all downstream levels."

**Plain is also a style.** Kennedy, Hill, Aiello and Allen name four conventions that "imbue visualisations with a sense of objectivity, transparency and facticity": "a) two-dimensional viewpoints; b) clean layouts; c) geometric shapes and lines; d) the inclusion of data sources" — the last of which "performs the rhetorical, persuasive work of ascribing transparency and trustworthiness to visualisations." Data Feminism says the same more sharply: "The more plain, the more neutral; the more neutral, the more objective; and the more objective, the more true" — "or so this line of reasoning goes." Correll: "The clean lines and structured layouts of traditional visualizations communicate authority and certainty in implicit but measurable ways." The Style Guide's evidence standard — "Strong design must never make weak evidence look stronger than it is" — therefore cuts both ways. A plain chart with a source line at the bottom is making a claim about its own trustworthiness, and the claim has to be true. The conventions paper itself tested no readers: thirteen designer interviews and a semiotic reading of ten pieces; its claim about what readers perceive is an argument, and the chapter says so.

**What expression is measured to buy is engagement, mood and recognition — and, from Chapter 3's line, memorability — and not accuracy, and not action beyond a small effect on a hypothetical allocation.** Humanising the marks did not move people: across seven experiments of fifty participants each, Boy and colleagues "consistently find that anthropomorphized data graphics and standard charts have very similar effects on empathy and prosocial behavior"; the largest preregistered test since, with 786 participants, put the effect of an information-rich anthropographic on hypothetical allocation at 3.4 points in a hundred, 95% CI [0.021, 6.8] — an interval whose lower bound sits just above zero, and whose standardised form includes it. The ethics paper most cited for humanising data concedes it: person-shaped glyphs have "not been shown to produce any additional empathic response" (read once). Mood can be carried without a measured reading cost: animated charts built to express five positive feelings scored the same as a baseline animation on three comprehension questions and higher on engagement (197 readers; comprehension differences not significant, which is not equivalence); in a corpus of 976 infographics rated by 490 readers, negative feeling "are mainly caused by poor usability" and small type, so in that corpus feeling and legibility ran together; small palettes carry calm, excitement, seriousness and playfulness through lightness and chroma, while "Trustworthy showed very little structure." Visualisations that readers rate as images rather than as information are found "more aesthetically appealing, enjoyable, and pleasing" and convey less of their trend and message (250 online, 101 in person; correlational). And the desirable-difficulties argument for making charts harder on purpose does not survive the meta-analysis: across 3,135 participants, "there was no effect of perceptual disfluency on recall (d = − 0.01) or transfer (d = 0.03)", while it made people slower and less confident.

**Costs the register must price.** Dark grounds: 85 older and 84 younger adults read small text better dark-on-light, with "A positive polarity advantage was found for both age groups" (d = 2.17 for the young at threshold); the penalty appears in dark rooms and disappears in daylight-like light; for charts rather than text, which polarity helped was individual and preference did not predict it, so the sound position is "making visualizations available in both contrast polarities." Saying it twice: redundant coding yields "a large benefit for rapidly segmenting objects within a dense display", which grounds carrying a category in two channels and is a different claim from the twin's; and putting a text and a chart side by side did not raise accuracy (55, 56 and 59 correct of 87) and eye-tracking suggested readers "do not integrate information well across the two representation types" (read once) — the twin must be reachable as well as present. Reading in passing: the sign trade's planning average is thirty feet of reading distance per inch of capital height; four to eight words in "approximately four seconds"; "one-tenth of a second per letter"; a 1965 report summarised by a reviewer as "Only 3-4 short, familiar words can be read in a single glance at a sign"; in an unprimed threshold test of 33 fonts, capitals out-read lower case in all 31 fonts that had both. The "seven words" rule the trade repeats has no study behind it on any page that repeats it.

**The removal test — mono, silent, still, undressed, then stripped beside dressed — is run by official bodies under other names.** The Analysis Function's infographic rule is a plain-text twin: "provide a plain text alternative explaining all the information within the infographic", and a user test — "do they understand the story without explanation?" The ONS's motion rule: "never make an animation the only way to get the information." The W3C's own definition of decoration is a removal test in a sentence: "Text is only purely decorative if the words can be rearranged or substituted without changing their purpose"; and it has a third category the house's tiers lack, the "specific sensory experience", which "words cannot fully capture" (read once) and which must be identified in words rather than ignored. In the regulator's casework read here the line runs through encodings and not ornament: an inflation chart was "misleading" because "the y-axis beginning at 8%", and "This would still be the case had the axis been labelled clearly with a break"; the government's own teaching case is an infographic whose two houses — one "much larger" than the other, perhaps, the guidance suggests, "because it has more writing on it" — were read as the size of the numbers. Tufte, in his own hand, comes nearest to the two-layer position: "design architectures should be spare, straightforward, conventional, and self-effacing; AND the evidence presented should be rich, complex, multivariate, causal."

**The gallery holds, and almost none of it was tested.** Audited live, the strong work still leads with a written sentence, keeps provenance a scroll away, makes interaction optional and spends its craft on one authored thing; the Doughnut's authors print their own perceptual defect in the caption — "a quadratic scaling of wedge area that may lead some readers to perceive small changes as more significant than they are" — and supply a bar chart; Chalabi says her drawings are registered "pixel-for-pixel" to computed charts, and, in another interview, that "computer-generated images overstate certainty"; the museum records are real (*Wind Map* and *Noise Pollution* given by their designers; *Dear Data* bought with committee funds) and a MoMA label calls the field's subject "an expressive interpretation of reality" (read once). The evaluated exception survives, qualified: a 2026 peer-reviewed paper with 177 survey responses and 23,346 logged sessions, in which "20% of all analyzed sessions reach the flowchart's final node", stated preference (40 per cent for narration) and logged behaviour (54 per cent of sessions spent mostly exploring) diverge, there is no control, the raw survey is withheld, and the journal's own open-practices reviewer could not open the evaluation materials. The field's main prize has eight criteria — impactful, engaging, clarity, innovative, inclusive, accessible, effective, beautiful — and none of them is accuracy.

**A dashboard about sound owes its deaf reader a document.** Level A, in the standard's words: for prerecorded audio-only content, "An alternative for time-based media is provided that presents equivalent information"; where audio accompanies video, captions, in the Understanding text, "include non-speech information conveyed through sound, including meaningful sound effects"; and "If any audio on a web page plays automatically for more than 3 seconds, either a mechanism is available to pause or stop the audio, or a mechanism is available to control audio volume independently". *Silent* is one of the four removals, and for sound that is content the standard makes it a requirement rather than a test.

Beneath the dashboard the chapter leaves an eleventh record: the *boundary log*, an entry of eleven lines per expressive element naming what it buys, which encoding or claim it touches, what happened under each of the four removals, where its precise twin lives, how it is identified to a reader who cannot see or hear it, and whether it was ever tested with a reader. Shipping is gated by the four removals and the fifth check, run and recorded; the log stays open until readers who saw only the stripped version and readers who saw the dressed one agree on what the data said. And the division the brief asked for is section 11's: the whole register is the studio's, part of it priced by evidence; the evidence layer and the audit's order are not.

## 1 · What the boundary is, and what the chapter is not

Version 1 made the case, in its Chapter 13, that "the rules don't forbid the art; they name where it lives", and it located the art in two places: the Decorative tier and the register. This chapter takes the same position and tests it, because a position that says where expression lives has to say three further things to be usable. It has to say what the line is made of, so a designer can tell which side a given choice falls on. It has to say what is measured about the choices that fall on the expressive side, so the house can say what a treatment buys without claiming what it cannot. And it has to say how the line is checked, by a procedure that someone other than the designer can run and record.

**What the line is made of.** Section 3 finds that the field has had a formal statement of it since 1986 — Mackinlay's expressiveness criterion — and a model of where it sits since 2009 — Munzner's nested levels — and that the house's tier test is those two things in plainer words. This converts the house's "Fixed" from a stance into a definition: a Fixed rule is one whose breach makes the picture say something the data does not say, or fail to say something it does.

**What is measured on the expressive side.** Section 5 reads the affect, persuasion and perception evidence that Chapters 2 and 3 did not reach — humanised marks, affective design, affective colour, display polarity, redundancy, disfluency, physical and glanceable displays, reading at a distance — and finds that expression is measured to buy engagement, mood and recognition (and, from Chapter 3, memorability) and is not measured to buy accuracy or action. That finding is the licence's wording: a treatment may claim what expression is measured to give and may not be allowed to touch what it is not.

**How the line is checked.** Section 6 formalises the removal test and finds it in official guidance under other names, and section 10 writes the record that makes it auditable.

**What the chapter is not.** It is not a brief. Nothing here decides the Vibe Map's palette, type, imagery, sound, motion or opening; where the chapter walks through an example in section 12 the example is invented and the walk is the method. It is not a critique of the gallery's works as art; section 7 reads each for what it breaks of this book's rules and what substitutes, which is the only reading this book is entitled to. And it is not an argument that plainness is wrong: section 4 finds that plainness is a rhetoric, and the finding's consequence is that plainness, too, must earn its claim, not that it should be abandoned.

What the failure register asks of this chapter is the two failures that sit on either side of the line. "It fails if a viewer reads it and asks 'what am I looking at'" is the expressive layer eating the evidence layer. "It fails if it's just a fancy spreadsheet with some charts that tell you lots of big numbers, but not what actually happens" is the evidence layer with no expressive layer at all — accurate, and unread. The boundary is drawn so that neither happens, and the chapter's claim is that it can be drawn by rule and checked by procedure, leaving the whole of the expressive side to the people whose job it is.

## 2 · What people believe about expression and evidence, and what the evidence says

**Belief: decoration harms a chart.** Chapter 3 settled the embellishment line and this chapter cites it rather than re-running it: the measured verdict is that titles and text carry the message, memorable charts are often also effective, and the costs that are measured are costs of distortion, not of ornament. What this chapter adds is the official version of the same finding: the Analysis Function's list of what to avoid in a chart — "shaded backgrounds", "unnecessary borders", "patterns, textures and shadows", "3D shapes", "thick or dark gridlines" — is a list of things that touch the encoding or the reading, and its infographic guidance never uses the words "pictogram", "icon", "3D" or "decorative" at all; it governs infographics through user need, clutter and a plain-text alternative. Newsrooms and chart publishers forbid "3-D charts at all costs" (ProPublica) and "Stay away from 3-D" (Urban Institute); none of the house-style documents read forbids decoration by name. The regulator's casework is about axes, bar sizes, sources and context. Where decoration is forbidden it is forbidden because it distorts; the EU's public guide says it plainly — "a lot of chart junk is actually harmless: the message of the chart survives the embellishments and decorations" — and separates the harmless from what it calls, after Kosara, "harmfull junk" (spelling as printed), the kind that "actively interferes with the reading" (read once). *(Standard (official statistics); Practice; Reading.)*

**Belief: a chart that moves people moves them to act.** The one line of evidence that tests it directly — charts whose marks look like people, shown to people asked to feel and to give — finds little or nothing. Seven experiments found "very similar effects on empathy and prosocial behavior"; the largest since found at best "a small effect on money allocation" and said that "the large sample sizes required to observe an effect and the noise involved in measuring it make it very difficult to study in more depth" (†, read once). Section 5 gives the numbers. Chapter 2's persuasion line (Pandey; Liem; Allcott) stands: a chart can shift an attitude, and the shift is measured on the claim, not on the ornament. *(Evidence.)*

**Belief: plain is neutral.** It is a style, and the field's reading of it is that it persuades. Section 4. *(Evidence of method; Reading.)*

**Belief: the art end and the dashboard end are opposite ends of one scale, and a public display picks a point between them.** Kosara put pragmatic and artistic visualisation "on opposite ends of the sublimity scale" and "thus theoretically impossible to reconcile", then proposed "a third way". The EU guide puts Tufte and Holmes as "two extremes of a continuum" and lets "your own taste and style" choose the point. The house's tiers refuse the scale: a display has an evidence layer with an expressive layer on it, rather than a position between evidence and art, and the test is what remains when the layer is removed. The chapter argues this in section 3 and holds it as a stance in section 10. *(Standard (method); Reading.)*

**Belief: beauty earns a licence.** The field's prize has eight criteria and none of them is accuracy; a chart can be impactful, engaging, clear, innovative, inclusive, accessible, effective and beautiful by that rubric and wrong. The regulator's rubric is the reverse: encodings first, and "The misleading presentation of statistics cannot be dismissed as just a matter of opinion." (read once) The Decision Book already names "Beauty as an accessibility exemption" as "The most common failure in award-winning work." Section 7. *(Practice; Standard (regulator); Reading.)*

**Belief: dark is the night, so a dashboard about the night is dark.** The register may be; the text has a measured price, and so does the chart. Section 5. *(Evidence.)*

**Belief: harder to read is better remembered.** The 2011 paper that proposed "visual difficulties" for charts drew on a psychology effect that a 2018 meta-analysis of 25 empirical articles and 3,135 participants found null on recall and transfer, while it made readers slower and less sure; a 2024 preregistered replication found "null results for disfluency" (†, read once). *(Evidence.)*

**Belief: seven words.** The trade's rule for a sign read in passing is repeated on billboard companies' pages with no study behind it; the nearest research statement is three or four short words per glance, itself a reviewer's summary of a 1965 report, and the planning rule is a letter-height ratio and about four seconds for four to eight words. Version 1's "five words where possible, ten at most" is a house mantra and the chapter re-tiers it in section 5. *(Evidence; Practice.)*

**Belief: the hand-drawn look is a style choice.** It is also read as a cue about precision. Sketchiness "is a viable alternative for the visualization of uncertainty in lines and is as intuitive as blur" (†, read once); the practitioner whose illustrated statistical reporting won a Pulitzer says "computer-generated images overstate certainty" and that "You'll never see decimal places on my illustrations." On that evidence — one abstract and one practitioner — a drawn line is read as rough, and the house's position is that it should then be. *(Evidence, †; Practice; Reading.)*

## 3 · The vocabulary: what the field says expression is, and how it is judged

### Mackinlay: all the facts, and only the facts

The oldest formal statement of the boundary is in a paper about automating chart design. Mackinlay's 1986 *Automating the Design of Graphical Presentations of Relational Information* (ACM *Transactions on Graphics* 5(2)) separates two criteria. "Expressiveness criteria determine whether a graphical language can express the desired information." "Effectiveness criteria determine whether a graphical language exploits the capabilities of the output medium and the human visual system." Expressiveness has a precise definition: "A set of facts is expressible in a language if it contains a sentence that (1) encodes all the facts in the set, (2) encodes only the facts in the set." The second clause is the one most designers have never been told. A picture can fail by saying too much as well as by saying too little, and Mackinlay's example is a bar chart of a nominal variable: "The difficulty with this intuition is that the sentence may encode additional incorrect facts", because "Most people perceive the lengths of the bars as an encoding of an ordered or quantitative set" (read once). *(Standard (method).)*

Effectiveness is a different kind of question and Mackinlay is careful about its status. "Effectiveness criteria can be based on a number of different factors": "a design can be judged effective when it can be interpreted accurately or quickly, when it has visual impact, or when it can be rendered in a cost-effective manner". The ranking of perceptual tasks that the field has since treated as settled he presents with a caution: "The core of this conjectural theory is an observation, made by Cleveland and McGill" that "people accomplish the perceptual tasks associated with the interpretation of graphical presentations with different degrees of accuracy"; of the quantitative ranking "Cleveland and McGill empirically verified the basic properties", and the extension to ordinal and nominal data "has not been empirically verified." And from the ranking comes a principle the house will recognise: "Principle of Importance Ordering: Encode more important information more effectively." *(Standard (method).)*

The tier test is, for a chart's encoding, these two criteria in other words. A Fixed rule, in the Decision Book's language, is "uncertainty — there is a right answer": contrast passes or fails, a cell of two is suppressed or breached. Some Fixed rules are floor rules of that kind (Chapter 13) and some are disclosure rules (Chapter 10); the Fixed rules about what a picture says are expressiveness rules, and expressiveness is the same kind of thing: a picture either encodes all and only the facts or it does not, and a breach is an encoding that says something false or leaves something out. An Adaptable rule is "ambiguity — no single correct answer": type scale, grid, density, chart form within a task. Effectiveness is that: a matter of degree, of which perceptual task the reader is asked to do and how well people do it, with a ranking that its own author calls conjectural. And the Decorative tier is what neither criterion sees — everything whose presence or absence changes no fact encoded and no perceptual task required. Version 1's formula, "nothing load-bearing in decoration", is Mackinlay's second clause read from the other side: if removing an element changes what the picture says, the element was encoding a fact, and it was never decoration. *(Reading.)*

### Munzner: where the line sits, and why care below it cannot repair error above

Munzner's *A Nested Model for Visualization Design and Validation* (IEEE TVCG, 2009) puts four levels inside one another: "characterize the task and data in the vocabulary of the problem domain, abstract into operations and data types, design visual encoding and interaction techniques, and create algorithms to execute techniques efficiently". The model's purpose is to say what can go wrong at each level and how each is checked, and its central sentence is about direction: "an upstream error inevitably cascades to all downstream levels". At the encoding level "the threat is that the chosen design is not effective at communicating the desired abstraction to the person using the system", and "One immediate validation approach is to justify the design with respect to known perceptual and cognitive principles" — but "The immediate validations only offer partial evidence of success" and "none of them are sufficient to demonstrate that the threat to validity at that level has been addressed" (read once). The sentence the house should keep is this one: "If a poor choice was made in the abstraction stage, then even perfect visual encoding and algorithm design will not create a visualization system that solves the intended problem." (read once) *(Standard (method).)*

For this chapter the model does two things. It places the boundary: the evidence layer is the abstraction (what is counted, how it is defined, what is compared to what — Chapters 4 to 9 of this book) and the encoding that carries it; the expressive layer is a set of choices made at the encoding level and around it that do not change the abstraction. And it warns that the removal test is an encoding-level check and cannot substitute for the upstream ones: a beautifully stripped chart of the wrong measure is still the wrong measure, and a chart that passes every test in this chapter can still fail Chapter 9's. A compressed statement of the two principles — "expressiveness principle – match channel and data characteristics" and "effectiveness principle – encode most important attributes with highest ranked channels" — was read from her 2015 slides, since the textbook itself was not reachable; the version two course pages attribute to the book, "Visual encoding should express all of, and only, the information in the dataset attributes", is Mackinlay's clause made into a rule for students, and the chapter cites it as secondary. *(Standard (method); secondary.)*

### Kosara: the sublimity scale, and the picture that stands on its own

Kosara's *Visualization Criticism — The Missing Link Between Information Visualization and Art* (IV 2007) is a position paper, and the most useful thing in it for a boundary chapter is a three-part definition of what counts as a visualisation at all. "It is based on (non-visual) data." "It produces an image." "The result is readable and recognizable." Each has a body, and two of the bodies are rules. Under the second: "the visual must be the primary means of communicating the data. Other media can be part of a visualization, but the visualization must be able to stand on its own." (read once) Under the third: "Visualization images must also be recognizable as such, and not appear to be something else", and "The use of additional elements (or even "eye candy") is certainly possible, but must not take precedence over the communication goals of the visualization". The "stand on its own" sentence (read once) is about the image surviving without the other media that may accompany it, and the eye-candy sentence is about precedence; together they are the nearest published statement from inside the field to the position this chapter holds. *(Standard (method).)*

The paper's other contribution is a scale that the chapter's stance rejects, and it should be stated accurately before it is rejected. "One aesthetic criterion of particular interest is the sublime", which "can be understood as that which inspires awe, grandeur, and evokes a deep emotional and/or intellectual response". Technical visualisations "are thus designed to remove any sublimity, and instead foster immediate understanding"; "while the classical technical information visualization is entirely anti-sublime, artistic visualizations are primarily sublime". The sublime "subsumes the two criteria of readability and recognizability, since for a work of art to be sublime, it cannot be easily readable (or user friendly)"; "It must present enough of an enigma to keep an audience interested without being easy to solve". Pragmatic visualisation "is what we term the technical application of visualization techniques to analyze data"; "The goal of artistic visualization is usually to communicate a concern, rather than to show data"; "Visual efficiency does not play a role in artistic visualization, quite the contrary". And the two are "on opposite ends of the sublimity scale" and "thus theoretically impossible to reconcile, should not deter us from trying, though"; hence "a third way". He never uses the word continuum. *(Standard (method).)*

Where the house departs is on the geometry. A scale with two ends invites a public display to pick a point along it, and every point short of the pragmatic end gives up some readability for some enigma. The tiers say instead that a display has an evidence layer, which must be readable in full, and an expressive layer, which may be as sublime as its maker likes provided the first layer survives its removal. On that account the two things Kosara found irreconcilable are not on one axis at all; one sits on top of the other. Kosara's own second criterion — the picture "must be able to stand on its own" (read once) — is the sentence that lets the house say this with some support from him rather than against him. The chapter holds it as a stance in section 10, and names the cost: a display built this way can never be sublime in Kosara's sense, because it can always be solved. *(Reading; Nightlife Culture.)*

Kosara's four rules for criticism are also worth the house's attention, because section 7 is criticism and should be held to them: "Criticism must be expressed in a way that states the facts and presents them in the most balanced possible light"; "Every statement must be backed up by facts that can be independently checked"; "A critique must serve a goal"; and "The critics' own work can only be cited as an example if there are also other examples, and cannot be used as the gold standard." (read once) *(Standard (method).)*

### The artistic, the aesthetic and the casual

Viégas and Wattenberg's *Artistic Data Visualization: Beyond Visual Analytics* (HCII 2007) gives the definition the field uses: "Artistic visualizations are visualizations of data done by artists with the intent of making art" — "This definition may seem like a tautology, but in fact it specifies a coherent and interesting class of work." Two conditions follow. "First, the artworks must be based on actual data, rather than the metaphors or surface appearance of visualization"; and "we do not contend that beautiful scientific visualizations are automatically artistic, or that visualization art must be pretty". What the artworks share is "a forceful point of view", and "In a sense, the artworks derive their power from the fact that the artists are committing various sins of visual analytics." (read once) Then the question the chapter has to answer: "traditional analytic visualization tools have sought to minimize distortions, since these may interfere with dispassionate analysis" — "Is it possible that this focus on minimizing "point of view" is misguided?" — "A more radical change in attitude would be to embrace the fact that visualizations can be used to persuade as well as analyze". The paper contains no discussion of the sublime or of scale; those belong to Kosara. *(Standard (method).)*

The house's answer to their question is in two parts and both are already in this book. Yes, a public display has a point of view, and Chapter 6 made it a rule that the claim is stated in the title so the point of view is declared rather than smuggled. And no, a display that makes claims about a place's people may not commit what they call "sins of visual analytics" (read once) in its evidence layer, because its readers will act on it; the point of view lives in what is claimed and how it is framed, and the sins are reserved for the layer that can be removed. *(Reading.)*

Lau and Vande Moere's *Towards a Model of Information Aesthetics in Information Visualization* (IV 2007) could be read only in abstract: it "proposes a model of information aesthetics in the context of information visualization", "based on analysing existing visualization techniques by their interpretative intent and data mapping inspiration", and "reveals information aesthetics as the conceptual link between information visualization and visualization art" (†). Its two axes — data focus from intrinsic to extrinsic, mapping from direct to interpretive — are reported only through a 2024 secondary account and the chapter does not print them as the authors' words. Vande Moere and Purchase's *On the role of design in information visualization* (*Information Visualization* 10(4), 2011) likewise: "balancing the three requirements of utility, soundness and attractiveness within given constraints" and "a model of three potential roles for design" (†), with one sentence reaching the chapter second-hand: "complex and socially relevant issues might best be communicated to a large audience through popular media using an artistic and engaging visualization (even if its designer knows that such a method is not the most effective or efficient)" (secondary, read once). The chapter records the three requirements because they name the triangle a public display sits in; the full texts remain to be read. *(Standard (method), †.)*

Pousman, Stasko and Mateas's *Casual Information Visualization: Depictions of Data in Everyday Life* (IEEE TVCG 13(6), 2007) matters because it is the frame in which a public display read by non-analysts already sits. "Casual Infovis is the use of computer mediated tools to depict personally meaningful information in visual ways that support everyday users in both everyday work and non-work situations." Its four marks: "The user population is enlarged to include a wide spectrum of users from experts to novices"; "Systems are intended for usage that is momentary and repeatable (over weeks and months), or contemplative" — "(a long moment at an art gallery)"; "The data is typically personally important and relevant, as opposed to work-motivated"; and its evaluations "are not tied to productivity metrics like efficiency and effectiveness". Its four kinds of insight are the useful part for this book, because they say what a public display can be *for* beyond answering a question: "Analytic insight is the most traditional sense of insight that we find supported in visualization systems"; "Awareness insight is the insight that comes from maintaining awareness of a particular data stream"; "Social insight is insight about social networks, social situations, and social life" and "the sense of understanding of a social group and one's place in it"; "Reflective insight is insight about oneself, the world, and one's place in it". Chapter 12's whole subject — the reader looking for a named place — is social insight by this taxonomy, and it means the house can name what the expressive layer is for in the field's own terms: awareness, social and reflective insight, which analytic efficiency does not measure. *(Standard (method); Reading.)*

### The critical, the feminist and the ethical

Dörk, Feng, Collins and Carpendale's *Critical InfoVis: Exploring the Politics of Visualization* (alt.chi 2013) is an argument paper with no data, and its four principles are a check on the house's own position. "So far the research community has not given sufficient thought to how values and assumptions pervade information visualization"; the authors "outline a critical approach that promotes disclosure, plurality, contingency, and empowerment". Disclosure: "Creating visualizations involves a range of decisions about data, representation, and interaction" (read once); "Disclosing some of these decisions is a way to establish trust between visualization creators and viewers". Plurality: "exposing multiple facets and enabling a variety of interpretations is preferable to limited views and singular readings". Contingency: "tools should provide for a range of possible ways viewers experience a visualization". Empowerment: "Empowering visualizations should also allow viewers to question visual representations, utilize them to tell their own story" "and shift from awareness to action" (read once). They add: "These principles do not provide checklist-criteria to deem visualizations good or bad", and, under plurality, that "There may be situations in which the visualization designer deliberatively chooses to advocate a specific standpoint instead of offering a nuanced set of perspectives." (read once) The disclosure principle is the records beneath this book's dashboard, stated as politics; the chapter notes it and moves on. *(Standard (method).)*

D'Ignazio and Klein's *Data Feminism* (MIT Press, 2020), chapter 3, is where the argument against plainness is made most fully, and section 4 takes its central sentences there. What belongs here is its principle and its concession. The principle: "Elevate Emotion and Embodiment" — "Data feminism teaches us to value multiple forms of knowledge, including the knowledge that comes from people as living, feeling bodies in the world". Their account of a studio whose "work is framed around an emotion: loss", which "leads with loss, grief, and mourning", ends in a sentence the house should keep: "emotion and visual minimalism are not incompatible here". Their concession, under the subheading "Don't Never Do a God Trick" (read once): "there are also good reasons to use the god trick as a form of recuperation, contestation, or empowerment" (read once). And one sentence the chapter must mark as stronger than the evidence it found: "Scientific researchers are now proving by experiment what designers and artists have known through practice: activating emotion, leveraging embodiment, and creating novel presentation forms help people grasp and learn more from data-driven arguments, as well as remember them more fully." (read once) Nothing section 5 reads measures a memory gain from emotion or embodiment (memorability is Chapter 3's line, and it is about the chart's features, not the reader's feeling); the experiments do not support "grasp and learn more"; and on embodiment they find nothing. *(Standard (method); Reading.)*

Correll's *Ethical Dimensions of Visualization Research* (CHI 2019) states the premise the chapter works from: "All visualizations are rhetorical, and have the power to potentially persuade"; "All visualization research, no matter how superficially apolitical or trivial, has a moral character". Its nine obligations are imperatives — "We ought to visualize hidden labor"; "We ought to visualize hidden uncertainty"; "We ought to visualize hidden impacts"; "We ought to encourage "small data.""; "We ought to anthropomorphize data"; "We ought to obfuscate data to protect privacy"; "We ought to support data "due process.""; "We ought to act as data advocates"; "We ought to pressure or slow unethical analytical behavior" — and under the fifth he concedes the finding section 5 reports: "Proposed solutions to recenter human beings in data visualizations using person-shaped glyphs has not been shown to produce any additional empathic response". (read once) Correll and Heer's *Black Hat Visualization* (2017) supplies the framing a boundary needs most: the manipulations they catalogue are of "visualizations which have fidelity to the underlying data" — faithful, and still harmful — because "By choosing how to aggregate and normalize the same data, the designer of the visualization can control the message of the chart", and visualisation makers "are well-situated to perform "man in the middle" attacks". The removal test checks the encoding; it does not check the aggregation, and this book's Chapters 5 to 9 are where that check lives. *(Standard (method); Reading.)*

### Data humanism and *Dear Data*, as practice

Lupi's *Data Humanism* (PrintMag, dated on the page 30 January 2017, read once) is a manifesto, and the chapter reads it as practice rather than evidence. Its four headings on PrintMag — "Embrace Complexity"; "Move Beyond Standards"; "Sneak Context In (Always)"; "Remember That Data is Flawed (As We Are)" — differ in wording from the version on her own site, where the fourth reads "FINALLY, REMEMBER THAT DATA IS IMPERFECT. (AS WE ARE)"; the chapter quotes the PrintMag text. "We are ready to question the impersonality of a merely technical approach to data, and to begin designing ways to connect numbers to what they really stand for: knowledge, behaviors, people". ""Data-driven" doesn't mean "unmistakably true," and it never did"; "Data is a tool that filters reality in a highly subjective way"; "A dataset might lead to many stories"; "Data visualization should embrace imperfection and approximation". The phrase "Data is people" appears on neither page. Her method, stated: "What I always do when I start a new data project is to move away from the screen and start drawing." (read once) *(Practice.)*

*Dear Data*, in its makers' words: "Dear Data is a year-long, analog data drawing project by Giorgia Lupi and Stefanie Posavec"; "Each week, and for a year, we collected and measured a particular type of data about our lives"; "We prefer to approach data in a slower, more analogue way"; "we argue we can use data to become more humane and to connect with ourselves and others at a deeper level" "Instead of using data just to become more efficient". Posavec: "104 unique esoteric datasets and visualisation methods"; "The 104 postcards and accompanying preliminary sketchbooks for this project are now held in the permanent collection of the Museum of Modern Art, New York". Section 7 verifies the record. What the manifesto and the project give a boundary chapter is a stated purpose for an expressive layer — connection, reflection, the social and reflective insights of Pousman's taxonomy — and a stated condition on the data it carries, which is that the data is each maker's own. A public display that counts other people is not in that condition, and Chapter 10's safeguards record is where that difference is already written. *(Practice; Reading.)*

## 4 · The conventions of objectivity

### Four conventions that do work

Kennedy, Hill, Aiello and Allen's *The work that visualisation conventions do* (*Information, Communication & Society* 19(6), 2016) "argues that visualisation conventions work to make the data represented within visualisations seem objective, that is, transparent and factual". The four: "a) two-dimensional viewpoints; b) clean layouts; c) geometric shapes and lines; d) the inclusion of data sources". The claim is about the conventions and not the designers: "It is not visualisation designers who are creating this sense of objectivity, we propose, but rather the conventions on which they necessarily draw in producing visualisations". On each: "Two-dimensional viewpoints look objective even though they are not"; they "create the illusion of objectivity by disguising perspective". "The use of clean layouts to present data in visualisations creates an aura of clarity and simplicity" and "This aura of simplicity 'rubs off' on the data". Geometric shapes and lines result "in the dramatic and systematic simplification of data by highlighting only some of their characteristics" (read once). And the one the house must sit with: "Thus the inclusion of data sources performs the rhetorical, persuasive work of ascribing transparency and trustworthiness to visualisations", because, in the preceding sentence, "It suggests that the designer feels confident that s/he has been truthful to the data, and this in turn suggests that there may be no need for the user to check." (read once) Their conclusion: "Visualisation conventions sometimes work to undermine the good intentions of visualisers, by imbuing data with a sense of objectivity". *(Evidence of method.)*

The method bounds the claim. The study is "Skype interviews with 13 visualisations designers" and "detailed social semiotic analysis of 10 visualisations circulating in the media"; "Four of these 10 visualisations were produced by interviewees" (read once). No reader was tested. The focus groups belong to a sister paper from the same project (Kennedy, Hill, Allen & Kirk, *First Monday*, November 2016), which ran "nine focus groups with a total of 46 participants", aged "from 11 to 70", and found that "What constitutes effectiveness is fluid", "no single definition will apply across all engagements with visualizations", and the six factors that shaped engagement — "subject matter; source/media location; beliefs and opinions; time; emotions; and confidence and skills" (†, read once) — "should be seen as dimensions of effectiveness". One sentence from that paper the house should carry into any new form it adopts: "This type of visualization was new to many of our participants who therefore did not like it." (read once) So the claim that the four conventions make *readers* perceive objectivity is an interpretive argument from the designers' side and the semiotics of the pieces; what the reader side measured is that engagement depends on subject, source, belief, time, emotion and skill, which is consistent with the argument and does not test it. *(Evidence of method; Evidence.)*

### The same argument, made harder

*Data Feminism*'s chapter 3 names the reasoning and refuses it: "The more plain, the more neutral; the more neutral, the more objective; and the more objective, the more true" — "or so this line of reasoning goes" (read once). "Any communicating object that reflects choices about the selection and representation of reality is a rhetorical object"; "This includes visualizations that do not deliberately intend to persuade people of a certain message"; "It especially and definitively includes those so-called neutral visualizations that do not appear to have an editorial hand"; "In fact, those might even be the most perniciously persuasive visualizations of all!" "So if plain, "unemotional" visualizations are not neutral, but are actually extremely persuasive, then what does this mean for the concept of neutrality in general?" Their borrowed name for the posture is Haraway's "the god trick of seeing everything from nowhere"; "The view from nowhere—from a distance, from up above, like a god—may be data visualization's most signature feature"; "The god trick and its underlying assumptions about neutrality and truth are baked into today's best practices for data visualization". They report the conventions paper as "four conventions of data visualization reinforce people's perceptions of its factual basis", and the chapter notes that "reinforce people's perceptions" is a stronger description than the method supports, and that their gloss of the fourth convention as "the inclusion of data sources at the bottom" (read once) adds "at the bottom". And they quote the objection a reader will raise, in Cairo's words: "Is it clear to a general audience that what they see is the work of professionals who actively shape data to support a cause, and not the product of automated processes?" *(Standard (method).)*

Correll closes the loop between the two: "The clean lines and structured layouts of traditional visualizations communicate authority and certainty in implicit but measurable ways", citing Kennedy et al.; the chapter notes that "measurable" is his word and the measurement is the one described above. The same claim reaches the house from the practitioner side in a sentence the Sunlight Foundation wrote, in 2014, about its own style guide: "Charts that are well designed and professional are taken more seriously because it conveys an attention to detail that implies that similar care was taken with the data." That sentence is the convention admitting what it does. *(Standard (method); Practice.)*

Two further readings keep the finding in proportion. Quispel, Maes and Schilperoord (2018), interviewing ten professional information designers and reviewing 26 handbooks, found that "according to the information designers, clarity and aesthetics are the main criteria, with clarity being the most important" and that "They aim to objectively inform the public, rather than conveying personal opinions" (read once) — the designers do not experience themselves as persuading, which is the conventions paper's point. And Arunkumar, Padilla, Bae and Bryan (2023), with 500 annotated visualisations, 250 online participants and an in-person recall study of 101, found that "Image-rated visualizations were perceived as more aesthetically appealing, enjoyable, and pleasing" and that "visualizations internalized as images are less effective in conveying trends and messages, though they elicit a more positive emotional judgment" — the one reader study found that separates aesthetic pleasure from taking the message, and it cuts the other way from plainness: in that corpus, the more a display was rated a picture, the less of its trend was taken from it; the design is correlational. *(Evidence.)*

### What follows for the house

The Style Guide's evidence standard is one sentence: "Strong design must never make weak evidence look stronger than it is." Version 1 wrote its twin — strong evidence must never be made to look weaker by timid design — and this chapter accepts both and adds the sentence the conventions paper requires: plain design can make weak evidence look stronger too. A clean two-dimensional chart with geometric marks and a source line at the foot is, by the field's own account, making a claim about its trustworthiness that readers may take as a reason not to check. The house's position, held in section 10, is therefore not that the conventions should be dropped — they are also the conventions that make a chart readable, and Chapter 13 found that the plainest forms are the ones disabled readers can use — but that each of them must be earned. A source line earns its place when the source is reachable and the provenance set (Chapter 4) stands behind it; a clean layout earns its aura when the recipe card (Chapter 5) and the claim record (Chapter 6) exist; a two-dimensional view earns its look of having no perspective when the frame (Chapter 7) says which comparison was chosen and why. The conventions of objectivity are a promise about the records beneath the dashboard, and the ten records of Chapters 4 to 13 are what makes the promise true. *(Nightlife Culture; Reading.)*

Two consequences for the chapters behind this one. Chapter 12 found that sceptics of a consensus used "orthodox" visual aesthetics — Lee et al.'s finding, cited there — and the conventions paper offers the reason: the plain look is available to anyone, carries its aura regardless of the data beneath it, and so cannot be the house's evidence of trustworthiness; the records are. And Chapter 11's beauty-to-trust path (Lin & Thornton) now has a plain-to-trust path beside it; both are routes by which a look does the work a record should do, and the house's answer to both is the same. *(Reading.)*

## 5 · What the affect and persuasion evidence licenses

Chapter 3 settled the embellishment line and Chapter 2 the persuasion line; the chapter cites both and reads what lies beyond them. The question in every subsection is the same: what does this measured effect let an expressive treatment claim, and what does it tell the house the treatment may not be allowed to touch?

### Humanising the marks

The direct test of whether a chart that shows people as people moves its readers is the anthropographics line, and it is the clearest null in this chapter. Boy, Pandey, Emerson, Satterthwaite, Nov and Bertini's *Showing People Behind Data* (CHI 2017) ran "seven experiments" on Amazon Mechanical Turk, recruiting "50 participants" for each, measuring "empathic concern, personal distress, perception of story-protagonists' responsibility, and perception of justice of donating" on seven-point scales, plus donation likelihood and allocation (read once). The finding: "Contrary to our expectations, we consistently find that anthropomorphized data graphics and standard charts have very similar effects on empathy and prosocial behavior." They write that "anthropographics are neither truly beneficial, nor detrimental" and that such charts "can afford to be abstract". *(Evidence.)*

Morais, Jansen, Andrade and Dragicevic followed with a design space (TVCG 2020) and a preregistered large-sample study (CHI 2021). The design space — read through Sorapure (2022) and the authors' own catalogue, since the paper itself was unreachable — has "seven dimensions in two broad categories: what is shown and how it is shown"; "the four dimensions are granularity, specificity, coverage, and authenticity" and "the three dimensions are realism, physicality, and situatedness" (secondary); it was built from "an analysis of 115 visualizations collected from newspapers, websites, and research papers" (read once). It is a vocabulary for what humanising varies and not evidence of what it does. The 2021 study's abstract: prior findings "have been inconclusive so far"; "We tested an information-rich anthropographic against a simple bar chart, asking participants to allocate hypothetical money in a crowdsourcing study"; "We found that the anthropographic had, at best, a small effect on money allocation"; and "Such a small effect may be relevant for large-scale donation campaigns, but the large sample sizes required to observe an effect and the noise involved in measuring it make it very difficult to study in more depth." (†, read once) The numbers are on the authors' published analysis pages. Experiment 1, 128 participants: primary effect "3.8, 95% CI [-1.7, 9.3]". Experiment 2, "786" submissions across four conditions: the effect of information richness on allocation "3.4, 95% CI [0.021, 6.8]" on a 0 to 100 scale; the effect of anonymising the people shown "0.14, 95% CI [-3.3, 3.5]"; the standardised effect of richness about 0.14 with an interval that includes zero, and a probability of superiority of about 0.54 (read once). The authors' later summary: "The results from our studies call for caution in the belief that current design strategies for anthropographics are substantially effective than using other charts." (read once, grammar as printed) *(Evidence.)*

Two framings of the same data sit beside the null. Campbell and Offenhuber (*Information Design Journal* 25(1), 2019), read in abstract only, report "appeals to emotion through proximity techniques – the contextual framing of a visualization": "people feel greater interest towards a topic when the visualized data are more relevant to them" and "data representing events closer in time are more affecting" (†). That is a content choice — which data, framed how — rather than a styling of the marks, and it is Chapter 12's finding about the named place arriving from the affect side. And Correll's concession, quoted in section 3: person-shaped glyphs have "not been shown to produce any additional empathic response" (read once). *(Evidence, †; Reading.)*

What this licenses. An expressive treatment that shows people as people may claim recognition and register — and may not claim, on the evidence, to make readers feel more or give more. The failure register's "it fails if it's an aggregation of 'the biggest'" is answered by what is counted and framed (Chapters 7 and 12), not by what the marks look like. And Chapter 10's finding applies with full force: one mark per person is a re-identification risk the safeguards record must price, and the evidence gives it nothing in return that a bar would not give. *(Reading.)*

### Mood without a measured cost

The affective-design line asks the other question: can a display carry a feeling without a reading cost? Lan, Shi, Wu, Jiao and Cao's *Smile or Scowl?* (TVCG 2021) rated a corpus: from 1,526 infographics "We identified 976"; "We recruited 490 participants from MTurk" (245 in the first study, read once), "resulting in 117,120 ratings in total"; the analysis identified "12 infographic-associated affective responses" — happy, surprised, excited, content, awestruck, hopeful; sad, concerned, shocked, overwhelmed, bored, annoyed (read once) — and two families of design factor, "Usability, and Expressiveness". The finding that matters here: negative responses "are mainly caused by poor usability (H1), especially low accessibility (H1-1), small font size"; and "Positive affects such as happy and content may occur when infographics present a comprehensible data visualization (H1-1-1), a clear layout (H1-1-3), and detailed explanations (H1-3-3) to the audience." (read once) In this corpus bad feeling tracked unreadability and good feeling tracked comprehensibility. The study is correlational and its heuristics were checked with fifteen designers; it does not show that feeling causes reading, only that in practice the two were not opposed. *(Evidence.)*

The same group's *Kineticharts* (VIS 2021) built "60 animated charts for the five affects" — "joy, amusement, surprise, tenderness, and excitement" (read once) — and tested them twice: "80 college students (55 females), aged from 18 to 41" for affect recognition, and "197 participants who speak English as their first language from Prolific" for expressiveness, engagement and three comprehension questions (the paper cites four aspects from Bateman et al. and asks about three, read once). The comprehension result, in full because its status matters: "Mann-Whitney U tests showed that Kineticharts earned similar scores in people's comprehension of the story subject (M = 2.92), data categories and values (M = 2.22), and trend (M = 2.85) with the baseline (M = 2.90, 2.18, 2.90, respectively), and the differences were not significant (p = .737, .257, .348, respectively)." (read once) The abstract's claim is that the charts "enhance user engagement without hindering data comprehension compared to the animation design from DataClips" (read once). Three cautions: only positive affects were built; the baseline was another animation, not a static chart; and a non-significant difference is not demonstrated equivalence. With those, it is the one reachable study that measured feeling and comprehension together and found no detected cost; the same pass reports engagement ratings, including focused attention, significantly higher than the baseline (read once), which is the one measured attention gain in this chapter. *(Evidence.)*

Lan, Wu and Cao's review, *Affective Visualization Design* (TVCG 2023), "kept 109 papers" and "identified 61 projects", built a design space of "design fields (where), design tasks (what), and design methods (how)" (read once), and listed ten tasks — inform, engage, experiment, provoke, advocate, socialize, heal, empower, commemorate, archive (read once) — and four strategies, "sensation, narrative, behavior, and context". Its sentence on the question this section asks is this: "In several user studies [44, 47], researchers indeed observed that affective visualization design was not at odds with data comprehension." (read once) The field's own survey rests the "no conflict" claim on two studies; the chapter reports it at that weight. The review also names the open problem: "the power of emotion may be exploited by people with malevolent intents", alongside "no data visualization is truly objective" and "emotion is not separate from, but is a huge part of rationality". *(Evidence of method.)*

Lee-Robbins and Adar (TVCG 2022), from twelve interviews ("we invited 17 people to participate in an interview, of which 12 accepted"), give the house a way to write an affective intent down so it can be checked. Their verbs: "observe, position, strengthen, connect, and behave" — the paper's names for what a viewer is intended to do, which the house would define before use — and their template, "The viewer will [verb] [noun]" (read once). The last verb is the one the evidence in this section does not license as a purchase for a styling choice. Their observation is the reason the template is needed: "visualization designers rarely acknowledge or formalize affective objectives", because "such goals are often considered a violation of perceived 'neutrality' or are 'political,' designers may resist or be unable to describe these intents, let alone formalize them as learning objectives." (†, read once) The boundary log in section 10 asks for exactly this sentence per expressive element. *(Evidence of method.)*

Colour. Bartram, Patra and Stone's *Affective Color in Visualization* (CHI 2017) analysed "the palettes of 8608 images", had "76 persons" choose five-colour palettes for a bar chart or a map, and "38 users" pick best and worst palettes per affect, across Calm, Exciting, Positive, Negative and four "pragmatic" affects, "Serious, Playful, Trustworthy and Disturbing" (read once). Results: "Calm, Playful and Positive were much lighter than Disturbing, Serious and Negative"; "Calm was the least saturated of the affects" and "makes strong use of cool colors (blues and greens) and is overall high lightness and low chroma"; "Exciting emphasizes warmer and higher chroma colors: more reds and yellows" (read once); "Negative, as anticipated, has more browns, dark reds, and greys" (read once); and "Trustworthy showed very little structure". A small data palette carries a mood through lightness and chroma, in these samples of 76 and 38, and the one affect a public body might most want to encode in colour had no clear colour signature. Anderson and Robinson (TVCG 28(8), 2022), read in abstract, tested "affectively congruent colors" on maps with "both objective (pattern detection; area comparison) and subjective (affective quality; appropriateness; preference) measures" and found that congruent colours "amplify perceptions of the affective qualities of maps with emotive topics, affective incongruence may cause confusion" (†); the abstract claims no accuracy gain from congruence. Blair, Wang and Perin (TVCG 2024), five crowdsourced experiments, found that "certain data characteristics influence the emotion of viewers even when the data has no meaning" and that "The effect of color palette on emotion (for both arousal and valence) is larger than that of data trend" (read once) — the palette moved feeling more than the data's trend did, which is the strongest reason in this chapter for keeping palette out of the evidence layer. And Lan's *The Chart Excites Me!* (TVCG 2023), 265 visualisations and 184 participants with "all texts were blurred to exclude the influence of semantics", found "four design features (e.g., colorfulness, the number of different visual channels) cross-validated as important features correlated with affective arousal" — correlations, the authors say, "rather than causal effects" (read once). *(Evidence.)*

What this licenses. A register may carry a mood, and the evidence says it can do so in motion without a detected reading cost in the one study that measured both, and in colour with a measured gain in felt quality and a possible confusion cost when the colour is incongruent (†). The conditions are the ones the studies themselves impose: the mood is carried in channels that encode nothing (palette, motion that means nothing, type); the data's own channels stay plain; and the intent is written down in a sentence that can be checked. The register's measured risk is Blair's: colour moved feeling more than the data's trend did, so a palette chosen for mood and applied to a scale is an encoding of feeling laid over an encoding of fact, and the removal test will find it. *(Reading.)*

### The dark ground

Version 1 called the dark room part of the culture's visual tradition. The chapter does not dispute the tradition and prices the ground. Piepenbrock, Mayr, Mund and Buchner (*Ergonomics* 56(7), 2013): "Participants were 85 older and 84 younger adults" (60 to 85 and 18 to 33), tested on acuity and on proofreading 250-word texts in ten-point type; "A positive polarity advantage was found for both age groups"; "The presentation in positive polarity is recommended for all ages"; "Dark characters on light background lead to better legibility and are strongly recommended independent of observer". The effect at threshold: d = 2.17 for the younger group, 0.58 for the older. The mechanism: "The brighter display leads to a greater pupillary contraction that, in turn, reduces the effects of spherical aberrations and increases the depth of field" (read once). Buchner and Baumgartner (*Ergonomics* 50(7), 2007), abstract: "proofreading performance was consistently better with positive polarity"; "This positive polarity advantage was independent of ambient lighting"; "colour contrast ( red text on green background) could not compensate for a lack of luminance contrast" (†). Dobres, Chahine and Reimer (*Applied Ergonomics* 60, 2017), abstract: legibility thresholds "were highest for the negative polarity configurations under dark ambient illumination, indicated worse performance", while "the positive polarity conditions under dark ambient illumination and all conditions under bright illumination demonstrated significantly reduced thresholds, indicating greater legibility" (†, read once). The two disagree on lighting: in sustained proofreading the advantage held regardless of ambient light; in glance-like reading the dark-ground penalty appeared in a dark room and not in bright light. Neither favours the dark ground. *(Evidence.)*

For charts rather than text the picture is different and the house should know it. While and Sarvghad (VIS 2024 short paper), with participants above and below sixty, found that "the contrast polarity that led to better performance did not always match their preferred polarity", an "average percent difference of around 36%" between a person's better and worse polarity, and that "the polarity that led to better performance and the resulting amount of improvement varied on an individual basis, with each polarity benefiting comparable proportions of participants" (read once); their recommendation is "making visualizations available in both contrast polarities". The abstract's participant count (134) does not match its own breakdown (69 and 66), and the chapter reports the discrepancy rather than a number. A practitioner synthesis (Nielsen Norman Group, 2020) takes the text evidence's side for people with normal vision — performance "tends to be better with light mode" — and notes that "some people with cataract and related disorders may perform better with dark mode" (read once). *(Evidence; Practice.)*

The official line is light. The Analysis Function: "We recommend using white as a background colour."; "All our palettes are designed to be used with a white background."; "Some users may find a white background difficult to look at because of glare or the high contrast of colours against it."; and the admission, "A perfect solution would be to have data visualisations that can be fully customised to meet specific user needs." "As this is not possible at the moment we recommend a white background because it provides a helpful 'anchor' in general." (read once) The ONS lists "backgrounds" among chart decoration to avoid. The GOV.UK Design System has had an open dark-mode issue since December 2018. The one UK public body found publishing palettes for dark grounds is the Greater London Authority's City Intelligence guide (version 2.0, May 2019): "All colours work on a dark background" (read once); "Darker categorical colour palette on a dark application background" (read once); it does not say when a dark ground should be used, and its own first rule is "Remove decoration and effects." *(Standard (official statistics); Standard (design system).)*

What this licenses. A dark register for a page is a Decorative-tier choice until text or a chart sits on it, at which point it has a measured price: small text read worse on it in both age groups tested, and worst, in one study, in a dark room; for charts the better polarity is a property of the reader. The condition the evidence sets is the one While and Sarvghad state and the Analysis Function wishes for: both polarities available, with the reader's choice honoured. Chapter 13's floor already carries user override of colour and contrast; this is the measured reason it is not negotiable for a dark register. *(Reading.)*

### Saying it twice, and whether the twin is read

The Decision Book's precise-twin rule rests on an assumption that redundancy helps. The measured ground is Nothelfer, Gleicher and Franconeri (*Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance* 43(9), 2017), a set of small lab experiments (twelve to seventeen participants each) on telling apart marks in a dense field. The prior state they report: redundant coding "may carry costs, and there is no direct evidence for a benefit". Their finding: "this practice can indeed yield a large benefit for rapidly segmenting objects within a dense display", with a boundary — "when one dimension, such as color, is more easily discriminable, adding shape differences will likely not yield a redundancy advantage." (read once) Saying the same thing in two channels helps the eye separate groups in a dense field, in small lab samples; it is measured support for carrying a category in two channels, and it is a different claim from the one the twin rule makes. *(Evidence.)*

The twin rule says: beside the expressive form, ship the precise one. Whether a reader uses it is measured once, and the answer is cautionary. Ottley, Kaszowska, Crouser and Peck (EuroVis 2019), eye-tracking a Bayesian reasoning task with "32 participants" (29 analysed, three problems each, which is the 87): "We found no significant effect of presentation on accuracy"; "Text = 55/87 correct, Vis = 56/87 correct, Text +Vis = 59/87 correct"; and "These tendencies persist even when text and visualization are presented together, indicating that users do not integrate information well across the two representation types." (read once) In that task, showing both forms did not make readers use both. Stokes, Setlur, Cogley, Satyanarayan and Hearst (TVCG 2022), abstract, had "302 participants" rank "univariate line charts containing varying amounts of text" and found "heavily annotated charts were not penalized" (†, read once) — text on the chart was preferred, not punished. *(Evidence.)*

What this licenses, and what it conditions. The twin is still required — Chapter 13 found it is the only route some readers have — but the evidence says a twin placed beside an expressive form is not thereby read, so the twin must be reachable from the form (a link, a toggle, the table route), its finding must be stated in words on the form itself, and the form must not rely on the reader going to the twin to correct an impression the form gives. Version 1's "name the purchase, ship the precise version alongside" becomes, in section 10, "name the purchase, state the finding in words on the form, and make the precise version one step away." *(Reading.)*

### Harder is not better

Hullman, Adar and Shah's *Benefitting InfoVis with Visual Difficulties* (TVCG 2011) is a position paper, and the position is that "non-efficient visual elements may benefit comprehension and recall on the part of users", "providing a counterpoint to efficiency-based design theory with guidelines that describe how visual difficulties can be introduced"; the authors "characterize effective graph design as a trade-off between efficiency and learning difficulties", and warn that difficulty "may induce unproductive levels of frustration". The borrowed term is psychology's "desirable difficulties" (read once). No direct visualisation test of the proposal was found. The underlying effect has been tested: Xie, Zhou and Liu's meta-analysis (*Educational Psychology Review* 30(3), 2018), "based on 25 empirical articles involving 3135 participants", found "there was no effect of perceptual disfluency on recall (d = − 0.01) or transfer (d = 0.03)", while disfluency did "reduce participants' judgments of learning (d = − 0.43) and increase learning time (d = 0.52)". A preregistered replication (Weissgerber, Terhorst & Rummer, *Metacognition and Learning* 19, 2024, N = 96) reports "null results for disfluency, WMC, and their interaction for all learning outcomes" (†, read once). The practitioner rebuttal, from Few (2011): "Making visualizations of data unnecessarily difficult to read and interpret leads to unnecessary cognitive effort, without benefit." (read once) *(Evidence.)*

What this licenses: nothing. A treatment that makes the evidence layer harder to read may not claim a memory benefit for it; the measured result is slower reading and less confidence for the same recall. Kosara's enigma — "enough of an enigma to keep an audience interested without being easy to solve" — is available to the expressive layer and forbidden to the evidence layer, and this is the evidence for the prohibition. *(Reading.)*

### The physical and the glance

Jansen, Dragicevic and Fekete (CHI 2013) "present the first infovis study comparing physical to on-screen visualizations" and found that "moving visualizations to the physical world can improve users' efficiency at information retrieval tasks", while "augmenting on-screen visualizations with stereoscopic rendering alone or with prop-based manipulation was of limited help" — the gain seems to come from properties "such as their ability to be touched and their perfect visual realism". Sixteen participants ("Sixteen participants were recruited", confirmed in the authors' 2018 re-analysis, which found "The study's findings appear to be robust to choices in statistical analysis"). The abstract was the only part of the paper reached, so its sentences carry †. The gain was for three-dimensional bar charts made physical against the same charts on a screen; it says nothing about physical over flat. Version 1's physical layer — posters, cards, the workshop wall — is licensed by it only as a different medium with its own rules, and the Decision Book's condition that a digital equivalent exists stands. *(Evidence; Reading.)*

Blascheck, Besançon, Bezerianos, Lee and Isenberg (TVCG 25(1), 2019) measured how fast a two-value comparison can be read from a watch-sized chart: eighteen participants in each of two studies; "Participants' task was to select the larger of the two targets" (read once) on bar, donut and radial bar charts of 7, 12 and 24 values. Thresholds: "< 300 ms for the bar chart, < 220 ms for the donut chart, and in < 1780 ms for the radial bar chart"; "a vast majority of smartwatch interactions last under 5 s"; and the second study's thresholds "were on average 1.14–1.35× higher than in the first study", with the authors noting their figures are "a lower bound because we used a static watch" (read once). One comparison can be made in a fraction of a second from a conventional form; the radial form took about six times longer, in this one study of eighteen people, while the donut was as fast as the bar — so the cost is of a form the eye reads badly, not of unfamiliarity as such. That ratio is the kind of number the boundary log should hold for any form the house invents. *(Evidence.)*

### Reading at a distance and in motion

Version 1 took the club poster as the display's genre, and a poster is read in passing, at a distance, with the reader's attention elsewhere; the only literature on those conditions is the sign trade's, which is why a dashboard chapter reads it. Garvey, Pietrucha and Meeker (1997) is the Clearview font study, not a mixed-case study: in it the authors "exposed 48 older drivers to high-brightness guide signs displaying either the current or the Clearview font" and found "nighttime recognition distances 16% greater than those allowed by the Standard Highway Series E(M) font" "without increasing overall sign dimensions" (†); its abstract says nothing about case. The case finding reaches the chapter second-hand: the sign-industry standard refers to "the 15 percent increase in letter height required when all upper case letters (all caps) are used instead of upper and lower case letters with initial caps, a difference in recognition distance documented in earlier studies" (read once), and a 2024 *Human Factors* study found "No support for differences in the legibility distance between titledcase and all-uppercase", while "the recognition distance was significantly longer with titled-case" (secondary, read once). *(Evidence, †; secondary.)*

Against that, Garvey, Eie and Klena (*Interdisciplinary Journal of Signage and Wayfinding* 1(1), 2016), a threshold-legibility study: "Sixty-four subjects from 19-87 years of age viewed 64 displays using 33 fonts", "with uppercase often performing significantly better than lowercase"; "For all the 31 fonts that had upper and lowercase conditions, the uppercase words had higher mean LIs than the lower"; "In 22 of the cases, that difference was statistically significant". The authors reconcile the two literatures: in a recognition task "the reader knows what word he or she is looking for and merely has to match a mental image of that word with the word on the display", while in a legibility task "the reader does not know what the display will say and therefore has to read all or most of the individual letters to build the word" (read once). Also: "The choice of serif versus sans-serif alone does not have an important effect on display legibility." (†, read once) So the two literatures do not fully agree: at threshold, with the word unknown, capitals read better in this study; in recognition, with the word known, title case was found further; and the 2024 study found no difference in legibility distance at all. For a public display whose headline is new to its reader, the rule of thumb for mixed case is weaker than it looks, and the question is open. *(Evidence.)*

The planning numbers. The sign-industry standard (Bertucci & Crawford, *Best Practice Standards for On-Premise Signs*, 2015, as republished by the United States Sign Council Foundation) defines the legibility index as "representing the distance in feet for every inch of capital letter height at which a sign may be read", says "an average Legibility Index value of 30 may be used", that "signs displaying four to eight words in simple typography can be comfortably read and comprehended in approximately four seconds", and allows "one-tenth of a second per letter". Bullough's 2017 review records values of 30, 35, 40 and 50 from different sources. The United Kingdom's Traffic Signs Manual, Chapter 7, sizes by x-height — "The size of an alphabet is specified in terms of its x-height"; "The overall size of a sign is determined by the chosen x-height", which "will depend on the type of sign and, in most cases, the 85th percentile speed" — and limits content: a sign "should not indicate more than three directions as the sign would then become difficult to read". Words per glance: Bullough's summary of Forbes, Snyder and Pain (1965) is "Only 3-4 short, familiar words can be read in a single glance at a sign" — a reviewer's sentence, not the 1965 authors'. The "seven words" rule appears on billboard companies' pages — "say it in seven words or less" (read once); "Stick to seven words or less" (read once) — with no study cited on either; no primary trade document was found. *(Standard (method); Evidence; Practice.)*

Museum labels are the nearest literature to a display read standing up. Bitgood's 2000 review organises "three principles of attention" (selectivity, motivated focusing, limited capacity, read once) and reports others' numbers: "Adult visitors were found to read an average of only 18 percent of the labels" (Borun & Miller 1980), though "they did read 68 percent of the printed material on the exhibits at which they chose to spend a longer amount of time" (read once); a median exhibition time of "8 minutes" (Serrell 1998); and that "breaking down a long label into three smaller ones (chunks), resulted in increased reading" (Bitgood & Patterson 1993, read once). *(Evidence, secondary.)*

What this licenses. Version 1's rule — "five words where possible, ten at most" — is a house mantra, and the measured numbers around it are a letter-height ratio for distance, about four seconds for four to eight words, three or four words per glance, and capitals at threshold. The chapter re-tiers the mantra as Adaptable with the sign literature as its grounds, and keeps the two Fixed things the literature does support: a headline's letter height is set from its reading distance, and a headline that must be read in passing is a few short words whose finding is restated in the body at reading distance. *(Reading.)*

### The hand-drawn look as a claim

Boukhelifa, Bezerianos, Isenberg and Fekete (TVCG 18(12), 2012), abstract: "sketchiness is a viable alternative for the visualization of uncertainty in lines and is as intuitive as blur; although people subjectively prefer dashing style over blur, grayscale and sketchiness" (†, read once). The study tested sketchiness as an uncertainty signal and is not a credibility study; its finding is that a drawn line works as an uncertainty cue. The practitioner evidence agrees from the other side. Chalabi, whose illustrated statistical reporting won the 2023 Pulitzer Prize for Illustrated Reporting and Commentary — the citation: "For striking illustrations that combine statistical reporting with keen analysis to help readers understand the immense wealth and economic power of Amazon founder Jeff Bezos" — describes her method in three interviews: "Then I load the charts into Photoshop" and draw "to line up pixel-for-pixel with the computer-generated graphics", "so they're as accurate as any computer graph that you're going to see anywhere else" (Dezeen, 16 November 2023); "computer-generated images overstate certainty"; "my hand-drawn graphics show the real margin of error"; "It's transparent about its lack of certainty." (It's Nice That, 8 March 2018); "You'll never see decimal places on my illustrations"; "They're just trying to communicate the imprecision of the data that underlies it." (Nightingale, 1 November 2019). Also, in the Dezeen interview: "I try to produce journalism where I'm explaining my methods to you." and "Every single word that you add to an image reduces engagement". The two statements — as accurate as a computed chart, and showing the margin of error — are reconciled by her method: the computed chart fixes the values and the drawing's roughness carries the uncertainty on top of them. None of her published pieces could be read, so whether they carry a source line is not reported. *(Evidence, †; Practice.)*

What this licenses. The drawn register is available, and it comes with a claim attached that the evidence layer must honour: a drawn mark says the number beneath it is rough. Where the number is rough — an estimate, a modelled figure, a small base — the register and the data agree and Chapter 6's uncertainty rule is served by the form. Where the number is exact, a drawn mark understates it, and the removal test in section 6 asks of any such mark whether its precise twin is one step away. Chalabi's method is itself the removal test run in reverse: the computed chart exists first and the drawing is registered to it. *(Reading.)*

## 6 · The removal test, formalised

### Mackinlay's second clause as a procedure

The test the Decision Book names (BRK-07, "Run the removal test for the relevant chapter") and version 1 put in four words — "print the page mono, silence the audio, disable the motion, strip the poster layer — the evidence must stand complete in what remains" — is Mackinlay's expressiveness criterion run as a procedure. It has a step before it that the four words leave implicit: the sort. The designer declares, for each element of a display, which layer it is in — evidence (it carries a fact) or expressive (removing it would, on the designer's claim, change no fact). The removal test then checks the claim for every element declared expressive. The claim under test is that the display, with the expressive layer removed, still encodes all the facts and only the facts. The method is to remove, one at a time, each channel the expressive layer uses, and after each removal to ask two questions: is anything the data says now missing (a fact that lived only in the removed channel), and does anything the picture now say differ from what it said before (a fact that the removed channel was adding)? The two answers mean different things. A missing fact means the sort was wrong: the element was carrying a fact and belongs in the evidence layer, where it is judged as an encoding by effectiveness and must meet the floor; it goes back, and the test is run again. An added fact is a breach of the second clause: the element made the display say something the data does not, and it is fixed or removed. In both cases the answer names the channel that was load-bearing. The test is the designer's to run and the log's to record; a reader meets only its result. *(Reading; Standard (method).)*

The four removals, and what each tests.

*Mono.* Render the display in greyscale. Tests whether any fact lived only in hue: a category told apart only by colour, a scale whose direction only colour gives, an emphasis carried only by a warm against a cool. Chapter 13's floor already demands this for colour-blind readers; here it is a test of the expressive palette, which is licensed to carry mood (section 5) and forbidden to carry a fact. The Decision Book's own print rule, "Check greyscale as well as colour if it will be printed", is the same removal for a different reason.

*Silent.* Mute it. Tests whether any fact lived only in sound. For a dashboard about sound the removal is also a requirement, because the W3C makes it one at Level A (section 8). Any sonification is a route beside a text route (Chapter 13); any ambient sound is Decorative and must be stoppable.

*Still.* Disable motion, as a reader who has set reduced motion would. Tests whether any fact lived only in a transition, a build, or an animated change between states. The ONS's rule is the test stated as an instruction: "never make an animation the only way to get the information", and "make sure there is a way for the user to control the animation, for example, to pause or reset and navigate through the frames".

*Undressed.* Strip the register: the display type, the illustration, the pattern, the imagery, the poster layer, the dark ground, the house's whole Decorative tier. What remains should be the evidence layer in the plainest form the house allows — the committee export. Tests whether the register was carrying a claim: a headline whose meaning depended on an image beside it; a scale whose direction a texture gave; a comparison implied by a layout the dressing made.

The fifth check is a comparison rather than a removal. Put the stripped display beside the dressed one and ask whether they say the same thing. Mackinlay's second clause is about facts encoded in addition, and the dressed version can add a fact the stripped one does not carry — a size that reads as a quantity, an ordering that reads as a rank, an image that reads as a cause. The government's own teaching case is this exact failure, below.

### The official bodies run it under other names

*The plain-text twin.* The Analysis Function's infographic guidance never says "removal test", and prescribes one. "Infographics can be effective but can be difficult to do well."; "For example a simple chart with some explanatory text could be clearer and more accessible for your users."; "If you have lots of information to communicate, an infographic is probably not the best choice." "Infographics are normally produced as PDF or image files. Generally, these are not accessible." Therefore: "The content within the infographic should also be available in an accessible format."; "For infographics published on webpages, a good way to do this is to provide a plain text version on the same webpage."; "provide a plain text alternative explaining all the information within the infographic". And the user test: "Test your infographic with users – do they understand the story without explanation?" GOV.UK's content guidance says the same: "If you do use an infographic you should provide a plain text version of the content." The guidance's negative is as instructive as its rule: the infographics page itself never uses the words "pictogram", "icon", "3D" or "decorative", and no rule about pictograms or icons of people was found on any of the five Analysis Function pages read; the official line governs the form through need, clutter and the twin. *(Standard (official statistics).)*

*The two senses of "decorative".* The Analysis Function also uses the word in its technical sense, and the two must not be confused. Its charts checklist: "Marking images of charts as decorative means they have nothing in the alt attribute. This means a screen reader user will skip directly from the chart title to the text alternative (which should be directly underneath the chart)." Its charts guidance: "In this situation the image of the chart should be marked as decorative and therefore hidden from screen reader software." Here "decorative" is a status given to the chart image once its content lives in adjacent text — the chart has passed its own removal test, so the picture may be skipped. Chapter 13 reported this rule; what this chapter adds is that it is the removal test's logic made into markup. *(Standard (official statistics); Reading.)*

*The W3C's definition.* Under 1.1.1: "If non-text content is pure decoration, is used only for visual formatting, or is not presented to users, then it is implemented in a way that it can be ignored by assistive technology." "Pure decoration" is "serving only an aesthetic purpose, providing no information, and having no functionality", and the note on text is the test in a sentence: "Text is only purely decorative if the words can be rearranged or substituted without changing their purpose." The example: "The cover page of a dictionary has random words in very light text in the background." The W3C's images tutorial, the precedent the UK systems follow: "Decorative images don't add information to the content of a page."; "Whether to treat an image as decorative or informative is a judgment that only the author can make, based on the reason for including the image on the page."; the bullet "Illustrative of adjacent text but not contributing information ("eye-candy")"; and for "graphs and diagrams: To convey data or detailed information, provide a complete text equivalent of the data or information provided in the image as the text alternative." *(Standard (W3C).)*

*The third category.* The standard has a slot the house's tiers lack. "If non-text content is primarily intended to create a specific sensory experience, then text alternatives at least provide descriptive identification of the non-text content." The definition: "a sensory experience that is not purely decorative and does not primarily convey important information or perform a function"; "Examples include a performance of a flute solo, works of visual art etc."; the intent: "Sometimes content is primarily intended to create a specific sensory experience that words cannot fully capture." (read once) This is where an authored opening image, a sound piece or a data artwork on a public page belongs, and it is neither of the house's two non-Fixed tiers: it may not be ignored, as decoration may, and it need not carry the data, as the evidence layer must; it must be identified in words. Section 10 adds the category to the register. *(Standard (W3C); Reading.)*

*Design systems.* GOV.UK's Design System, written for services: "Avoid using images for unnecessary decoration."; "Only use images if there's a real user need."; "images should only be visual aids – written content must contain all the information the user needs to complete the service."; "Do not use photography to represent abstract concepts, such as to convey an impression or emotion to the user."; "Use as few visual elements as possible to communicate your message."; "Avoid depicting people in illustrations."; "Avoid using icons in most cases."; "People can understand a single icon to mean different things, so they can be confusing." GOV.UK's content guidance, by contrast, defines decorative images — "generic images which do not give any additional information to the content" — and permits them; "Do not use decorative images." appears nowhere on it. The NHS: "Use icons sparingly, and not for decoration."; "Use images only if there is a real user need. Avoid unnecessary decoration."; and the observation that "users with some access needs (such as dyslexia) navigate health information through images" (read once). The ONS, in one manual, says both things the house says: for charts, "Do not add any other chart decoration, such as borders or backgrounds, as these distract users from the data and message."; "Only include the data that are needed to show the important trends clearly and accurately."; "Only consider using an interactive chart where the most important information for the user cannot be clearly shown through a non-interactive chart."; and for brand, "Icons should be used to support content, not replace it."; "They should not be used in isolation, as the meaning can be interpreted in different ways by different people."; "Our icons use simple geometric shapes and rounded corners to convey trustworthiness and professionalism, but with a friendly feel." Its brand pattern is a data-derived decoration — "We are taking the most basic representation of data, a dot, and turning it into an identity"; "A grid of dots that uses scale to represent numbers 0 to 15 in binary to produce a seemingly random pattern that still feels structured."; "Basing the pattern on data ties in with our purpose of producing and presenting statistics."; listed use, "background decoration"; "positive emotional messages (circles seem softer and more welcoming than harshly angled shapes such as triangles)" — and the page says nothing about applying it to charts. The ONS's aims for its charts include "engaging". The house's tiers are close to the public sector's practice stated as a rule: no decoration in the encoding; feeling permitted, and reasoned about, in the brand; nothing carried by an icon alone — with one official sentence, GOV.UK's on photography and emotion, that the house's Decorative tier does not adopt, because it was written for a transactional service. IBM's Carbon states a two-register rule for motion and pictograms — "Productive motion creates a sense of efficiency and responsiveness, while remaining subtle and out of the way."; "Expressive motion delivers enthusiastic, vibrant, and highly visible movement."; "Reserve expressive motion for occasional, important moments"; "expressive pictograms should be used selectively and only in scenarios that call for a graphic with strong presence." — which is the Decorative tier with a budget attached. *(Standard (design system).)*

### The regulator's line runs through encodings

The Office for Statistics Regulation's casework is the removal test's fifth check applied to real graphics, and it never mentions ornament. On a Treasury chart: "We consider that the way the tweet presents this data to be misleading."; "The graph gives a misleading impression of the scale of the deceleration in inflation, due to the y-axis beginning at 8%."; "This would still be the case had the axis been labelled clearly with a break to signify the gap."; "An important role of data visualisation is to aid understanding of trends in data by showing them in their relevant policy context." (27 February 2023). On election charts: "Starting the vertical axis for such charts at zero for each party is generally advisable in this regard."; bars "should directly and accurately reflect the number or proportion of votes being cast or the opinion poll support being registered, rather than exaggerating the closeness or distance between the support for particular parties" (read once). On a social-media infographic: "The y axis on this infographic was not labelled and it also didn't start at 0."; "the lines appear to suggest a bigger relative increase in pay for newly qualified nurses than the data justify."; and "This infographic has no identifiable source" (24 May 2024, read once). And the sentence that settles whether any of this is taste: "The misleading presentation of statistics cannot be dismissed as just a matter of opinion." (30 August 2024, read once), relaying a High Court judgment that statistics "should not be misused so as to mislead". The Code of Practice 3.0 itself has one practice naming visualisation — "Provide a clear description of the main messages with suitable data visualisations" (9.2) — and one line on colour, "Don't present too much information, use confusing colours or jargon" (read once); its Public Use standards never mention a chart. The regulator's line on visuals lives in casework, and in the four items read the casework is about axes, bar sizes, sources and context: the evidence layer. *(Standard (regulator).)*

The government's teaching case is the fifth check failed. The Analysis Function's advice for policy professionals, section 10, "Presentation really matters": "Poorly chosen presentation methods can hide things or mislead and confuse people."; "Statistical graphics in government are increasingly self-contained."; "In 2014, the Department for Work and Pensions press office published an infographic with a press release." in which one house "is much larger than the house on the right which represents 50,000 households" while the left house "represents 12,000 households" (read once); "The positive message from the press release was lost in discussions about inappropriate use of graphics."; "Even worse was the suggestion that the government were trying to mislead the public on purpose."; "An innocent explanation for the size of the houses might be that one is bigger than the other because it has more writing on it."; "But the authors had not thought about how people might interpret the image." A container sized, on the guidance's own suggestion, to fit its words was read as an encoding of the number inside it: a decorative choice that became a fact the data did not say, which is Mackinlay's second clause breached by an illustration. *(Standard (official statistics).)*

### Tufte, in his own hand, and the continuum refused

Tufte's books were not reachable and the chapter quotes his rules only as others quote them. What could be read in his own words is a 2006 note on his site, and it is the nearest thing in his hand to the two-layer position this chapter holds: "If possible, design architectures should be spare, straightforward, conventional, and self-effacing; AND the evidence presented should be rich, complex, multivariate, causal." The next sentences (read once): "The idea is to maximize the viewer's content reasoning time, and to minimize the design figuring out time and to reduce impediments to content reasoning."; and that this theme "runs through the first 3 books … the 'data-ink ratio' … micro/macro designs, and layering and separation … and the 'smallest effective difference'". As quoted at secondary sources: "The interior decoration of graphics generates a lot of ink that does not tell the viewer anything new."; "The purpose of decoration varies—to make the graphic appear more scientific and precise, to enliven the display, to give the designer an opportunity to exercise artistic skills."; "Above all else show the data"; and the "duck", a graphic "taken over by decorative forms or computer debris, when the data measures and structures become Design Elements, when the overall design purveys Graphical Style rather than quantitative information" (secondary). The phrase "Erase non-data-ink, within reason" could not be found verbatim anywhere reachable and the chapter does not print it as his. *(Practice; secondary.)*

The EU's public data-visualisation guide states the position the house refuses, and states it well: "A sane way of looking at the Tufte versus Holmes is to see them as two extremes of a continuum."; "How much ink you can and should spend on non-data elements in your designs depends on the medium, the audience, the topic and also of your own taste and style."; "If you are after readable values, effective comparisons, and a scientifical look: go towards minimalistic."; "If you want engagement, some humour and memorability, go more for the Nigel Holmes style." It separates "harmless" junk — "the message of the chart survives the embellishments and decorations" — from what it calls, after Kosara, "harmfull junk" that "actively interferes with the reading" (read once), and concedes of the 2010 study it leans on that "what the researchers did not study, was how well people were able to read data values from both kind of charts" (read once). The house's answer is the same as to Kosara's scale: the continuum describes a dial; the tiers describe two layers, and the removal test is how a reader tells which layer a given mark is in. On a continuum, "taste and style" set the point; under the tiers, taste and style have the whole of one layer and none of the other. *(Practice; Nightlife Culture.)*

### What the newsrooms forbid, and permit

Practising newsrooms forbid distortion by name and decoration almost never. ProPublica: "Avoid 3-D charts at all costs."; "Humans have enough problem perceiving area accurately."; "Avoid pie charts, with one exception: Use them to show the relationship between part and whole."; "Don't show interpolated or predicted data without saying you're doing so." (read once). The Urban Institute, a research institute with a published house style: "Stay away from 3-D."; "The 3-D effect available in many software tools actually distorts the data values because the effect is not actually 3-D but rather a perspective rendering of 3-D."; and its logo on every chart is a provenance device — "so that when others copy and paste our charts, Urban is identified as the source", which is Chapter 12's cut-out rule from a publisher of charts. The *Economist*'s own confession groups its "crimes against data visualisation into three categories: charts that are (1) misleading, (2) confusing and (3) failing to make a point", with mistakes headed "Truncating the scale"; "Forcing a relationship by cherry-picking scales"; "Choosing the wrong visualisation method"; "Confusing use of colour"; "Including too much detail" (read once); none is decoration. The FT's Visual Vocabulary exists "to assist designers and journalists to select the optimal symbology for data visualisations" and is "at the core of a newsroom-wide training session aimed at improving chart literacy"; "This is not an attempt to teach everyone how to make charts, but how to recognise the opportunities to use them effectively alongside words." (read once). It has one explicit licence for the pictogram, with a truth condition: "Isotype (pictogram): Excellent solution in some instances – use only with whole numbers (do not slice off an arm to represent a decimal)." And Datawrapper's rule for colour as emphasis: "Color is our most powerful tool to control where the reader looks."; "If a category is as important as other, colorful ones, don't make it gray." *(Practice.)*

The FT's pictogram condition is the removal test for a humanised mark in one clause: the mark may be a person so long as each person is one whole unit, because a sliced person encodes a fraction the data does not express as a fraction of a person. It is a newsroom's statement of Mackinlay's second clause. *(Reading.)*

## 8 · The deaf reader of a dashboard about sound

Chapter 13 read the W3C's criteria for an interactive data display and flagged the deaf reader to this chapter, because a dashboard whose subject is heard as much as seen may carry sound as content — recordings, sonifications, a narrated route — and the floor was written for the eye. The criteria that bite, under four headings, in their normative words and with the Understanding pages' intent text marked as such (WCAG 2.2, read through the Understanding pages, which reproduce the criteria):

**1.2.1 Audio-only and Video-only (Prerecorded), Level A.** "For prerecorded audio-only and prerecorded video-only media, the following are true, except when the audio or video is a media alternative for text and is clearly labeled as such:" — prerecorded audio-only: "An alternative for time-based media is provided that presents equivalent information for prerecorded audio-only content." The defined term: a "document including correctly sequenced text descriptions of time-based visual and auditory information and providing a means for achieving the outcomes of any time-based interaction"; "audio-only" is "a time-based presentation that contains only audio (no video and no interaction)". Intent: "to make information conveyed by prerecorded audio-only and prerecorded video-only content available to all users", because text "can be rendered through any sensory modality (for example, visual, auditory or tactile) to match the needs of the user." The word "transcript" appears only in the examples; the requirement is the document, and the page states no exemption for music. *(Standard (W3C).)*

**1.2.2 Captions (Prerecorded), Level A.** "Captions are provided for all prerecorded audio content in synchronized media, except when the media is a media alternative for text and is clearly labeled as such." The intent text: "Captions not only include dialogue, but identify who is speaking and include non-speech information conveyed through sound, including meaningful sound effects."; the definition's note: "Captions convey not only spoken dialogue but also equivalents for non-dialogue audio information, including sound effects, music, laughter, speaker identification and location." (read once) *(Standard (W3C).)*

**1.2.3 Audio Description or Media Alternative (Prerecorded), Level A, and 1.2.5 Audio Description (Prerecorded), Level AA.** "An alternative for time-based media or audio description of the prerecorded video content is provided for synchronized media"; at AA, "Audio description is provided for all prerecorded video content in synchronized media." — the blind reader's side of the same content, which Chapter 13's routes already cover. *(Standard (W3C).)*

**1.4.2 Audio Control, Level A.** "If any audio on a web page plays automatically for more than 3 seconds, either a mechanism is available to pause or stop the audio, or a mechanism is available to control audio volume independently from the overall system volume level." The note: "Since any content that does not meet this success criterion can interfere with a user's ability to use the whole page, all content on the web page (whether or not it is used to meet other success criteria) must meet this success criterion." (read once) Intent: "Individuals who use screen reading software can find it hard to hear the speech output if there is other audio playing at the same time."; "Therefore, it is important that the user be able to turn off the background sound."; and the W3C's preference, "that the sound be started by an action initiated by the user after they reach the page, rather than requiring that the sound be stopped by an action of the user after they land on the page." (read once) *(Standard (W3C).)*

Two design systems say the same in fewer words: Material, "Give visual alternatives to sound, and vice versa." (read once); Carbon, "Find an alternative way to convey information exclusively with sound." and "Transcribe and caption all videos and animations that have meaningful audio." (read once). *(Standard (design system).)*

What follows is three rules, one distinction and one observation. Any sound that is content — a recording, a sonified series, a narrated route — has a text document equivalent to it, at Level A, with no exemption for music; any sound that accompanies video is captioned, including non-speech sound; and no sound starts by itself for more than three seconds without a stop. The distinction is the W3C's own, and the author makes it: sound that conveys information is content and owes the document; sound that is "primarily intended to create a specific sensory experience" — the W3C's flute solo — owes "descriptive identification" in words; and a dashboard about a culture's sound will carry mostly the first kind, because its recordings are evidence of something. The observation is that *silent*, the second of the four removals, is for sound-as-content a requirement before it is a test: the standard asks the house to build the silent version as a document, and the removal test then checks that the dressed version added nothing the document lacks. The flowchart paper, the gallery's evaluated exception, has fifty-two minutes of narration and no sentence on a transcript; the house's probes should not repeat that silence. *(Reading.)*

Chapter 13 reported that sonification is a route beside a text route and never instead of it, from the blind reader's side. This section adds the deaf reader's: the same rule, for the opposite reason. A sonified series without its document excludes one reader; a sonified series without its chart excludes the other; the series, the chart and the document are three routes to one view, and the view record (Chapter 11) lists all three. *(Reading.)*

## 9 · The failures, read for the boundary

The brief's register, each failure read for what this chapter adds.

**"It fails if a viewer reads it and asks 'what am I looking at'."** This is the expressive layer eating the evidence layer, and the removal test is its diagnosis: if the stripped version is readable and the dressed one is not, the dressing is in the way. Section 5 adds the measured form of the failure: visualisations readers rate as images are found more pleasing and convey less of their message; and one badly read form cost about six times a bar's glance in one study. The focus-group authors said it of their participants in one sentence: "This type of visualization was new to many of our participants who therefore did not like it." (read once)

**"It fails if it's just a fancy spreadsheet with some charts that tell you lots of big numbers, but not what actually happens."** The other side of the line: an evidence layer with no claim stated and no register at all. Chapter 6 answered it with the claim in the title; this chapter adds that plainness does not make the numbers true and that the ONS's own aims for a chart include "engaging". Version 1's twin to the Style Guide line stands: strong evidence is never made to look weaker by timid design.

**"It fails if it looks the same every month."** Not this chapter's, except in one respect: an expressive layer is the thing most tempting to change for change's sake, and the Decision Book already names "The bespoke annual encoding" as a failure — "Invent a new form each year and you have made your own work incomparable with itself." The register may change; the encodings do not.

**"For nightlife, it fails if they do not recognise their place within the front-end interface."** Chapter 12's, and this chapter sharpens one part of it: recognition is social and reflective insight in Pousman's terms, which the expressive layer can serve and the evidence layer cannot by itself, and the FT's pictogram condition is the test for any humanised mark that serves it — whole units only.

**"It fails if people don't use it."** The affect evidence says what expression is measured to buy — engagement, mood and recognition, and from Chapter 3 memorability — and these are the use failure's levers, within the conditions section 5 sets. It also says what it does not buy, which is action; a display that is used is not thereby acted on.

**"It fails if it breaks or isn't reliable."** The expressive layer is the least reliable layer on a page: fonts fail to load, images are blocked, scripts are off, audio is muted, motion is disabled by preference. A display whose evidence survives the four removals is one whose evidence survives those failures, which is Chapter 8's service record arriving by another route.

**"It fails if people don't trust it."** Section 4's finding is the hard one: the plain look makes a trust claim readers may take as a reason not to check, and so does the polished look (Sunlight's sentence), and so does beauty (Chapter 11). None of the three is evidence of trustworthiness; the records beneath the dashboard are, and the conventions of objectivity are a promise about them. Chalabi's practice names the one way the register can carry a trust claim that is true: a drawn mark that says, accurately, that the number is rough.

**"It fails if it's an aggregation of 'the biggest'."** Not this chapter's. The one thing it adds is that humanising the marks does not answer it; the choice of what is counted and framed does.

## 10 · What holds true regardless

Seven things this chapter can state for any public dashboard, each with its kind and its grounds.

**1. Expression is tested by what remains when it is removed.** Sort each element into a layer by the designer's claim; strip the expressive layer — mono, silent, still, undressed — and the evidence layer must still encode all of the data and only the data (a lost fact sends the element back to the evidence layer; an added fact is a breach); then put the stripped and dressed versions side by side and they must say the same thing, judged by readers asked what the data said. For sound that is content, *silent* is a Level A requirement before it is a test. *Fixed (requirement).* Grounds: Mackinlay's expressiveness criterion ("encodes all the facts in the set" and "encodes only the facts in the set"); Kosara's "must be able to stand on its own" (read once); the W3C's definition of decoration ("rearranged or substituted without changing their purpose"); the Analysis Function's plain-text twin and user test; the ONS's "never make an animation the only way to get the information"; WCAG 1.2.1 and 1.4.2; the government's own two-houses infographic (section 6) as the fifth check failed.

**2. Expression is measured to buy engagement, mood and recognition — and, from Chapter 3's line, memorability — and is not measured to buy accuracy, and not action beyond a small effect on a hypothetical allocation.** A treatment may claim what it is measured to give and may not claim, or be built as if it gave, what it is not. *Fixed (requirement)* (the house's reading of the state of the evidence, which new evidence can move). Grounds: Boy et al. ("very similar effects on empathy and prosocial behavior"); Morais et al. (3.4 points in a hundred, 95% CI [0.021, 6.8]); Kineticharts (no detected comprehension cost; engagement higher; not equivalence); Lan 2023 ("not at odds", on two studies); Bartram (mood in lightness and chroma; "Trustworthy showed very little structure"); Arunkumar (image-rated displays convey less); Xie et al. (disfluency null on recall; slower; less confident). Counter-grounds the house records: Chapter 2's persuasion line, which is about the claim and the frame.

**3. Plainness is a register too, and makes a trust claim that must be true.** The conventions of objectivity — two dimensions, clean layout, geometric marks, a source line — do persuasive work, and a source line in particular suggests "there may be no need for the user to check". So the house's evidence standard reads three ways: strong design never makes weak evidence look stronger; timid design never makes strong evidence look weaker; and plain design never stands in for a record. *Fixed (requirement).* Grounds: Kennedy et al. 2016 (13 interviews; 10 pieces; no readers tested — "there may be no need for the user to check", read once); *Data Feminism* chapter 3; Correll; the Sunlight Foundation's 2014 sentence; the ten records of Chapters 4 to 13 as the thing the conventions promise.

**4. A register that signals imprecision may be worn only where the data is imprecise.** A drawn, sketched or approximate mark is an encoding wearing a register, and the register is read as a cue about the number beneath it; where the number is an estimate the form and the data agree; where it is exact the form understates it and its precise twin is one step away. *Adaptable.* Grounds: Boukhelifa et al. (sketchiness works as an uncertainty cue, †, read once); Chalabi's stated method ("pixel-for-pixel"; "computer-generated images overstate certainty"; "You'll never see decimal places"); Lupi's "embrace imperfection and approximation" read as a stance about her own data.

**5. A dark ground is priced, and the price is paid by the reader's choice.** Small text read worse light-on-dark in both age groups tested, and in glance-like reading the penalty appeared in a dark room; for charts, which polarity helps is individual and preference does not predict it. A dark register ships with the light one and the reader chooses. *Fixed (requirement)* (the choice), *Adaptable* (the register, which is Decorative until text or a chart sits on it). Grounds: Piepenbrock et al. (85 and 84; d = 2.17 and 0.58); Buchner & Baumgartner (†); Dobres et al. (†, read once); While & Sarvghad ("making visualizations available in both contrast polarities"); the Analysis Function's "anchor" and its wish for customisation; Chapter 13's floor.

**6. The twin is required and is not thereby read.** Every expressive form has a precise twin one step away; the form itself states its finding, and any distortion it carries, in words on itself; and no form relies on a twin the reader must go and find to correct an impression the form gives. *Fixed (requirement).* Grounds: Ottley et al. (text and chart together did not raise accuracy; not integrated, read once); Stokes et al. (text on the chart not penalised, †, read once); the Decision Book's Chapter 7; the Doughnut's caption and Figure 2 as the rule done by its authors.

**7. The field's prize scores no accuracy; the regulator's casework scores encodings, sources and context; the house audits in the regulator's order — accuracy, then the floor, then beauty.** Beauty is never a licence, and the strongest expressive work in the field holds by carrying its message in words and structure, spending craft on one authored thing, making interaction optional, and — in the one case where its makers checked — publishing its own defect with a twin. Almost none of it was evaluated. *Adaptable (house default).* Grounds: the eight IIB criteria, with none on accuracy; OSR's casework and "cannot be dismissed as just a matter of opinion" (read once); the gallery audit; the flowchart paper's terms.

Three things the chapter will not state as truths. That expression harms comprehension (the one study that measured feeling and comprehension together found no cost; the costs that are measured are of distortion, of forms the eye reads badly, and of displays taken as pictures). That the plain look is wrong (it is the look disabled readers can use; it must be earned rather than dropped). And that any of the above says what the Vibe Map should look like.

### Beneath the dashboard: the boundary log

Each earlier chapter left a record beneath the display; this one leaves the eleventh, and it is a log rather than a sheet, because it grows by one line each time an expressive element is added. The *boundary log* records, for each expressive element, what the removal test found. It is the Decision Book's override log ("Trigger · rule and tier · alternative chosen · evidence") specialised to the line this chapter draws, and it is where version 1's Chapter 13 said a leaked meaning "wants the entry". It has eleven lines per element. The four removals and the fifth check gate shipping; the reader comparison in the last line may follow shipping in a probe, and the entry stays open until it is done.

## 11 · Where the choice is yours

The chapter has drawn a line and said how to check it; everything on the expressive side of the line is the studio's, and the chapter's only contribution there is to say which choices the evidence prices and which it leaves free.

**Free, by the evidence.** The register as a whole, within the conditions sections 5 and 10 set: the palette beyond the encoding; the display type; illustration; pattern; imagery; pacing; the poster layer; ambient sound that is stoppable; motion that means nothing and can be disabled. Kosara's enigma is available here. So is the whole of the Decorative tier, which the Decision Book says is "larger than most designers use", and which this chapter has not shrunk.

**Priced, by the evidence.** The genre the display claims (re-tiered Adaptable in the rules table). The dark ground (truth 5). A drawn or approximate register (truth 4). An invented form, at a measured glance cost (one radial form took about six times a bar) and at the cost of readers taking it as an image (section 5). A humanised mark, which may serve recognition and may not be priced as buying empathy or giving, and which must be whole units (section 6). A mood palette placed beside a scale, since colour moved feeling more than the data's trend did (Blair); on the scale itself it is forbidden by the rule that mood is carried only in channels that encode nothing. Headline length for a display read in passing, set by distance and seconds rather than by a word count (section 5).

**Not open, by this chapter.** The facts the evidence layer must carry and the furniture that carries them: that every encoding says all and only what the data says (which encoding, among effective ones, is an Adaptable choice and the studio's); the title's claim; the unit, base, date, source and gate; the twin; the identification text for a sensory experience; the stop control for sound; the pause control for motion; the records. And the order of the audit: accuracy, then the floor, then beauty.

**Open, and the house's to decide with a reason recorded.** Whether the boundary log is public or internal. Whether the committee export is a separate deliverable or the stripped state of the same page. Whether the studio adopts the W3C's third category, "specific sensory experience", into its tier vocabulary as a fourth tier or as a sub-kind of Decorative with an identification duty; this chapter uses it, requires the identification the W3C requires, and leaves its place in the house's vocabulary to Chapter 16. Whether a probe is built to test the two-audience claim, which has no measurement anywhere in this book.

## 12 · A nightlife illustration — method only

The method only. Suppose the studio proposes an expressive element for a view: a form in the culture's visual tradition in which a measured quantity about a place's activity is carried by something other than a conventional mark — a scaled shape, field, texture or line of any kind. The chapter does not say which, and the walk is the same for any of them.

*First, the tier.* Ask whether removing the element changes any fact the view states. If the quantity is carried only by the form, the form is in the evidence layer and is an Adaptable encoding choice, to be judged by effectiveness — which perceptual task it asks of the reader, and how well people do that task (Mackinlay's ranking, conjectural; Blascheck's glance costs). If the quantity is also carried by a conventional mark and the form repeats it, the form is Decorative or a sensory experience and the removal test governs it.

*Second, the purchase, written.* "The viewer will [verb] [noun]", from the five verbs, limited to what expression is measured to buy. "The viewer will connect the count to the place" is a claim the evidence allows; "the viewer will feel more and act" is not.

*Third, the four removals, run and written into the register.* Mono: does the form survive greyscale, or did hue carry its direction? Silent: does it make sound, and if so where is the document? Still: does it build or move, and if so what remains with motion disabled? Undressed: with the register stripped, is the quantity still stated — in a conventional mark, a number, a sentence — with its unit, base, date and gate?

*Fourth, the fifth check.* Put the stripped view beside the dressed one and ask whether they say the same thing. The commonest failure for a form that carries quantity in size, brightness or density is the one the Doughnut's caption names and the DWP houses show: an area that scales as the square of the value, a brightness normalised to the frame, a texture whose density the eye reads non-linearly — each adds a fact (how much bigger) that the data does not say. The fix is to scale the form to the value, or to do what the Doughnut's authors did: state the distortion in words on the form and ship the bar beside it.

*Fifth, the twin and the identification.* Where is the precise form, and how many steps away; what words identify the element to a reader who cannot see it, and, if it sounds, to one who cannot hear it.

*Sixth, the test, planned.* Readers from the three user groups Chapter 0 names, some shown the stripped view and some the dressed, each asked what the data said about the place; compared on the figures and the trend, as Chapter 13's pass test asks, and additionally on recognition, which Chapter 12 asks. The entry stays open until the two groups' answers agree on the figures and the trend; a difference that is small in two readers is not agreement, and the sample is the user groups', as in Chapter 13. What they may differ on — how the view felt, whether it looked like the place — is the purchase, and the register records it as such.

Nothing above names the form, the palette, the type, the sound or the place. It is the procedure by which any of them can be proposed by the people whose decision it is and checked by someone else.

## What proves a break works

Run the four removals and the fifth check, and write the register entry. If the stripped version states every figure with its unit, base, date and gate, and the dressed version says nothing the stripped one does not, the expressive layer was free and the entry closes. If the stripped version lost a fact, the sort was wrong: the element was encoding it and belongs in the evidence layer, to be judged as an encoding and to meet the floor, and the test runs again. If the dressed version added a fact — a size, an order, a brightness, a cause — the element has failed Mackinlay's second clause, and the fix is to scale it to the data, or to state the distortion in words on the form and ship the twin beside it as the Doughnut's authors did, or to re-tier it as Decorative and remove the scaling. Then the comparison: readers who saw the stripped view and readers who saw the dressed one, asked what the data said; the entry closes when the two groups agree on the figures and the trend and may differ on everything else.

## Words this chapter uses

- The *evidence layer* is everything in a display that makes or supports a claim about the world: the data, the encodings that carry it (position, length, colour, area, motion where it means something), the titles, the units, the sources, the dates, the gates on a comparison and the written account.
- The *expressive layer* is everything else that a designer adds on purpose: register, palette beyond the encoding, type beyond legibility, illustration, pattern, sound, motion that does not mean anything, pacing, and the form chosen for its feel.
- The *removal test* is the procedure this chapter formalises: take the expressive layer away — in version 1's four words, *mono, silent, still, undressed* — and check that the evidence layer still expresses all of the data and only the data.
- *Expressiveness* and *effectiveness* are Mackinlay's 1986 names for two different questions about a graphic — whether it can say exactly what the data says, and how well a human can read what it says — and the chapter uses them as the formal names of the house's tier test.
- An *anthropographic* is a chart whose marks are made to look like the people they count.
- *Affective design* is the field's term for designing a display to carry a feeling.
- *Display polarity* is whether text is dark on light (positive) or light on dark (negative); *contrast polarity* is the same thing said of a chart.
- *Redundant encoding* is carrying one variable in two visual channels at once.
- A *legibility index* is the sign trade's ratio of reading distance to letter height.
- *Register*, on its own, means tone and style — the look a display wears; where this book names a log (the *failure register*, the Decision Book's *rule register*) it says "log" or "register" with its qualifier, and the record this chapter leaves is called the *boundary log* to keep the two apart.
- The *precise twin* is the Decision Book's rule that any expressive form ships beside the same data in a precise, standard form; the *committee export* is version 1's name for the same thing at the scale of a whole page.
- *Decorative* has two official senses that the chapter keeps apart: ornament, in the plain meaning; and the technical status a design system or the W3C gives to an image that carries no information and may be skipped by assistive technology.
- A *specific sensory experience* is the W3C's third category, between information and decoration, for content "that words cannot fully capture" (read once).
- The *gallery* is version 1's set of studied exemplars, audited live for this chapter. The *evaluated exception* is version 1's name for the one gallery entry with a published evaluation.
