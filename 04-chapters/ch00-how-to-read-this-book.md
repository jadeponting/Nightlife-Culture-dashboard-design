# Chapter 0 — How to read this book

*Dashboard Design · the research book behind the Vibe Map's screens · Nightlife Culture, 2026*

---

## What this book is for

**To set out how dashboards work and why — the methodological and theoretical foundation on which a build team can decide how evidence becomes a display that a reader trusts, reads and acts on — so that the Vibe Map can prototype its *purpose* without having to prototype the function of data visualisation from scratch.**

That sentence is the whole job. Everything in the chapters that follow is either evidence for it, a rule derived from it, a choice it leaves open with the trade-offs named, or a note that the evidence ran out.

The book was written for Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio led by Carly Heath, during 2026. It is research about *how dashboards work* — from the perspectives of data analysis, economic and policy evaluation, public-sector decision-making, persuasion research, ethics and accessibility, and the craft of display. It is written for someone meeting that subject for the first time. It is not research about nightlife, and it is not an assessment, specification or design of the Vibe Map. The studio holds the sector knowledge and the creative decisions; where the book needs a fact about the sector, it takes it from Carly or from the studio's published work and says so, and where a chapter illustrates with the Vibe Map it says "an illustration, not a specification".

Carly's own statement of the mission, and the eight ways she says a dashboard fails, are in `00-plan/BRIEF-v2.md` section 2 and are the spine of Chapter 1.

## Who it is written for

In order of priority:

1. **The people building the Vibe Map** — the creative technologists, analysts and designers named in the project's funding deck. You will have the deck, the design system and the prototype repository, but not the conversations this book came out of. The book is written so that you do not need them.
2. **The people running the testing** with the three user groups the project has chosen — policymakers and politicians; advocates and trade bodies; funders and investors — with artists, DJs, promoters, crews and venues as co-developers who check whether they recognise themselves in it. (Chapter 2 finds that those three groups are, on the evidence, at least seven kinds of reader who grade evidence by different rules; the testing may want to reflect that.)
3. **Research partners** who may be asked to strengthen the method or evaluate what happens.
4. **Carly**, who set the mission and is its final reader, and whose decisions it records.

If you are none of these and have arrived cold: read this chapter, then whichever chapter's question you are facing. Each chapter is a standalone addition to the argument and opens with a short "Reading this chapter" paragraph that repeats the conventions below, so no chapter assumes you read another. Chapter 16 is read last, because it assembles the other fifteen.

## What the Vibe Map is, in the project's own words

From the funding deck (September 2026): *a living, interactive map of the people, activity, value and support systems that sustain a place's nightlife culture.* It is a free, public-facing, non-commercial resource, developed with the people who create and use it. The project's priority places are Bristol first, then Bath and North East Somerset, South Gloucestershire and North Somerset, with national coverage as context. It is a UK platform; the standards and law referred to in this book are the UK's. Nightlife Culture is not a public body, and where a chapter adopts a public-sector standard (the Code of Practice for Statistics, say) it says that the adoption is voluntary.

The project's own tests for whether it is working are four: can something universally useful be co-designed; does it paint a picture a scene recognises; does it steer decisions and collective action; can it travel to another place and stay useful. Where this book proposes a test of its own, it says which of the four it serves.

## What this book is not

- **Not a programme plan, a task list or a question catalogue.** The project's live priorities and its research questions live elsewhere, under their own IDs. This book schedules nothing and asks only the questions a methodological foundation needs.
- **Not an assessment of the Vibe Map.** It does not judge the prototype; it gives the prototype grounds to judge itself.
- **Not the creative decisions.** The question "how do we make nightlife data as exciting as a club flyer" belongs to Carly and her designers. Chapter 14 sets out where evidence meets expression and stops there.
- **Not the design system.** Colours, type, components and their rules live in the Nightlife Culture Design System (Style Guide v1.5 at the time of writing). This book defers to it for every visual value and logs, chapter by chapter, where it found the system silent or in tension.
- **Not a data contract.** How data is sourced, counted, stored and released is governed by the prototype repository. This book's rules about *showing* gaps, uncertainty and provenance are written to match it, not replace it.
- **Not research into nightlife.** Never. Where a source happens to use a music scene as its example (as Chapter 2's key source does), the chapter quotes it as the source's illustration and goes no further.

## The legend: what kind of claim is this, and how firm is it?

Carly asked for two things of every claim in this book: say what kind of thing it is, and say whether it can be crossed. Both are answered on the face of every rule.

### Kind — where the claim comes from

| Kind | Meaning | What you may do with it |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard** | An external rule nobody in this project wrote: a law, a W3C standard, a Treasury manual, a statistical disclosure rule, a UK data-protection requirement. The text says whether it *binds* Nightlife Culture or is *adopted voluntarily*. | Comply, or record why not and get it signed off. A binding standard is never a design choice |
| **Evidence** | A finding from a named study, with its strength stated in plain words: how many people, whether it was replicated, whether it was peer-reviewed, whether it was read in full or at the abstract only (†). | Treat strong evidence as a default you need a reason to leave. Treat contested, single-study or abstract-only evidence as a prompt, not a rule |
| **Reading** | Our interpretation of reference material — a paper, a theory, an exemplar, the Decision Book. It is marked as ours so that you can disagree with it. | Challenge it. If you have a better reading, say why, and the chapter changes |
| **Practice** | A practitioner's framework or craft knowledge, not tested as research: a definition from a working designer, a funder's reporting principles, a placemaking protocol, a price list of social-value proxies. It is often what readers actually use. | Treat it as how a field works, not as proof that the field is right. Where a Practice source conflicts with Evidence, the chapter says so |
| **Nightlife Culture** | A decision, finding or piece of practice from Carly or the studio's published work: the failure register, the co-production theory of vibe, the five-word poster rule, the 78%/32% finding. | Fixed when Carly has said so; otherwise a default the prototype may test |

*Practice* was added after Chapters 1 and 2, which both leaned on practitioner frameworks that fitted none of the other kinds; the original four are unchanged. Three things the chapters added to the five, which the rule register keeps as fields beside the kind. A *sub-kind of Standard*, in brackets, says what kind of body wrote it — regulator, law, method, design system, official statistics, W3C, government guidance, community — and the chapters printed thirty such brackets, which the register reduces to those eight. *Evidence of method* is a sub-kind of Evidence: a published procedure whose results are reported. And *(adjacent domain)* after Evidence or Standard marks a finding from a field other than dashboards — a price index, a psychometric standard, financial reporting — that the chapter reads across; it is a Reading at the point where it crosses. Whether a Standard *binds* the studio or is *adopted* is a one-letter field of its own in the register, because the chapters' tables said it in eight rows of 241.

### Tier — how firm it is

The book uses the Decision Book's three tiers, and they answer "can this be crossed?" directly:

| Tier | Plain meaning | Breaking it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed** | **A hard line**, in one of three senses, which the register names for every Fixed rule. *As a requirement*: there is a right answer and it can be checked — contrast, keyboard access, disclosure thresholds, provenance on every number, no league table of identities, no implied counterfactual — and the build enforces it as a check that fails. *As a sequence*: the order of operations is the hard line (define the construct before the proxy; check recognition before release; write the retirement plan before launch) while the form each step takes is Adaptable, so the rule carries both tiers. *As a statement of the record*: a finding the book is confident of, from which a design rule may or may not follow — a truth, listed apart, and not a rule at all. | Negligence, for a requirement or a sequence. Do not do it for taste |
| **Adaptable** | **A default you may argue with.** There is no single right answer; the book states a position and the reasons. You may take a different one if you can defend it and you log it. Where the position is the house's own, held with its grounds, the register says *Adaptable (house default)*. | The job. Deviation is expected — with a record |
| **Decorative** | **Free.** Register, imagery, pacing, texture — provided nothing that carries meaning lives only there. An expressive element is Decorative only if it can be ignored; one that cannot — the W3C's "specific sensory experience" — is a tier of *element*, not of rule, and owes identification in text (Chapter 14). | Expected |

Two wordings the chapters' tables once used, which this legend does not, and which you will now meet only in Chapter 16's account of them. Chapters 5 to 15 printed **Fixed as a stance** (or "(stance)") for a rule the studio adopts whose source is a reading or a practice, and **Fixed as a procedure** (or "(procedure)") for a rule that fixes the steps and not the parameter. Chapter 16 counted them — 90 and 102 rows — and re-based them: a stance is *Adaptable (house default)* unless the chapter's own grounds show a requirement, and a procedure is a requirement (the procedure is the check) or a sequence, by its wording. The rule register carries the re-based tier, and the chapters' tables follow it. Chapter 16 also reconciled the eleven rules that carried different tiers in different chapters, and the register marks the superseded rows. Where you deviate from a Fixed or Adaptable rule, Chapter 16 gives the nine-line deviation entry to write first and the test to run, and section 8 of that chapter the build's checks.

### The marks you will meet

- **†** after a figure or quotation means it was taken from the authors' published abstract or from a secondary account, not from the full text. Chapters 1 to 6 used it for abstracts only; from Chapter 7 the chapters widened it, and this is the widest definition. A Fixed rule resting on a † source carries the status *full text not read* in the rule register, which the build lists; the chapters' tables did not carry the flag row by row, and the register does.
- **"Secondary"** means the quotation reached the chapter through another named document, which is cited. **"Reconstructed"** (from Chapter 6) means an abstract rebuilt from an index record.
- **"(read once)"** means a quotation returned once by the fetch tool without a presence check; **"two passes"** and **"three passes"** mean one returned identically that many times without a presence check. A presence check opens the page again and asks only whether the exact string is on it, with a false string beside it to show the tool will say no.
- **In the reader**, these marks are rendered as bracketed words — [abstract or secondary], [read once], [two passes], [secondary] — because the Analysis Function advises against symbols such as the dagger, which screen readers find hard; the dagger remains a convenience of the manuscript (Chapter 15 §4; Chapter 16 §7).
- **"At source · spot-checked · verified"** in a sources list says how far a claim has been traced: a research agent opened the link; the orchestrator re-opened it; an independent agent re-traced it. Every chapter went through all three before it was published.

### The flags

Loose ends were marked in square brackets as the chapters were written, so they could be searched across the book:

| Flag | Means |
|---|---|
| `[Further research]` | A thread worth pulling that this chapter did not have room or evidence to finish |
| `[Prototype flag]` | Something the Vibe Map can settle with evidence that no literature can |
| `[Validate with users]` | Something to test in conversation with the three user groups or the scenes |
| `[Revisit → Ch n]` | A question that belongs to a later chapter and is handed to it |
| `[Revisit at end]` | A question to return to once all chapters exist, because its answer depends on them |
| `[Rich reference]` | A source worth your own time, or worth returning to when building |
| `[Counterbalance → X]` | This finding pulls against an earlier chapter's, or against the design system, the Decision Book or version 1; recorded, not resolved. The chapters wrote it with a chapter number or a document's name |

The chapters no longer carry the brackets. The `[Prototype flag]` and `[Validate with users]` lines became the probe candidates in `03-tests/PROBES.md`; the `[Further research]`, `[Revisit]` and `[Counterbalance]` lines are in the working record; `[Rich reference]` became each chapter's "Read next". The table stands as the history of the marks, so that the probes and the working record can be read.

### The symmetry test

Every chapter from Chapter 4 ran a *symmetry test* on its own brief, and four chapters credited the test to this chapter before it was written here. It was the test a chapter ran on what it was asked: whether what it found was what the brief expected — the sources, the quotations, the shape of the answer — and what it did when the two differed. It was born in Chapter 4's gap table, used by Chapter 5, and from Chapter 7 was a numbered section. The tests are in the working record.

### Why the old marks have gone

Version 1 carried marks like **[Measured]**, **[Evaluated]** or **[House steer]** in bold brackets mid-sentence. They were a grading system that was never explained, and they made the text unreadable to anyone outside the project. They are gone. The prose now says in words what kind of claim it is making ("a controlled study of 81 people found…", "our reading is…", "Carly's rule is…"), the rules table carries the kind and the tier, and the sources list says how each source was read. Version 1 also called the Vibe Map "the instrument". It does not any more: the book is about dashboards, and the Vibe Map is one.

## Words this book uses, and what they mean

Plain definitions, in roughly the order you are likely to meet them. Each term is also explained the first time it appears in a chapter. Where a word is one chapter's and the others borrow it, the entry says whose.

- **A dashboard** — the book's working definition, from Chapter 1: *a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader.* An argument, not a window. Few's single monitored screen, a layered analytic system, a public health tracker, a council's performance report and the Vibe Map are all dashboards, differing in reader and decision. Power BI is one species of the genus, not the genus.
- **A reader** — whoever the display is for. Chapter 2 names eight kinds and finds that each makes a different decision, is trained to accept different evidence, and keeps a different clock; the same display is graded by all of them at once.
- **A place** — whatever unit a reader is looking at: a city, a neighbourhood, a ward, a scene's territory. Not necessarily a council boundary; the chapters say which, and Chapter 1 shows that the choice of unit changes the answer.
- **A scene** — a community of people who make and attend a kind of night: a genre, a crew, a venue's regulars, a diaspora's nights. Scenes overlap and cross boundaries. The *scene scale* is version 1's phrase for the unit at which a scene lives, which is rarely an administrative one (Chapter 12).
- **The three user groups** — the readers the studio tests with, from the funding deck: policymakers and politicians; advocates and trade bodies; funders and investors. Artists, DJs, promoters, crews and venues are co-developers, who check whether they recognise themselves, not a fourth group. Chapter 2 finds the three are at least seven kinds of reader.
- **The house; the studio** — Nightlife Culture. Some chapters say "the house" for the studio's own rules and positions, as in *Adaptable (house default)*.
- **Version 1** — the book's archived first draft, written in August 2026. Its chapters are cited as positions to test, not as sources; where a version 2 chapter re-tests one, it says so.
- **The sceptical reader** — Chapter 2's reader who arrives already opposed, for whom the handsome chart may read as advocacy and the plain table as evidence; Chapters 3, 4, 9 and 10 use the name.
- **The failure register** — Carly's eight ways a dashboard fails: it looks the same every month; it is a fancy spreadsheet; the viewer asks "what am I looking at"; people do not recognise their place; people do not use it; it breaks; people do not trust it; it is an aggregation of the biggest. Chapter 1 reads them against the evidence.
- **Monitoring and evaluation** — the Treasury's distinction, from Chapter 2. Monitoring answers "did we do what we said we would do; how are our success metrics changing over time?"; evaluation answers "did it work, what was the impact, on whom, and why?" A dashboard is monitoring. It can host a comparison; it cannot supply one.
- **Appraisal; the business case; the five cases; social value; the benefit–cost ratio** — the official reader's grammar, explained in Chapter 2 section 3.
- **The counterfactual** — what would have happened without the intervention; the thing every claim of impact needs and no dashboard contains. Chapter 7.
- **An evidence ladder** — a grading of studies by causal design (Maryland, Nesta, the padlocks). A dashboard alone stands on the first or second rung. Chapter 2.
- **WELLBY; wellbeing valuation** — the one official route from how people feel to pounds (£13,000 per one-point change in life satisfaction per person per year, in the 2021 guidance), and the method behind most British "value of culture" headlines. Chapter 2 introduces them; Chapter 5 asks what it takes to use them.
- **Reactivity** — a published measure changes the behaviour it measures. Strathern's and Campbell's laws; Espeland's rankings. Chapter 1.
- **The unit of aggregation; the ecological fallacy; the modifiable areal unit problem** — the same numbers, regrouped, can give any answer; a place-level figure is not a person-level one. Chapter 1.
- **The suppression threshold** — a statistical disclosure rule: small counts are not shown, and a second suppression stops the hidden cell being worked out from the others. Shown as `[c]`, never as a blank. Chapter 1; its ethics in Chapter 10.
- **Six kinds of nothing** — not collected, unknown, zero, free, unavailable, suppressed: kept distinct on screen because they mean different things. The prototype's data contract uses the same six. Chapter 10 names the *three nothings* a reader meets on a face — *withheld* (a disclosure rule removed the cell), *unmeasured* and *held* — which must be told apart without placing the held units; Chapter 11 uses them.
- **The data contract** — the prototype repository's own rules for its data, which this book's rules about *showing* gaps match (Chapters 9, 10, 12).
- **The heartbeat** — the visible age of a dashboard's data, on every state, so a live container can never look more alive than its stale contents. Chapter 8 uses the word in the engineers' sense too — a signal a scheduled job sends to say it ran, and the service that listens for it — and calls the date on the face the *stamp*, which is usually the date the page was built, not the date the data was collected; Chapters 4 and 11 use *stamp* the same way.
- **The grain** — what one row of the data is (a person, a venue, a night, a ticket) and what one unit of time is: Chapter 4's word for both, chosen before any analysis. A *vintage* is the dataset as it stood on a given release date, so a number has at least three dates (Chapters 4 and 15).
- **Baked data** — Chapter 8's architecture, from Willison: "bundling a read-only copy of your data alongside the code for your application, as part of the same deployment", so that the dependencies move from view time to build time (Chapters 8, 13, 14).
- **A panel; a view; the face** — a dashboard's parts are *panels*, never tiles (Chapter 8). A *view* is a panel in one filter state (Chapters 10, 11). The *face* of a panel is its visible surface — what a reader sees without opening anything (Chapters 9, 10, 11); the three distances from it are below.
- **A boundary object** — a thing several communities can each use in their own way because it has a shared structure, not a shared meaning. Chapter 1 argues the Vibe Map must be one, and that being one is a social achievement, not a design feature.
- **Member checking; recognition** — taking an account back to the people it is about and asking whether they recognise it. Chapter 2: a test of credibility, not accuracy, and only when the answer can change the account.
- **Issue bias** — the way a choice of what to count decides which questions get asked. Chapter 2; Chapter 10.
- **Numbers for understanding and numbers for advocacy** — the studio's distinction, from Chapter 2: the first are produced with their caveats and released with their data; the second are selected from the first for a room. A sector's dashboard can only be the first kind.
- **Headline-to-caveat decay** — every valuation report carries its hedges; every downstream release drops them; the dashboard is the last place in the chain they can still be shown. Chapter 2.
- **Co-production of vibe** — Carly's principle that a vibe cannot be consumed, only made together; so feeling is *collected with* people, never scraped. Chapter 9.
- **Identity and conditions** — version 1's split, settled in Chapter 12: a place's cultural identity is never ranked, as a stance; the *conditions* places give their culture may be compared, with the comparison gated and shown with intervals. A place's *identity* is its profile — what it is like, what it sounds like, the shape of its week, who comes — and its *conditions* are what it has or lacks: space, support, funding, affordability, security of tenure. Chapters 14 and 15 call the stance the *identity line*: a place's people are never ranked by a condition.
- **The gate; the comparability gate** — a *gate* is the condition a comparison must meet before it is shown (Chapter 7). The *comparability gate* is the design system's rule that any comparison between places states whether the data is actually comparable (same period, same geography, same definition); Chapter 12 gives it as the house's eight-criterion check (Style Guide §12; Comparative Methodology v1.0). A comparison without that note is not publishable. Chapter 4's *ten-line declaration* is what a comparison declares it holds equal.
- **The peer method** — showing a place beside places like it rather than ranked above and below them (Chapter 12); Chapter 7 says what one looks like on a dashboard — a fixed number of peers, a stated distance, a stated assumption, and a label for how like the neighbour is. A *spread* is Chapter 7's display of every unit's value as a distribution with one unit marked and no positions given; an *ordered list* is a table of units in rank order with positions (Chapter 12).
- **The two columns** — version 1's name for the deck's slides 7 and 8: what systems already count about a place (incidents, licensing, complaints, footfall, spend) beside what a scene holds and almost nobody counts (belonging, trust, care, talent, joy) — the *counted* column and the *missed* column (Chapters 11, 12, 13).
- **The money view** — version 1's view of underinvestment shown as visible absence, "the map of money over the map of scenes" (Chapter 12). Chapter 10: lit for a scene only after its governance has agreed, the speculation reading has been assessed, and both are on the record.
- **The small room** — the brief's small venue, which an aggregate hides and a disclosure rule would blank. Chapter 10: shown as present in a coarser count, never as empty; a room under both floors is present and unmeasured, never absent (Chapter 16).
- **The floor** — two senses in Chapter 10, qualified every time: the *disclosure floor* is the minimum count below which a cell is withheld, set by the community's knowability above any national rule; the *precision floor* is Chapter 9's fifty answers, a rule about reliability and not about identification. Chapters 13, 14 and 15 use *the floor* for a third thing, version 1's name for the accessibility requirements the house adopted — keyboard operation including chart elements, visible focus, 200 and 400 per cent zoom, semantic structure, alt text generated from the current state, user override of colour, contrast and font, and observed testing.
- **Lit; held; the held layer** — a layer is *lit* when shown at its own resolution and *held* when shown coarser or not at all by decision (Chapter 11). The *held status* is Chapter 10's: a declared status for a category of thing that is known to the house and not shown, with a reason, a resolution, a decider and a review date — never a marker on a named room or business. The *held layer* is the set so held, tested by the walk against public lists.
- **The walk** — Chapter 10's friendly-intruder test of a view's filters against public lists: before release, someone who knows the scene and has a bounded time is asked to find a person or a venue, and writes down what they found and how sure they are. A small number of low-confidence correct claims is the standard, not zero. Chapters 11 and 12 use it.
- **The forum** — Chapter 10's standing body of people from the scenes, consulted on what is shown; it convenes the scene — not a leader, a forum — and the decision to hold a layer is a condition the studio sets on itself at the community's request through a body it names (Chapters 10, 12).
- **The safeguards record** — the record beneath any dashboard that shows people (Chapter 10): what is held and about whom, the basis, the release model, the method per view, the walk, who was consulted, who governs and who stood at the door, the held layer, withdrawal, harm, regimes, moderation and review. Its first entry is written before the first row is collected. One section of the one record, below.
- **The table twin** — Chapter 8's rule that every map, and Chapter 11's that every dense chart, has the same figures as a table reachable without the map and before any script (Chapters 10, 13, 14). The *precise twin* is the Decision Book's rule that any expressive form ships beside the same data in a precise, standard form; the *committee export* is version 1's name for the same thing at the scale of a whole page (Chapters 14, 16).
- **Provenance** — where a number came from, for what period, for what geography, by what method. The design system puts a provenance code on every chart; Chapter 1 puts it on the face of the display. The *provenance set* is Chapter 4's ten lines, shipped with every figure: where it came from and what was done to it (Chapters 11, 14). A *provenance tier* is the house's text code for how a figure was obtained, and a *claim rung* the status Chapter 6 gives its causal strength; both sit on the face as chips (Chapters 11, 12).
- **The default view; the first screen** — what the dashboard shows before anyone touches it. Chapter 11 (version 1's Chapter 1 and its six landing patterns feed it). The *sentence* is a view's headline message — what it says, to whom — written within the house's word limit. An *endorsement* is Chapter 11's finding that whatever a page shows first is read as what the maker stands behind (Chapters 3, 8, 12). An *address* is the canonical web address that encodes one state of a view (Chapters 11, 12). An interaction's *purchase* is what it buys, version 1's word, kept (Chapters 11, 14, 16).
- **The three-phones test** — Chapter 11's: a reader on a phone says the sentence, the figure it rests on and a next step, run with one reader from each user group (Chapter 12).
- **The face sheet** — the record beneath every view, one per view, whose first line is the view's sentence (Chapter 11); one section of the one record.
- **The crop test** — screenshot any state; if what travels could be read as a league table of places, the state fails, and whatever is cut out carries its title, unit, source, base, address and date. Version 1; measured in Chapter 12; one of the build's four checks in Chapter 16. Chapter 14 calls it the *cut-out rule*: a chart that may be screenshotted carries its title, unit, source, base, address, snapshot date and any gate on the image itself.
- **On the face · one step away · on request** — the three distances from a panel's surface, from Chapter 11, which the book uses for where a thing sits: seen without acting; opened by one action and shown beside it; on a separate page by a link. Chapters 4 to 7 wrote "one tap" for the second.
- **The removal test** — take the interaction, colour, sound or decoration away; if the evidence is still complete, the thing removed was free. Chapter 14's form is four removals — mono, silent, still, undressed — and a fifth check, stripped beside dressed. It is one of six forms the book's tests take; the others are the reader test (the commonest), the adversarial walk, the recomputation, the record check and the automated scan (Chapter 16 §7). The *evidence layer* is everything in a display that makes or supports a claim about the world — the data, the encodings that carry it, the titles, units, sources, dates, the gates on a comparison and the written account; the *expressive layer* is everything else a designer adds on purpose (Chapter 14).
- **The tier test; deference; negligence** — the Decision Book's first move on any disagreement: decide whether a rule is Fixed, Adaptable or Decorative before arguing about it. *Deference* is the Decision Book's name for treating an Adaptable decision as if it were Fixed; *negligence* for treating a Fixed one as if it were Adaptable (Chapters 14, 16; this chapter's tier table).
- **A canonical truth** — a finding is canonical when it passes two tests, from Chapter 6: it holds out of sample, and the competent agree, the people described among them. Adopted as this book's definition at Chapter 16.
- **The five-word rule; the poster rule** — Carly's rule from club-poster practice: a headline is five words where possible, ten at most. Chapters 11 and 12 call it the *poster rule*. Chapter 11 sets it beside the official limits; Chapter 14 re-tiered it Adaptable (house default), with the reading distance and seconds beneath it as the requirement.
- **The price list; the one-tailed search; the refusal test** — Chapter 5's words for what an index does with its parts. Any aggregation is a hidden *price list*: the rate at which an index trades one part for another. A *one-tailed* instrument is built to find shortfall, or thriving, and finds the small and the lucky (Chapters 3, 6, 7, 9, 16). Version 1's *refusal test* stands as the studio's values test: the card must say how many new venues the measure thinks compensate for the loss of one forty-year-old institution, in venues, and if the answer is a number the studio would not defend in public, the aggregation should change until it does.
- **The forking paths; the holdout; the badge rule** — Chapter 6's, used by Chapter 7: every filter is a fork, and a pattern found at the end of one is a multiple-comparisons result; a pattern is a finding only on data that did not produce it; and no "significant" badge stands alone as the reason for a highlight. The *gross-to-net discipline* is Chapter 6's arithmetic of subtracting what would have happened anyway before claiming value (Chapters 15, 16).
- **Validity; reliability** — validity belongs to an interpretation of a number for a use, never to the number itself; reliability is whether the same measure gives the same answer twice (Chapter 9). Chapter 5 uses reliability in three senses — revisions, consistency across replications, inter-rater — and says which.
- **The 78%/32% finding** — from Nightlife Culture's ticketing analysis: venues under 1,000 capacity put on 78% of paid events but take 32% of ticketing revenue. The reason aggregates hide the grassroots. Chapter 1's "aggregation of the biggest".
- **The club flyer** — the brief's image of a display that turns information into a decision quickly and with pleasure; the standard the interactive layer is asked to reach. Chapter 1 introduces it; Chapter 2 finds it persuades the persuadable; Chapter 14 is about it.
- **The seven tests** — the design system's checks on any output: committee-room, dancefloor, phone, screen-reader, hostile-reader, partner, paid-work.

The Decision Book's stored form adds a consequence (red, amber, yellow) and a status; the colours are internal, and the chapters that printed them in a column did what this legend said not to, so the register keeps them and the reader shows words.

Three types of entry — a departure (the Decision Book's override log), an expressive element (Chapter 14's boundary log) and a borrowed mechanism (Chapter 15's precedent record) — share the nine lines and add their own. ## Three things to hold in mind while reading

**The evidence base is thinner than the field admits.** Several of the most-quoted rules in data visualisation have never been tested; the evidence that dashboards change decisions at all is thin everywhere and mixed where it is best; and the field that studies what moves a policy reader knows it mostly from asking them. Where the book finds that, it says so. A rule marked Adaptable with "no study exists" is an honest state, not a weakness.

**Strong design must never make weak evidence look stronger than it is** — the style guide's line — and its twin, which this book adds: strong evidence must never be made to look weaker than it is by timid design. Chapter 2 adds a third: for the reader who arrives already opposed, the handsome chart may read as advocacy and the plain table as evidence.

**Nothing here is finished until it has survived use.** The book is finished. Where the chapters leave a choice open, the chapter states the trade-offs, and the decision log (`00-plan/DECISIONS.md`) carries the book's steer for each and the moment the build will raise it. Nothing in the chapters waits on anyone; what waits is use.
