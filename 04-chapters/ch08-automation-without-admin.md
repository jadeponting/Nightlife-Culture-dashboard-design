## Before you start

This is the eighth chapter of a research book written for Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio led by Carly Heath, about how dashboards work and why. The book exists to support the Vibe Map — a planned public, non-commercial, interactive account of the people, activity, value and support systems that sustain a place's nightlife culture, built first for the West of England. You do not need to have read Chapters 1 to 7 to read this one. Five things from them recur: the working definition — *a dashboard is a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader*; Chapter 4's rule that every figure carries its provenance and its revision status, and its warning that a platform's data describes the platform's users; Chapter 6's finding that business closures "are not final until the third year" and that a modelled figure's history is revised; Chapter 7's finding that a seasonally adjusted series changes when the next month arrives and cannot exist under three years; and version 1's founding case, the observatory — a maintained container over unmaintained contents, with update labels reading "almost 7 years ago". Where this chapter refers to Carly, it means the studio's founder; where it refers to the Vibe Map, it is illustrating, not specifying. The studio's rulebook for design decisions is the Decision Book; its visual rules are in a design system (Style Guide v1.5).

Two conventions, explained fully in Chapter 0. Every rule carries a **kind** — *Standard*, *Evidence*, *Reading*, *Practice* or *Nightlife Culture* — and a **tier**: *Fixed*, *Adaptable* or *Decorative*; "Fixed as a stance" marks a rule the studio adopts whose source is a reading or a practice, and "Fixed as a procedure" fixes the steps and not the parameter. Flags in square brackets mark loose ends: `[Further research]`, `[Prototype flag]`, `[Validate with users]`, `[Revisit → Ch n]`, `[Revisit at end]`, `[Rich reference]` and `[Counterbalance → Ch n]`. A dagger (†) marks a source read at the authors' abstract or summary only; "secondary" marks a quotation reached through another named document; "(read once)" marks a quotation returned on a single reading and not confirmed by a second. The *symmetry test* (Chapter 0) means that every standard this chapter applies to others' systems it applies, in section 11, to the studio's own; the *failure register* is the brief's list, in Carly's words, of the ways a dashboard fails — among them "if it breaks or isn't reliable" and "if people don't trust it". Three terms of the trade are used throughout and defined where they first appear: an *API* (an application programming interface — the published way one piece of software asks another for data), a *rate limit* (how often it may ask), and a *tile* (one square of a map, served separately so that only the squares in view are fetched; a dashboard's panels are called *panels* here, never tiles). Four more are defined where they first appear and gathered here: a *heartbeat* is a signal a scheduled job sends to say it ran, and the service that listens for it; the *bus factor*, also called the *truck factor*, is the number of people who could leave before a project stopped; an *SLO* is a service level objective, a stated target for a measured property of a service, and an *SLA* the agreement about what happens if it is missed; a *page*, in the reliability engineers' vocabulary quoted in section 7, is an alert sent to a person's pager, not a web page. Sizes in KB and MB are decimal; KiB and MiB, used by one source, are binary (1 KiB = 1,024 bytes) and are quoted as written. Where a source is called "the fetch tool's reader", it means the text-only reader this book's research used to open pages.

A note on this chapter's sources. The research agents again had no web search; but this chapter's sources are mostly specifications, official guidance and platforms' own documentation, which live at known addresses, and the record is fuller than Chapters 6 and 7 could manage. Every quotation given was confirmed present on its page by a second fetch, spot-checked by the author (twelve sources; one reshaped sentence caught and corrected), or is marked as weaker than that; an independent verifier then re-traced about four hundred strings on some 125 pages and its corrections are in this draft, with the sources it confirmed marked "verified" in the list at the end. Developer documentation carries dates rarely and changes often: every limit, price and quota in this chapter is given as read on 6 October 2026, and several of the sentences the research was briefed to find had already been rewritten or removed by the time it looked. That drift is recorded in section 8 as evidence.

## The question

**What does it take for a dashboard to stay alive — refreshed, reliable, fast on a phone, its maps drawn — without a person tending it every day; what are the technological boundaries of that, what does each cost, and where does it break?**

---

## The 60-second version

> A dashboard that was true on the day it launched is a different thing a year later, and the difference is operations. This chapter is about what "living" commits a publisher to, what each dependency promises and does not, what breaks when nobody is watching, and what the phone and the map cost.
>
> **Freshness is a declared lifetime.** The web's own protocol defines it that way — "A 'fresh' response is one whose age has not yet exceeded its freshness lifetime" — and the catalogue standards separate three dates (issued, modified, the period covered) and a stated frequency, with the admission that an absent modification date "MAY indicate that the resource has never changed after its initial publication, or that the date of last modification is not known, or that the resource is continuously updated". A published figure's date is not the date its value became final: national accounts "are usually open to revisions for the period back to 1997". The base rate of decay is measured — "A quarter of all webpages that existed at one point between 2013 and 2023 are no longer accessible, as of October 2023"; one in five government pages has a broken link; "one out of five" scholarly articles suffers reference rot — and the UK's own guidance for dashboards says "Always make it clear which dates or period your dashboard is relevant to", and that a dashboard "is a digital service", "not an appropriate project for a single person or for temporary staff".
>
> **A dependency is a promise someone else made.** The open licence that most public data carries says "The Information Provider does not guarantee the continued supply of the Information." The UK's standard for government interfaces asks publishers to "try to give users 6 months notice" — for live interfaces, and most public ones call themselves beta. The platform interfaces a place dashboard would most want are the least stable thing it could depend on: one narrowed its interface three times in sixteen months, the first "Effective today", and its policy forbids "creating new or derived listenership metrics, benchmarking … usage statistics"; another reserves the right to block "applications that make a large number of calls to the API that are not primarily in response to direct user actions", which is what a scheduled refresh makes; a third is "non-commercial use only" with limits "continually changed". A map's free tiles come with "no SLA or guarantee" and the words "Our tile servers are not" free.
>
> **The chain breaks at its weakest link, silently.** "A service cannot be more available than the intersection of all its critical dependencies"; a stated 99.9% is 8.76 hours a year dark. A scheduled job on the free scheduler attached to the commonest code host "may be dropped" under load, is, "In a public repository", "automatically disabled when no repository activity has occurred in 60 days", and sends its failure notices "to the user who last modified the cron syntax". The reliability engineers' remedies are plain: monitor the effect of the job rather than the job; treat a page that loads with wrong content as an error; on failure keep the last good copy — "Stale data is almost always better than incorrect data" — and never empty the page because a refresh failed. The architecture that most nearly means "no admin" bundles a read-only copy of the data with the site and rebuilds on a schedule; its author draws its boundary at content that changes "at most a few times a day".
>
> **The phone is the binding constraint, and the map is the heaviest thing on it.** The median mobile page is 2.3 MB and more than half of sites fail the web's own speed thresholds on phones; a budget built for the device a typical user owns allows "650KiB of JavaScript" for a five-second load, and the vector map library alone is about 287 KB, by a third-party measurement, before a single tile arrives. The UK public sector's answer is that services "should be functional using only HTML", because "1.1% of people aren't getting JavaScript enhancements (1 in 93)", four-fifths of them involuntarily. A fifth of online adults are smartphone-only; in social grade DE households, three in ten. A map is a licence, a tile supply and a vintage: the full-resolution boundary file for the UK's local authorities is about 128 MB and the 200-metre generalisation under 2 MB; a map needs a non-map twin, and buttons to pan and zoom.
>
> So the canonical truths: freshness is a declared lifetime, stated as three dates and a frequency; a dashboard is a service with a bus factor, and "no admin" moves the person's job to reading the ticket; a dependency's terms, limits and notice are part of the design, and open licences give the right to reuse, not continued supply; availability is bounded by all the critical dependencies together, and the baked architecture moves them from view time to build time; a scheduled job fails silently by default, so an independent watch checks the effect and the ticket goes to a role; the phone sets the byte budget and the page shows its figures before any script runs; a map is a licence, a tile supply and a vintage, and has a non-map twin. Beneath the dashboard sits a *service record*, and its retirement is planned on the day it launches.

## 1 · What "living" means

Version 1 of this book opened its chapter on freshness with a case: a city observatory whose front page was maintained and whose datasets were not — update labels reading "almost 7 years ago", one dataset in roughly twenty-four current, and that one maintained by somebody else. The brief's failure register says a dashboard fails "if it breaks or isn't reliable" and "if people don't trust it", and the observatory failed both without a single error page: every chart drew, every number was wrong by years. This chapter is about the distance between a dashboard that works and a dashboard that is alive, and about what closing that distance costs.

"Alive" has a precise meaning in the web's own plumbing and a looser one in the catalogue standards, and the chapter will use both. In the protocol that carries every page, HTTP, freshness is a lifetime that a publisher declares: "A 'fresh' response is one whose age has not yet exceeded its freshness lifetime. Conversely, a 'stale' response is one where it has." The lifetime is "the length of time between its generation by the origin server and its expiration time". A response is not fresh because it is recent; it is fresh because its publisher said how long it would stay so, and that time has not run out. Chapter 4 asked every figure to carry its revision status; this chapter asks it to carry its lifetime — when it was made, when it was last revised, and how long its maker says it may be trusted before it must be looked at again.

The rest of the chapter is in four movements. Sections 3 and 4 are about freshness and the records that describe it. Sections 5 and 6 are about dependencies — the interfaces a dashboard draws on, what they promise, and how they have behaved. Sections 7 and 8 are about reliability — what breaks, how it is noticed, and the architectures that need the least tending. Sections 9 and 10 are about the phone and the map, which are the two places where a dashboard's weight becomes its reader's cost. Section 11 turns the whole on the studio's own positions.

## 2 · Why people believe what they believe

### Belief one: a dashboard is finished when it launches

The launch is the visible event; the operations are not. The UK government's own guidance for analysts who build dashboards begins from the opposite premise — a dashboard "is a digital service" that must be "continually" managed — and estimates the resource as larger than people plan for. Section 3.

### Belief two: "last updated" tells you how fresh the data is

The stamp on the page is usually the date the page was built, not the date the data was collected, not the date its value became final, and not a promise of when it will next move. The catalogue standards separate those and the national statistics offices revise figures for years. Section 4.

### Belief three: an API is a tap

If an interface exists today it will exist next year, at the same address, returning the same fields, at the same rate. The streaming and social platforms' interfaces have each been narrowed, re-priced or closed within the last five years; the ticketing platforms publish too little to know; and the terms of all of them permit narrowing without notice. Section 5 and 6.

### Belief four: free data means free supply

The open licence that covers most UK public data is generous in what it permits and explicit that it promises nothing about continuing to exist. The free map tiles are free data on servers that are not. Section 5, 6 and 10.

### Belief five: if it is up, it is working

Uptime measures whether the page loads. A page that loads and shows last quarter's figures as this quarter's is, in the reliability engineers' own definition, an error — "an HTTP 200 success response, but coupled with the wrong content" — and no uptime check will see it. Section 7.

### Belief six: automation removes the person

A scheduled job replaces the daily task; it does not replace the person who notices that the job stopped. Every scheduler documented here fails silently in at least one way, and every reliability text ends at the same place: someone reads the ticket. Section 7 and 8.

### Belief seven: it works on my phone

The phone a typical user carries is "15-25% as fast" as the developer's — the developer's is four to six times faster — and it is on a slower network. The budget for a page is set by the device at the seventy-fifth percentile, not the first. Section 9.

### Belief eight: a map is a chart with a background

A map is a dependency on a tile supplier with a licence and a policy, a boundary file with a vintage and a size, and a rendering library heavier than everything else on the page. Section 10.

## 3 · What a living dashboard commits to

### The official account

In February 2026 the Government Analysis Function published guidance on building and managing dashboards, and it is the nearest thing the UK has to an official statement of what a dashboard commits its publisher to. Its premise: "A dashboard is a digital service, which means you must have the skills and resource to continually manage" — among other things — "the flow of data going into the dashboard" and the "relevance of the dashboard over time". Its warning: "Dashboards often require more resource than initially identified due to their complexity." "They are not an appropriate project for a single person or for temporary staff." Its conditions for building one at all include that "data updates are frequent and can be automated, with users likely to revisit the dashboard". Its rules for the face: "Always make it clear which dates or period your dashboard is relevant to", and "When updating the data in your dashboard, add a change note explaining the change and when it happened." And its rules for the end: "Retire your dashboard if it is not being maintained or user feedback indicates that it is no longer useful, relevant or providing value for money"; "Add a banner on all pages of the service to let users know the dashboard will be retired and no longer updated. Do this at least six months before retirement"; and archive by "keeping data and metadata available, with a clear note that it is no longer updated and the dates or period the data is associated with", with the code kept "in a public GitHub repository marked as archived". `[Rich reference]`

Three things in that guidance organise this chapter. The dashboard is a service, with the continuing resource a service needs — which section 11 will turn on the studio, since a small studio is close to "a single person". The face states its period and logs its changes — the dated stamp version 1 asked for, now official. And the retirement is planned, announced six months ahead, and ends in an archive that says what it was — which is the anti-observatory move version 1 called "honest amputation", with an official procedure attached.

The GOV.UK publishing guidance has the same shape for a page. "When you update content on an already published page, you may need to write a change note"; change notes are "sometimes published on the page, along with the time and date" and "emailed to anyone who subscribed to the page"; and "Do not say the page has been updated without saying what has changed." Withdrawal is a state, not a deletion: "the page will remain on GOV.UK, but it'll have a banner saying that the content has been withdrawn", with the standard notice — "This publication was withdrawn on 24 February 2022" — at the top. The ONS's release calendar labels each release "Published", "Upcoming" or "Cancelled", and its time-series pages carry "Release date", "Next release" and "View previous versions". A living publication, in UK official practice, tells its reader three times: what period it describes, when it last changed and why, and when it will change next.

### Timeliness is not punctuality

The official statistical vocabulary makes a distinction that dashboards conflate. Eurostat's glossary: timeliness is "the lapse of time between the end of a reference period (or a reference date) and the dissemination of the data"; punctuality is "the possible time lag existing between the actual delivery date of the data and the target date when the data should have been delivered". The first is how far behind the world the figure is; the second is whether the publisher kept its promise about when the figure would arrive. A dashboard can be punctual and stale — refreshed every night from a source that is a year behind — or timely and unpunctual. The UK Code of Practice for Statistics uses the words without defining them, and asks producers to "Pre-announce the release of statistics with the time and date in a 12-month release calendar", to "Release on a timely basis, meeting the needs of users as far as possible and as soon as the statistics are ready", to "Give advance notice of material changes to the content of releases, such as the removal of data tables or variables", and to "Ensure that statistics continue to be publicly available, such as through web or data archiving". The stamp on a dashboard, if it is to mean what the official vocabulary means, carries both: the period the data describes, and the date the data was due — which is what a declared frequency, counted from the last publication, gives.

### What a living series commits to

Chapters 6 and 7 found that a living series is not a snapshot repeated. A closures count is provisional for two years because of reactivations; a seasonally adjusted series cannot exist under three years and its history changes when the next month arrives; a modelled small-area figure's interval "should not be used to measure change". A dashboard that commits to a living series commits to all of that: to showing provisional figures as provisional, to revising its own history and saying so, to not adjusting what is too young to adjust, and to a release discipline — the data.gov.uk publisher guidance puts it in one line: "Do not create new datasets for data you publish regularly, for example monthly spend data. Instead, create a single dataset and add new data to it." And: "In the interests of transparency, you should not delete datasets". The W3C's best practices for data on the web ask the same of a publisher — "Version information makes a revision of a dataset uniquely identifiable"; "Assign URIs to individual versions of datasets as well as to the overall series" — and the W3C's own implementation report records that, of the catalogue systems it surveyed in 2016, "Concerning BP27 none of the data catalog solutions implement it", BP27 being *Preserve identifiers*: "In general, when a dataset is not available then just a 404 error message is returned." The practice's title is "Preserve identifiers"; its body was beyond the reach of this research, and the report says that nobody surveyed had built it.

## 4 · Three dates and a frequency

### The catalogue vocabulary

The metadata standard that public data catalogues use, DCAT, separates what a dashboard's single stamp runs together. The release date is "Date of formal issuance (e.g., publication) of the resource", set "using the first known date of issuance". The modification date is the "Most recent date on which the resource was changed, updated or modified", and "The value of this property indicates a change to the actual resource, not a change to the catalog record." The period a dataset covers is a third thing, and the UK's profile defines it separately: "The date period that the dataset applies to." And then the frequency — in the UK profile "The frequency at which dataset is published", expressed as a repeating duration (annual, weekly) "or `irregular`"; in the European profile "The frequency at which the Dataset is updated"; in the Dublin Core vocabulary it derives from, "The frequency with which items are added to a collection". Three profiles, three meanings of "frequency": published, updated, added to. A dashboard that promises its reader a cadence should say which.

The standard's own usage note for the modification date is the chapter's warning about any "last updated" stamp. "An absent value MAY indicate that the resource has never changed after its initial publication, or that the date of last modification is not known, or that the resource is continuously updated." A missing date means one of three opposite things, and a reader cannot tell which. The American profile adds a fourth twist — "If there is a need to reflect that the dataset is continually updated, ISO 8601 formatting can account for this with repeating intervals" — so that its modification field may hold a repeating interval instead of a date, and "last updated" can carry a promise rather than a fact. For a dashboard, the stamp is three stamps or it misleads: the period the figure describes; the date the figure was last changed; and the cadence at which it is promised to change — with the word "promised" doing work, because section 7 is about the ways the promise fails.

The Data Quality Vocabulary, a W3C note, borrows two definitions that make freshness relative to a use rather than a clock. From an international standard, as the note reproduces it: currentness is "The degree to which data has attributes that are of the right age in a specific context of use." From the research literature: "Timeliness measures how up-to-date data is relative to a specific task". A footfall figure a week old is fresh for a quarterly report and stale for a live display; the same figure, two freshness judgements. Chapter 5 found that validity is of a reading for a use; freshness is too.

### Last updated is not last revised

The national statistics offices make the point that a published figure's date is not the date its value became final. The ONS's revisions policy for the national accounts: "A revision is a scheduled change to any published Office for National Statistics (ONS) output." "In early estimates of GDP, there is also inherent levels of uncertainty because of the trade-off between timeliness and accuracy." "Blue Book releases are usually open to revisions for the period back to 1997 (the earliest point for which data are available)." A figure for 1998 can still move. The Federal Reserve's archive of vintages states what that means for a reader who wants to know what was known when: it "allows you to retrieve each economic data release (vintage) that was available on a specific date in history." Chapter 7 called a vintage a snapshot; a dashboard that shows a series without saying which vintage it is showing has hidden the fact that the series it showed last quarter was a different series.

The consequence for the stamp is a fourth part, or a note beside the second: the date a figure was last *revised*, as distinct from the date the page last *refreshed*. The statistics office separates two kinds of change: "A revision is a scheduled change to any published Office for National Statistics (ONS) output"; "A correction is an unplanned revision to any published ONS output because of the identification of an error." A dashboard that carries a figure carries which of the two last touched it. Chapter 6's closures count is the simplest case — "not final until the third year" — and a dashboard that refreshes it nightly and stamps it "updated today" has told the truth about the page and not about the number.

### How fast things rot

The base rate against which a dashboard's dependencies should be judged is measured, and it is high. The Pew Research Center, in May 2024: "A quarter of all webpages that existed at one point between 2013 and 2023 are no longer accessible"; "38% of webpages that existed in 2013 are not available today" against "8% of pages that existed in 2023"; "21% of all the government webpages we examined contained at least one broken link"; and "54% of Wikipedia pages contain at least one link in their 'References' section that points to a page that no longer exists". The Harvard study of one newspaper's links, in its authors' own account for the *Columbia Journalism Review*: of 2,283,445 external links in 553,693 articles, "Of these deep links, 25 percent of all links were completely inaccessible", with the rot rising by age — "6 percent of links from 2018 had rotted, as compared to 43 percent of links from 2008 and 72 percent of links from 1998" — and "Fifty-three percent of all articles that contained deep links had at least one rotted link." Klein and colleagues, across 3.5 million scholarly articles: "We find one out of five STM articles suffering from reference rot, meaning it is impossible to revisit the web context that surrounds them some time after their publication." An older study found that a determined searcher could recover most lost addresses — "A single second searcher was able to locate 80 percent of the URLs reported as lost" — which is the distinction between the address rotting and the content being gone; a dashboard's automated fetch does not search.

Whether datasets rot at the same rate is not measured — no study of withdrawal from a UK portal was reachable, and the web-page figures above are the nearest evidence — but the instruments that once measured open-data freshness have themselves rotted. The Open Data Barometer's last edition found that "74% of the data we analysed is up-to-date, which is promising, but means that one quarter of all data surveyed has very limited value"; its page was last modified in 2018. The Global Open Data Index scored datasets on "Is the data up-to-date?" and now carries the notice "This is an archived project from the Open Knowledge Foundation and it is no longer active". The UK's own catalogue is mid-rename — "Since 2010, the data.gov.uk website, now known as the National Data Library" — and no measured study of its datasets' staleness was reachable. The one official form that retirement takes in the UK is the withdrawal banner, which keeps the address and says the date; a third-party archive now keeps a daily copy of the American federal catalogue. A dashboard built on links to other people's data is built on a base rate of a quarter gone in a decade, and the design question is not whether a source will go but what the dashboard does on the day it does.

## 5 · What an interface promises

### The UK's own standard for interfaces

An API — the published way one piece of software asks another for data — is a promise with terms, and the UK government has written down what it thinks the terms should be. Its API standards ask publishers to version carefully — "When you make new versions of your API, try not to make changes that will stop older versions of your API working properly"; "When you retire an old version you must tell users it is no longer supported and give them time to move on to the new version" — and to limit callers: "You should also implement rate limiting or throttling policies – making sure that users don't overuse your resources"; "Anonymous endpoints should also be rate limited to prevent excessive or malicious use." The one number in UK guidance is in the companion page on running a live interface: "You should try to give users 6 months notice if your API is 'live'." Beta interfaces are exempt — "You do not need to give as much notice if your API is in alpha or beta where your users are expecting breaking changes" — and, as section 6 finds, most public interfaces a place dashboard would use call themselves beta.

The guidance on service levels gives the provider's reason why a stated limit is the real limit, which a dashboard builder should read as a user: "When applying rate limiting or throttling, you should not let your users go over the limits you set even if you have spare capacity." "This is because if you need to take the spare capacity away in future and the user has developed their application with the higher rates in mind, their application could break". An interface that answered faster yesterday has made no promise. The same page gives the reason "uptime" hides the shape of failure: "your API could drop 1 in 20 connections or it could be unreachable for 2 hours a day" — the same percentage, a different experience.

### What the protocol guarantees, and what it does not

The only standardised signal that a caller has asked too often is a status code. "The 429 status code indicates that the user has sent too many requests in a given amount of time ('rate limiting')." The response "SHOULD include details explaining the condition, and MAY include a Retry-After header indicating how long to wait before making a new request" — *may*, not *must*. And the standard declines to say how the counting works: "Note that this specification does not define how the origin server identifies the user, nor how it counts requests." The header that would let a server advertise its quota in advance has been an Internet-Draft since before this book began; as of May 2026 it is at its eleventh revision with its intended status listed as "(None)". In practice every platform invents its own header, and a dashboard that calls several must learn several dialects. The protocol's own answer to the problem of asking too often is caching, which section 8 returns to; for now the rule is the one the national statistics office states for its own interface: "Your tooling should respect the Retry-After header and pause your requests for the specified duration", and "If this is not respected our algorithms may impose a block to our services for up to 1 hour."

### What the open licence promises

Most UK public data is published under the Open Government Licence, and a dashboard builder should read its last line first. The licence is generous: it grants "a worldwide, royalty-free, perpetual, non-exclusive licence to use the Information"; the licensee may "copy, publish, distribute and transmit the Information", "adapt the Information", and "exploit the Information commercially and non-commercially for example, by combining it with other Information, or by including it in your own product or application", in return for acknowledging the source — "Contains public sector information licensed under the Open Government Licence v3.0" — and not implying endorsement. It excludes "personal data in the Information" and "third party rights the Information Provider is not authorised to license". And it warrants nothing: "The Information is licensed 'as is' and the Information Provider and/or Licensor excludes all representations, warranties, obligations and liabilities in relation to the Information to the maximum extent permitted by law." "The Information Provider and/or Licensor are not liable for any errors or omissions in the Information". "The Information Provider does not guarantee the continued supply of the Information."

That last sentence is the licence's answer to belief four. It gives a perpetual right to reuse what has been received, and no right to keep receiving it. Transport for London's own licence, based on an earlier version of the same text, repeats it — "TfL does not guarantee the continued supply of the Information" — and adds "TfL may at any time revise this Licence without notice." The UK's open data is open the way a tap is open: the water that has run is yours, and the tap may be turned off.

## 6 · The interfaces a place dashboard would use

Section 5 read the standards and the licence. This section reads the interfaces themselves — the ones a dashboard about a place in the UK would most plausibly call — and reads them as technology only: what each states as its limit, what its terms permit, what it labels itself, and what it says will happen when it changes. No listing, event, track or venue was fetched from any of them; the chapter is interested in the pipe and not the water. Every page below was read on 6 October 2026, and most of them carry no date of their own, which is itself a finding: a limit read today from an undated page is a limit as of today.

| Food Standards Agency ratings API | None stated; a version header is mandatory | No | — | "at least one previous depreciated version" kept |
| Environment Agency flood-monitoring API | None stated; asks for "a single call every 15 mins" | No | "beta" | "do not offer a guarantee of service level" |
| OpenStreetMap Nominatim geocoder | "an absolute maximum of 1 request per second"; scheduled scripts "4 requests per minute" | No; a real User-Agent is required | — | "this usage policy may change without notice" |
| OpenStreetMap tile servers | None numeric; caching for "at least 7 days" required | No; a real User-Agent is required | — | "Availability is best-effort: there is no SLA or guarantee" |

Four things stand out from the table before any of it is interpreted.

First, the limits are stated in five different shapes. Transport for London and the Ordnance Survey count per minute; Companies House counts within a fixed five-minute window; the police interface runs "a 'leaky bucket' algorithm" that allows a burst and then a steady rate; the statistics office states three overlapping windows at once; Nomis limits cells rather than calls. The per-window and per-minute figures cannot be compared without arithmetic (the statistics office's 120 in ten seconds would allow 720 a minute if its 200 a minute did not also apply; whichever binds first binds), and a refresh job that reads from all of them must keep five different clocks. The Internet-Draft that would let a server state its policy in a standard header, section 5 noted, has no intended status, and so the clocks stay in the documentation and in the builder's head.

Second, the limits are not where the risk is. Every rate in the table but one — the geocoder's four a minute for scheduled scripts — is far above what a dashboard refreshed nightly, or even hourly, would use. A page that shows a borough's figures needs a few hundred calls a day, not a few hundred a minute. The number that will stop an unattended job is rarely the per-minute rate; it is the clause beside it. Transport for London deletes "User accounts that haven't made a request in the last year", and a job that has fallen silent for a year — because its source closed and nobody noticed — has lost its credentials as well. Companies House will "ban without notice" an application that "regularly" exceeds its limit — and a bug that retries in a loop is exactly such an application, which is why the same page explains that the limit exists "to protect client applications from unexpected loops". The statistics office blocks for a period that "varies and is dynamically changed by the ONS, depending on release schedules and observed demand", which means that the block is most likely on the morning a release lands, which is the morning the job most wants to run.

Third, several of the public interfaces that call themselves open also call themselves beta. The statistics office's interface is "open and unrestricted - no API keys are required" and in the same breath "currently in Beta and still being developed", with "breaking changes" expected. The Environment Agency's is "provided as open data under the Open Government Licence with no requirement for registration" and "should not be relied upon for safety critical applications". The UK's own guidance for live interfaces, section 5 showed, asks for six months' notice before an interface is deprecated or retired, and says that less is needed for "alpha or beta where your users are expecting breaking changes". Read together: the national statistics interface sits, by its own label, outside the UK's own notice expectation. A dashboard built on it is built on an interface that has told it so.

Fourth, one of them has written down what it will do when it closes something, and it is worth reading because so few have. The statistics office's "Guide to our approach to the retirement of API endpoints" says that a retiring endpoint will carry three response headers: `Deprecation`, "the date the decision was taken to deprecate this endpoint"; `Sunset`, "the date of when this service will cease to return data on its endpoints and instead return blanket 404 status codes"; and `Link`, "a link where you can find more information". "When an endpoint is retired, 404 error codes will be received by clients still querying the endpoint." Before that, "Rolling blackouts can be used to determine if any further users are impacted" — on the chapter's reading, the interface is switched off briefly, on purpose, to see who is still there. No minimum notice period is stated. This is the most explicit retirement procedure found in any UK public interface, and it means that a client which reads its response headers can see a closure coming, and a client which reads only the body cannot. The Food Standards Agency's version is shorter: it "will maintain at least one previous depreciated version of the API to allow developers to migrate their code steadily, however the FSA make no guarantees that older versions of the API will continue to work when they fall more than one version behind the current API" (read once; "depreciated" is the page's spelling). One version back is the promise; two is nothing.

### Where the catalogue points

The data.gov.uk directory deserves a separate paragraph because it is easily taken for a source when it is a signpost. Its API "uses the CKAN API and its extensions" and has no key and no limit, and what it returns is a catalogue: on the directory "you can: find links to data published by central government, local authorities and public bodies". The datasets live with their publishers, under their publishers' schedules, formats and retirements; the directory's durability says nothing about theirs. And the directory itself is mid-change — "Since 2010, the data.gov.uk website, now known as the National Data Library, has been helping people to find and use open government data", with the transformation begun "In early 2026". A dashboard that cites "data.gov.uk" as its source today is citing a name in the middle of being replaced. Section 4 made the point about dates; this is the same point about addresses.

### The police interface, read as a boundary on a map

One public interface in the table carries a method note that a map-maker has to read before plotting a single point, and it is quoted here as the publisher's own description of its method and for no other reason. The crime and anti-social-behaviour data at data.police.uk is published at locations that "always represent the approximate location of a crime — not the exact place that it happened". The publisher keeps "a master list of anonymous map points", and each point is chosen so that it "Appears over the centre point of a street, above a public place such as a Park or Airport, or above a commercial premise like a Shopping Centre or Nightclub", and "Has a catchment area which contains at least eight postal addresses or no postal addresses at all." Each incident is moved to the nearest such point; "If the nearest map point is more than 20km away, the co-ordinates are zeroed out." The list "was created in 2012" and "refreshed in 2022". The publisher adds that "Inconsistent geocoding policies in police forces mean we cannot be confident that the location data provided is fully accurate or consistent."

What this means for a dashboard is a matter of technology, not of crime. A dot on a map drawn from this interface is a dot on a snap point, and snap points are, by design, placed on the centres of streets and on named commercial premises. It follows — this is the chapter's inference, not the publisher's sentence — that a premises which sits at a snap point will appear to be where incidents happen, because incidents from the surrounding catchment are moved onto it. A heat map, a count by venue, a "hotspot" — each is a picture of the snap list as much as of the events, and the snap list is a 2012 drawing refreshed once. Chapter 4 asked that a provenance set declare how a location was produced; this interface shows why. The method is public, which is to the publisher's credit; it is also easy not to read.

### The ticketing interfaces, read as technology

A dashboard about where people go at night would want to know what is on and where. The ticketing platforms are where that is written down, and so the chapter read their developer pages — their terms, their limits, their labels — and nothing else. What follows is what those pages say about themselves; whether a dashboard should use any of them is a question for the prototype and for Chapter 10, and this chapter does not answer it.

Of the UK platforms tried, two publish developer documentation, one publishes it in a form this book's tools could not read, and four publish none that could be found.

Skiddle's interface is "still in Beta and being developed". Its limits are not stated: "We monitor all requests and reserve the right to rate-limit or block any excessive requests. Limits are continually changed - please contact us if you are expecting to hit our API frequently." Its default is non-commercial: "The API is for non-commercial use only. Any commercial use must be first approved in writing by emailing dev@skiddle.com". Its technical documentation lives in a code repository whose banner reads "This repository was archived by the owner on Apr 30, 2024. It is now read-only." The documented endpoints are events, venues and artists: it is a listings interface, and nothing in either source describes sales, orders or attendance. So the interface is beta, its limits are whatever they are this week, its documentation is frozen, and its licence is non-commercial by default — four facts about the pipe that a dashboard would need to have decided about before the first call.

Ticketmaster's is the opposite case: precise, dated, and written with an unattended job in mind. "All API keys are issued with a default quota of 5000 API calls per day and rate limitation of 5 requests per second." The Discovery interface "allows you to search for events, attractions, or venues" and pages no deeper than the thousandth result. Its Terms of Use, last updated 27 June 2023, forbid the client to "Cache or store any Event Content other than for reasonable periods in order to provide the service you are providing", and then say the sentence that every builder of a scheduled refresh should read twice: "Further, we reserve the right to rate limit or block applications that make a large number of calls to the API that are not primarily in response to direct user actions." A nightly job's calls are, by definition, not responses to direct user actions, and whether a few hundred a day is "a large number" is the platform's to decide. The terms also permit the platform to "change, suspend, or discontinue any aspect of the Ticketmaster API at any time, including the availability of any Ticketmaster API", and to restrict access "without notice or liability"; the interface is "PROVIDED ON AN 'AS IS' AND 'AS AVAILABLE' BASIS". Higher limits are granted "on case-by-case basis" to applications that comply with the terms, the branding guide and "representing the Ticketmaster data properly". One further detail is a matter of technology and is recorded as such: the terms define "Event Content" to include "Tickets, Attendees, Orders", while the public Discovery interface, as far as the fetch tool's reader could see, exposes no such fields. The definition covers more than the public pipe delivers.

Eventbrite's developer site could not be read: every page tried was a script-only shell that returned nothing to a text reader, and so the removal of its event-search endpoint, which the research expected to confirm, is recorded as unverified. One weak observation stands beside it — an unauthenticated request to the old search address returned a 404 on 6 October 2026 — and it is marked weak because an absence is not the platform's statement. DICE, Resident Advisor, See Tickets and Fatsoma publish no developer documentation that could be found; DICE has a partner endpoint behind a login, and the others returned a 404, a 403 and an application shell respectively. For four of the ticketing platforms, then, no public interface could be found to read terms from, which is its own answer about dependency: a dashboard cannot build on a pipe it has not been offered.

### The streaming interfaces, read as technology

The same discipline, applied to where music is listened to. These pages were read for their limits, their terms and their history of change. Nothing about what is on them was fetched.

Spotify's Web API has the most completely documented recent history of any interface in this chapter, and the history is of narrowing. Its rate limit "is calculated based on the number of calls that your app makes to Spotify in a rolling 30 second window", with no number given; a 429 "will normally include a Retry-After header with a value in seconds". New applications begin in "development mode", in which "Up to 5 authenticated Spotify users can use an app" and "The app owner must have a Spotify Premium account for apps in development mode to function." Then the dated sequence:

- **27 November 2024.** A post titled "Introducing some changes to our Web API": "Effective today, new Web API use cases will no longer be able to access or use the following endpoints and functionality in their third-party applications." The list, read identically on two passes: Related Artists; Recommendations; Audio Features; Audio Analysis; Get Featured Playlists; Get Category's Playlists; 30-second preview URLs in multi-get responses; algorithmic and Spotify-owned editorial playlists. The reason given was "creating a more secure platform". The notice period was none.
- **15 May 2025.** From a post of 15 April: "Starting May 15, 2025, extended Web API access will be reserved for apps with established, scalable, and impactful use cases". The quota-modes page now states that "as of May 15th 2025, Spotify only accepts applications from organizations (not individuals)", with criteria including an "Established Business Entity" and "Maintaining a minimum of active users (at least 250k MAUs)". The notice period was one month. Those already granted access "will remain unaffected".
- **11 February and 9 March 2026.** A migration guide for "the February 2026 announcement": new development-mode applications created under new restrictions from 11 February, existing ones migrated on 9 March. Batch endpoints were removed for development-mode applications — one request per track where one request fetched many (read once) — while "Apps in extended quota mode are not affected by any of the changes described in this guide". The guide gives no reason.

Three changes in fifteen months, the first with no notice for new applications and the second with one month's, each moving the free tier further from anything that could run a public display. A small studio is not a business with a quarter of a million monthly users, and so the extended mode is, on the stated criteria, closed to it; the development mode admits five authenticated users of the application — a page that drew only on its owner's credential is a different case, and the terms below still apply to it. The chapter records that and nothing more about whether to proceed.

The terms are the other half, and they are quoted because they describe dashboard-making almost exactly. Spotify's Developer Policy, effective 15 May 2025, clause III.13: "Do not analyze the Spotify Content or the Spotify Service for any purpose, including without limitation, creating new or derived listenership metrics, benchmarking, functionality, usage statistics, user metrics". Clause III.5: "Do not create any product or service which is integrated with streams or content from another service." The Developer Terms, version 10 of the same date: "you may not store, aggregate or create compilations or databases of Spotify Content, other than as strictly necessary to operate your SDA"; "Do not store Spotify Content indefinitely." "Spotify may limit the number of service calls that your SDA may make, the volume of Spotify Content that may be accessed, or anything else about the Spotify Service as Spotify deems appropriate, in its sole discretion, without notice." "Spotify has no obligation to maintain, support, upgrade, or update the Spotify Platform or Spotify Service, or to provide all or any specific content." A measure derived from listening, combined with another source, stored over time — three clauses, three prohibitions. The clause numbers are those the fetch tool's reader returned, and the research agent noted that sub-letter labels wavered between passes; cite them by checking the page, not from here.

YouTube's Data API is included for one reason: its quota changed four months before this chapter was researched, and so it is a live example of a number going stale inside a year. The research brief expected the long-standing rule that a search cost 100 of a daily 10,000 units; the page read on 6 October 2026 says "A search query costs 1 unit", and the revision history for 1 June 2026 says "The YouTube Data API is transitioning to a granular quota system covering smaller sets of methods, starting with videos.insert and search.list", each charged "to their own respective quota buckets" — a default of "100 search.list calls" a day. The agent's observation, marked as its own, is that a hundred searches a day is what ten thousand units at a hundred each used to buy, so the practical ceiling on searching is about where it was and the accounting has moved. Whether or not that is right, a book that printed the long-standing hundred-units-per-search rule in May 2026 was wrong by June.

The smaller interfaces each add one fact about the shape of a dependency. MusicBrainz declines over its rate with a 503 rather than a 429 — "Currently that rate is (on average) 1 request per second" — and also has a global cap, "300 requests each second (on average)", so a client that behaves may still be declined because of everyone else; its rules page dates itself "As of 2012-01-08", and "We may change the blocking/throttling rules at any time in order to protect the overall site health." Last.fm's terms permit the data "solely for non-commercial purposes", require a "powered by AudioScrobbler" button linking back, set a "Reasonable Usage Cap" of "a maximum of 100 MB, as may be updated by Last.fm from time to time", and reserve the right to terminate "with or without cause"; the page carries no date. SoundCloud's registration appears to be open again behind a paid artist tier, with stream-access calls "limited to 15,000 per 24-hour window" and no aggregate limit "currently enforced" — all read once and not re-verified, and recorded only as "check before relying". Bandcamp's interface is for "Labels and merchandise fulfillment partners" who "interact with their accounts", offering account, sales-report and merch-order endpoints and no public catalogue: it is an interface for a seller to read its own books, not for anyone to read the shop.

### How interfaces close: the pattern

Put the histories side by side and a pattern appears that no single page shows. On the handful of cases here, interfaces close in one of three ways, and the amount of notice follows the closer's stated reason.

**On the day, for safety or privacy.** On 4 April 2018 Facebook's chief technology officer published "An Update on Our Plans to Restrict Data Access on Facebook". Of the Events API: "Starting today, apps using the API will no longer be able to access the guest list or posts on the event wall." Of the Pages API: "So starting today, all future access to the Pages API will need to be approved by Facebook." And: "We're making the recently announced deprecation of the Instagram Platform API effective today." The justification was the one that had filled the previous two weeks' news, and the notice was none. Spotify's 27 November 2024 post used the same form — "Effective today" — and the same justification, "a more secure platform". The lesson is not that the reasons were bad. It is that when an interface closes for a reason of this kind, it closes on the day, and no amount of notice policy survives it.

**On a month's notice, for money.** Twitter's turn is reported here through a journalist, because the company's own forum post is closed to this book's tools and so every sentence is TechCrunch's, 29 March 2023: "Twitter's API saga started when the company announced in February that it is ending free API access in just a matter of days"; the new free tier "provides only 1,500 post requests per month"; "over the next 30 days, the company will discontinue old access levels". The platform's current pricing page, read at source, no longer carries any of those tiers: "The X API uses pay-per-usage pricing. No subscriptions—pay only for what you use", with plans "capped at 3 million Post reads per monthly billing cycle" — a second re-pricing since 2023, on the pricing page as it stands. Reddit's 2023 change could not be read at source either; its current wiki, updated 11 May 2026, states "100 queries per minute (QPM) per OAuth client id" and that "Traffic not using OAuth or login credentials will be blocked". Spotify's second step, one month from announcement to effect, belongs here too.

**On a year's notice, by contract — with exceptions.** The best case the research found is in Google's Maps Platform terms, last modified 26 August 2026, section 1.3(c): "Google will notify Customer at least 12 months before discontinuing a Service (or associated material functionality) without providing a materially similar replacement", and "at least 12 months before modifying any Customer-facing Google APIs in a backwards-incompatible manner." The next sentence takes some of it back: "Nothing in this Section 1.3(c) limits Google's ability to make changes required to comply with applicable law, address a material security risk, or avoid a substantial economic or material technical burden. This Section 1.3(c) does not apply to pre-general availability Services, offerings or functionality". So even the contractual year has three doors in it, and one of them — security — is the door Facebook and Spotify walked through on the day.

Set against these, the UK's own expectation, from section 5, is six months as "should try" and only for an interface that calls itself live. The table of notice promised, then, runs: twelve months by contract with exceptions (Google Maps); six months as an aspiration (UK government, live interfaces only); none stated (ONS, Skiddle, Ticketmaster, Spotify, Last.fm, Nominatim, the tile servers). Most of what a place dashboard would call is in the last group.

This is the evidence for belief three. An interface is not a tap. It is a service run by someone else for their reasons, on terms they may revise without telling you, and — this is the chapter's reading of the cases above — its history, more than its documentation, is the guide to what it will do next. A dashboard that depends on one should know which of the three closing patterns its supplier has used before, and should be built so that the closure is a loss of one series and not of the page. Section 8 is about how.

### Terms that forbid what a dashboard does

Read across the terms as a set, a last and uncomfortable pattern appears. The open-data licences forbid almost nothing and guarantee nothing. The platform terms guarantee nothing either, and they forbid, specifically, the things a dashboard does.

A dashboard *stores*: it keeps what it fetched so that it can show a series. Spotify: "you may not store, aggregate or create compilations or databases"; "Do not store Spotify Content indefinitely." Ticketmaster: no caching "other than for reasonable periods in order to provide the service you are providing". Google Maps: "Customer will not cache Google Maps Content except as expressly permitted under the Maps Service Specific Terms." The tile servers go the other way and *require* a seven-day cache, their stated problem being capacity rather than ownership; the Nominatim geocoder likewise: "Results must be cached on your side."

A dashboard *aggregates and derives*: it turns records into a measure. Spotify III.13 forbids "creating new or derived listenership metrics, benchmarking, functionality, usage statistics, user metrics".

A dashboard *combines*: it puts one source beside another. Spotify III.5: "Do not create any product or service which is integrated with streams or content from another service." Google Maps 3.2.3(e): "Customer will not use the Google Maps Core Services with or near a non-Google Map in a Customer Application."

A dashboard *calls on a schedule*: a job fetches at three in the morning with no one at the keyboard. Ticketmaster: the right to "rate limit or block applications that make a large number of calls to the API that are not primarily in response to direct user actions." Nominatim: "scripts that are run at regular intervals are restricted to 4 requests per minute".

A dashboard built by a studio *is, or may become, commercial*: Skiddle, "non-commercial use only"; Last.fm, "solely for non-commercial purposes"; Spotify's Developer Policy, "Except for the limited commercial uses for Non-Streaming SDAs (set out below) commercial uses are not permitted for SDAs" (read once; an "SDA" is the policy's term for an application built on the platform). Whether a studio's free public page is "commercial" under any of these is a legal reading this chapter cannot give; it is a question for line 2 of the service record, with the clause quoted.

And a dashboard *shows a source's name beside another's*: Spotify requires attribution "by using the Spotify Marks" and forbids offering metadata "as a standalone service or product"; Last.fm wants its button; Google Maps forbids the map near a non-Google map. The open licence asks only for a line of acknowledgement.

None of this says that a place dashboard cannot be built. It says that a dashboard which draws on platform interfaces is, in each of its ordinary operations, doing something at least one of its suppliers' terms addresses directly, and that the permission to do it is theirs to give and theirs to withdraw. The open public interfaces, by contrast, permit all of it and promise only that the tap may be turned off. Those are two different kinds of dependency and they belong in two different lines of the service record that section 12 sets out: the first is a dependency on terms, the second a dependency on supply. A builder who has read this section knows which kind each line of their page is.

### What this section leaves open

The interfaces have been read as they stand on one day. Three things the research expected could not be confirmed at source — the Eventbrite endpoint removal, the Reddit and Twitter 2023 announcements in the platforms' own words, the Ordnance Survey's free allowances behind a script-only page — and are listed as unreached in the sources. The retirement header RFCs that the statistics office implements were not fetched and are a thread worth pulling `[Further research]`. And the question this section most carefully did not answer — whether the studio's prototype should call any of the platform interfaces, on these terms, for this purpose — is the prototype's `[Prototype flag]` and Chapter 10's, and it should be answered with these pages open.

## 7 · What breaks, and how you would know

Sections 3 to 6 have been about what a living dashboard depends on. This section is about what happens when a dependency gives way, and it draws on the one body of practice that has written this down in full: the reliability engineering literature that grew up around running large services, most of it published openly by Google's site reliability engineers. That literature is practitioner authority, not measured evidence, and it is treated here as a careful account of how services fail, by people whose job was to watch them do it. Its lessons scale down further than its authors intended, because the failures it describes are not failures of scale. They are failures of chains, clocks and attention, and a small dashboard has all three.

### Availability is a chain

The first thing to understand is the arithmetic of a chain. A dashboard page that, when a reader opens it, calls a statistics interface, a geocoder, a tile server and a hosting platform is a service with four dependencies, and "A service cannot be more available than the intersection of all its critical dependencies." The sentence is from an article in the ACM's practitioner magazine, *Queue*, by four Google engineers, and it defines its terms: "A critical dependency is one that, if it malfunctions, causes a corresponding malfunction in the service." The authors' rule of thumb follows: "critical dependencies must offer one additional 9 relative to your service … because any service will have several critical dependencies, as well as its own idiosyncratic problems." If you want your page to be up 99.9 per cent of the time, each thing it cannot do without should be up 99.99.

The article does not print the multiplication, so the following is the chapter's own arithmetic, under the usual simplifying assumptions that the dependencies fail independently and that each is critical. Three dependencies at 99.9 per cent give 0.999 × 0.999 × 0.999 = 99.70 per cent. Five give 99.50. Ten give 99.00. The reliability engineers' own conversion table turns those percentages into time: 99.9 per cent allows "8.76 hours" of unavailability a year; 99.5 per cent allows 1.83 days; 99 per cent allows "3.65 days". A page that fetches live from ten sources, each individually excellent, has given itself three and a half days a year of showing nothing — and that is before any of the sources has a bad year, and before its own hosting is counted.

The conclusion is not "use fewer sources". It is the one the same article draws: "Can the service continue serving in degraded mode if one of its dependencies fails? In other words, design for graceful degradation." And it is the one section 8 builds on: a dependency that is called when the page is *built* is not a dependency of the page when it is *read*. The arithmetic above applies to whatever the page needs at the moment someone opens it. Move the calls to the night before, keep the result, and the reader's page depends on one thing — the host — while the night's job depends on ten and can fail without the reader seeing it. That is the move, and the rest of this section is about doing it without fooling yourself.

### Up is not working

Reliability engineers define four things to watch in any service they run — "The four golden signals of monitoring are latency, traffic, errors, and saturation" — and it is the third that matters most for a dashboard, because of how it is defined. Errors are "The rate of requests that fail, either explicitly (e.g., HTTP 500s), implicitly (for example, an HTTP 200 success response, but coupled with the wrong content), or by policy (for example, 'If you committed to one-second response times, any request over one second is an error')."

Read the middle clause again. A page that loads, returns a success code, and shows last month's figures under this month's heading has, in this definition, failed. An uptime monitor — the tool that pings a page every minute and reports "99.98 per cent" — will never see it, because the page came back. This is the evidence for belief five, and it is why the question "is the dashboard up?" is the wrong question to automate. The right question is "is what the dashboard shows the thing it says it is?", and no status code answers that.

The reliability literature draws the same distinction from the other side. "Your monitoring system should address two questions: what's broken, and why? The 'what's broken' indicates the symptom; the 'why' indicates a (possibly intermediate) cause." A stale page is a symptom; the failed job behind it is a cause. Watch the symptom. The SRE workbook's chapter on data pipelines gives the forms the watching takes, and they are the forms a dashboard can use directly — "Most pipeline data freshness SLOs are in one of the following formats:" "X% of data processed in Y [seconds, days, minutes]"; "The oldest data is no older than Y [seconds, days, minutes]"; "The pipeline job has completed successfully within Y [seconds, days, minutes]." The second of these is a sentence a dashboard can print on its face and check against itself: *the oldest data here is no older than thirty-five days*. If it is older, the page is in error, however well it loads. The same workbook also states the asymmetry that governs the whole of section 8: "Stale data is almost always better than incorrect data." The first is a known quantity with a date on it; the second is a trap.

### How much failure to allow

The literature's second lesson is that perfection is not the target, and this is said in order to be believed rather than as consolation. "100% is the wrong reliability target for basically everything (pacemakers and anti-lock brakes being notable exceptions)." "100% is probably never the right reliability target: not only is it impossible to achieve, it's typically more reliability than a service's users want or notice." "Put simply, a user on a 99% reliable smartphone cannot tell the difference between 99.99% and 99.999% service reliability!" And the mechanism that follows: a service sets a target — the *objective* — and the gap between the target and perfection is a budget. "The error budget provides a clear, objective metric that determines how unreliable the service is allowed to be within a single quarter." While there is budget left, change is allowed; when it is spent, change stops until reliability recovers.

The vocabulary is worth keeping because the three words are used loosely everywhere else. "An SLI is a service level indicator—a carefully defined quantitative measure of some aspect of the level of service that is provided." "An SLO is a service level objective: a target value or range of values for a service level that is measured by an SLI." "Finally, SLAs are service level agreements: an explicit or implicit contract with your users that includes consequences of meeting (or missing) the SLOs they contain." An indicator is what you measure; an objective is what you aim at; an agreement is what happens if you miss. The public interfaces in section 6 that "do not offer a guarantee of service level" are declining to sign the third; their users are left to measure the first themselves.

Two further sentences from the same chapter bear on a dashboard that others come to rely on. "Users build on the reality of what you offer, rather than what you say you'll supply, particularly for infrastructure services." And the practice that follows from it at Google's lock service, which is quoted here because it is startling and because the chapter does not recommend it: "In any given quarter, if a true failure has not dropped availability below the target, a controlled outage will be synthesized by intentionally taking down the system." A service that is more reliable than it promised trains its users to depend on the excess. A dashboard that has refreshed every night for a year without fail has made a promise its builder never wrote down — which is the practical reason for writing the promise down, in the service record, before the year begins. The UK's own guidance for interface providers, section 5 noted, gives the same reason from the provider's side: do not let users exceed the stated limit "even if you have spare capacity", because when the slack is withdrawn "their application could break".

For a dashboard, the useful move is to set the objective in the data's terms rather than the page's. Not "the page is up 99.9 per cent of the month" but "the newest figure on the page was published no more than N days ago, in 11 months of 12". That is an objective that can be measured by reading the page's own dates, that fails in the way readers notice, and that leaves a budget — one missed month a year — within which the night's job may break without anyone being woken.

### Alerts, tickets and logs

When the objective is missed, someone has to know, and here the literature is at its most useful for a small studio because it is about attention, which is the scarce thing. The reliability engineers divide every signal a system can send into three. "Alerts: Signify that a human needs to take action immediately in response to something that is either happening or about to happen, in order to improve the situation." "Tickets: Signify that a human needs to take action, but not immediately. The system cannot automatically handle the situation, but if a human takes action in a few days, no damage will result." "Logging: No one needs to look at this information, but it is recorded for diagnostic or forensic purposes. The expectation is that no one reads logs unless something else prompts them to do so."

The principle above the three is "Monitoring should never require a human to interpret any part of the alerting domain. Instead, software should do the interpreting, and humans should be notified only when they need to take action." And the discipline for the first category is strict: "Every page should be actionable" — a page, here, being an alert to a person's pager. "Every page response should require intelligence. If a page merely merits a robotic response, it shouldn't be a page." "Pages should be about a novel problem or an event that hasn't been seen before." The reason is human: "Every time the pager goes off, I should be able to react with a sense of urgency. I can only react with a sense of urgency a few times a day before I become fatigued." "When pages occur too frequently, employees second-guess, skim, or even ignore incoming alerts, sometimes even ignoring a 'real' page that's masked by the noise."

For a dashboard refreshed nightly, almost nothing is an alert. A failed refresh is a ticket: the page still shows last night's figures with last night's date, no damage results if someone looks in a few days, and the fix needs a person who can read the error. The log is the job's output, kept where it can be found when the ticket arrives. The one alert is the one that means the page is lying — the date on the face no longer matching the data behind it — and section 8 is about building so that this cannot happen, so that nothing has to be an alert at all.

This is also where belief six is answered precisely. Automation does not remove the person; it changes the person's job from doing the refresh to reading the ticket. "Humans add latency", the same chapter says — and a studio that has automated its refresh and routed its tickets to an inbox nobody opens has automated the failure and not the recovery. The literature's test for whether the automation is finished is Carla Geisser's, quoted in the chapter on toil: "If a human operator needs to touch your system during normal operations, you have a bug. The definition of normal changes as your systems grow." Reading a ticket about an abnormal operation is not toil. It is the job that remains.

### Scheduled jobs fail quietly

Everything in this section so far assumes that the night's job either runs or sends a signal. The third lesson is that the schedulers a small studio would use do neither reliably, and that each one says so in its own documentation.

The oldest scheduler, `cron`, has one reporting channel: "When executing commands, any output is mailed to the owner of the crontab (or to the user specified in the MAILTO environment variable in the crontab, if such exists)." "The syslog output will be used instead of mail, when sendmail is not installed." That is the whole of it. On a direct reading of both manual pages, neither says anything about a job's exit status or defines a failed job; the chapter's inference from that silence, marked as such, is that `cron` has no notion of failure to report. A job that fails with no output sends nothing. A job that never starts sends nothing. The manual does record one way a job silently never starts — "non-existent times, such as the 'missing hours' during the daylight savings time conversion, will never match, causing jobs scheduled during the 'missing times' not to be run" — and one way it runs twice. And the reliability engineers' own chapter on cron puts the deepest limit plainly: "Cron's failure domain is essentially just one machine." "If the machine is not running, neither the cron scheduler nor the jobs it launches can run."

The scheduler a studio without a machine would use — the free one attached to its code hosting — is documented with unusual candour, and the candour should be read as a list of ways the job will not run. "The shortest interval you can run scheduled workflows is once every 5 minutes." "The schedule event can be delayed during periods of high loads of GitHub Actions workflow runs." "High load times include the start of every hour." "If the load is sufficiently high enough, some queued jobs may be dropped." Dropped is not delayed: it is not run. "To decrease the chance of delay, schedule your workflow to run at a different time of the hour" — and Simon Willison's scraper, described in section 8, runs at six, twenty-six and forty-six minutes past for a reason of his own that comes to the same thing: "I like to offset my cron times like this since I assume that the majority of crons run exactly on the hour, so running not-on-the-hour feels polite." Then the clause that will catch a finished dashboard: "In a public repository, scheduled workflows are automatically disabled when no repository activity has occurred in 60 days." A dashboard whose code is stable is a repository that goes quiet; whether the job's own nightly commits count as "repository activity" is not stated on the page, and a studio should find out rather than assume. Then the clause that gives the alarm a bus factor of one: "Notifications for scheduled workflows are sent to the user who last modified the cron syntax in the workflow file." When that person leaves, the failure notices go to them. One thing has improved since earlier documentation: "By default, scheduled workflows run in UTC. You can optionally specify a timezone using an IANA timezone string for timezone-aware scheduling." Anything written earlier that said the schedule was UTC only is out of date, and the correction is itself a small instance of the chapter's subject.

The reliability engineers' answer to all of this is a single sentence: "Cron job owners can (and should!) monitor their cron jobs; for example, an owner might have the cron service expose state for its managed cron jobs, or set up independent monitoring of the effect of cron jobs." *The effect.* Do not ask the scheduler whether the job ran; look at whether the thing the job was supposed to change has changed. For a dashboard that is one check — did the data's date advance? — and it can be done by something other than the job itself, which is the point.

The practice built on that sentence is the *heartbeat*, or dead man's switch. A monitoring service "listens for HTTP requests ('pings') from your cron jobs and scheduled tasks"; "It keeps silent as long as pings arrive on time." "It raises an alert as soon as a ping does not arrive on time." The inversion is the design: the job's silence, which `cron` and the free scheduler both treat as success, becomes the signal. A job that was dropped, disabled after sixty days, or killed by a changed interface never pings, and the monitor — which does not depend on the job, the scheduler or the machine — raises the ticket. The job should send its ping *after* it has confirmed the effect, not when it starts, or the monitor reports a job that began and not one that finished. The monitor is itself a dependency, with terms, a price and an owner, and it takes a line in the service record like any other.

Two design rules from the same chapter complete the picture. The first is about running twice: "Some cron jobs, such as garbage collection processes, are idempotent. In case of system malfunction, it is safe to launch such jobs multiple times. Other cron jobs, such as a process that sends out an email newsletter to a wide distribution, should not be launched more than once". A dashboard refresh must be the first kind — in the workbook's definition, "An idempotent mutation is a type of mutation that can be applied multiple times with the same result" — so that a job run twice by a confused scheduler, or re-run by hand after a failure, leaves the page exactly as one run would. The second is about which way to err: "In general, we favor skipping launches rather than risking double launches, as much as the infrastructure allows." For a refresh that keeps the last good copy, a skipped night costs one day of age; a doubled night, badly built, can cost the data.

### The chain, the clock and the person

Put the three lessons together and the shape of a reliable small dashboard is already visible, before any architecture is chosen. Its availability is bounded by what it needs at the moment of reading, so it should need as little as possible then. Its failure is a date on the face falling behind, not a page refusing to load, so that is what should be watched. Its scheduler will fail quietly, so something independent of the scheduler should watch the effect and raise a ticket to a *role* — an address the studio reads — and not to whoever last edited a file. And the person is not removed; the person reads the ticket. Section 8 is about the architectures that make this cheap.

## 8 · The no-admin architectures

"No admin" is the studio's phrase for a dashboard that nobody has to tend, and the previous section has already narrowed what it can mean: nobody tends the *refresh*; somebody reads the *ticket*. Within that, there is a family of architectures built precisely so that a small team can run a public, data-backed page for years at little cost in money and, if section 7 is heeded, in attention. They share one idea — do the work before the reader arrives, and keep the result — and the literature on each is short, open and in its originators' own words. This section reads it in the order a builder would meet it: the protocol that already knows how to keep a stale copy; the patterns for failing without falling over; the architectures that move the work to build time; the arithmetic of live against nightly; and the cost, in attention, that none of them removes.

### The protocol already knows how to be stale

HTTP, the protocol every page is served over, has had a complete vocabulary for freshness for decades, and a builder who learns it finds that half the problem is solved by headers. The caching standard, RFC 9111, defines a cache as "a local store of response messages and the subsystem that controls storage, retrieval, and deletion of messages in it", whose purpose is "to reduce the response time and network bandwidth consumption on future equivalent requests". Then the three definitions this chapter has been using since section 1: "A response's 'freshness lifetime' is the length of time between its generation by the origin server and its expiration time." "A 'fresh' response is one whose age has not yet exceeded its freshness lifetime." "Conversely, a 'stale' response is one where it has."

The protocol does not stop at the definitions. It carries the age on the wire — "The 'Age' response header field conveys the sender's estimate of the time since the response was generated or successfully validated at the origin server" — so that what the reader receives says how old it is. It lets the origin declare the lifetime: "The 'max-age' response directive indicates that the response is to be considered stale after its age is greater than the specified number of seconds." It defines how a cache asks whether a stale copy is still good without fetching it again — a conditional request, answered by "A 304 (Not Modified) response status code" which "indicates that the stored response can be updated and reused" — and the entity tag, in the secondary gloss of the Mozilla documentation, is "an identifier for a specific version of a resource" that "lets caches be more efficient and save bandwidth, as a web server does not need to resend a full response if the content has not changed."

And it rules on the question that matters most: may a stale copy be served at all? "A cache MUST NOT generate a stale response unless it is disconnected or doing so is explicitly permitted by the client or origin server (e.g., by the max-stale request directive in Section 5.2.1, extension directives such as those defined in [RFC5861], or configuration in accordance with an out-of-band contract)." Disconnected, or permitted. And when a cache tries to check and the source errs: "if a cache receives a 5xx (Server Error) response while attempting to validate a response, it can either forward this response to the requesting client or act as if the server failed to respond" — in which case, the next sentence says, "the cache can send a previously stored response, subject to its constraints on doing so (see Section 4.2.4), or retry the validation request."

The extension the standard points to, RFC 5861, is the one that turns "the source is down" into "the reader sees yesterday's page", and it is Informational rather than Standards Track, which should be said. It "defines two independent HTTP Cache-Control extensions that allow control over the use of stale responses by caches." The first: "The stale-while-revalidate HTTP Cache-Control extension allows a cache to immediately return a stale response while it revalidates it in the background, thereby hiding latency (both in the network and on the server) from clients." The second: "The stale-if-error Cache-Control extension indicates that when an error is encountered, a cached stale response MAY be used to satisfy the request, regardless of other freshness information." "In this context, an error is any situation that would result in a 500, 502, 503, or 504 HTTP response status code being returned." Two directives in a header: serve the old copy while you fetch the new one; serve the old copy if the new one fails. A dashboard whose data layer is behind a cache that honours these has its degraded mode already, written by the protocol's authors in 2010.

The lesson for a builder is not that headers are enough — the page still has to say how old what it shows is, in words a reader can read, which the `Age` header does not do. It is that "keep the last good copy and say its age" is not a clever studio invention. It is how the web already works when it is allowed to.

### Failing without falling over

Above the protocol sit the patterns, and three of them are enough for a small dashboard. They are named by the people who named them.

The *circuit breaker* is Michael Nygard's, popularised in Martin Fowler's 2014 article, which gives the definition this chapter uses: "You wrap a protected function call in a circuit breaker object, which monitors for failures. Once the failures reach a certain threshold, the circuit breaker trips, and all further calls to the circuit breaker return with an error, without the protected call being made at all." The danger it guards against is the one section 6's interfaces create: "remote calls can fail, or hang without a response until some timeout limit is reached", and "if you have many callers on a unresponsive supplier, then you can run out of critical resources leading to cascading failures across multiple systems." Nygard's own chapter on the antipatterns, in the free excerpt, is blunter: "Integration points are the number-one killer of systems." "Every single one of those feeds presents a stability risk. Every socket, process, pipe, or remote procedure call can and will hang." For a refresh job that calls ten interfaces, the breaker is what stops one slow interface from stalling the other nine, and Fowler adds the two things a dashboard needs from it: the fallback — "failure to get some data may be mitigated by showing some stale data that's good enough to display" — and the signal — "Usually you'll also want some kind of monitor alert if the circuit breaker trips." "Any change in breaker state should be logged."

The *bulkhead* is the ship's partition. In the current wording of Microsoft's architecture guidance: "Isolate the elements of an application into pools so that if one element fails, the others continue to function." "This pattern is named after the sectioned partitions (bulkheads) of a ship's hull. If the hull of a ship is compromised, only the damaged section fills with water, which prevents the ship from sinking." For a dashboard, the bulkheads are the series. Each source's data is fetched, stored and shown in its own compartment, so that a closed interface floods one panel and not the page. Section 6 said the closure should be "a loss of one series and not of the page"; this is the pattern that makes it so.

*Graceful degradation* is the principle both of the others serve, and the clearest statement found is in Amazon's Well-Architected framework, as a best practice it rates "High" risk to omit: "Application components should continue to perform their core function even if dependencies become unavailable. They might be serving slightly stale data, alternate data, or even no data." "Failure modes of components should be seen as normal operation." The same page lists anti-patterns, and two of them describe the way most dashboards actually break. "Serving no data on errors or when only one out of multiple dependencies is unavailable and partial results can still be returned." And, exactly: "Invalidating or emptying local state as a result of a failed refresh without considering the consequences of doing so." The nightly job that fails and *blanks the page* — that replaces last night's good copy with nothing because tonight's fetch returned an error — is a named anti-pattern in the framework of the largest cloud provider. The remedy is the one the protocol already offered: on failure, keep the last good copy, and say its age.

One warning from the reliability engineers' chapter on cascading failure should be fixed to the degraded mode like a label: "Remember that the code path you never use is the code path that (often) doesn't work." The fallback that shows yesterday's data when today's fails will, if it is never exercised, fail on the night it is needed. Exercise it. A degraded mode that has never been seen is a hope, not a design. And when the job retries, "Always use randomized exponential backoff when scheduling retries" — wait longer each time, and not at the same instant as everyone else who was also refused.

### Do the work before the reader arrives

The architectures that make all this cheap share one move, and Simon Willison has named its two halves.

The first half is *git scraping*, from his post of 9 October 2020: "Git scraping is the name I've given a scraping technique that I've been experimenting with for a few years now. It's really effective, and more people should use it." The idea: "We already have a great tool for efficiently tracking changes to text over time: Git. And GitHub Actions (and other CI systems) make it easy to create a scraper that runs every few minutes, records the current state of a resource and records changes to that resource over time in the commit history." His own example: "Every 20 minutes it grabs the latest copy of that JSON endpoint, pretty-prints it (for diff readability) using jq and commits it back to the repo if it has changed." The result is "an extremely effective way of turning all sorts of interesting data sources into a changelog over time." For a dashboard, this is the archive that section 4 said a living series owes: every fetch kept, every change recorded, the vintage of any figure recoverable by date. Two things should be said about the post that it does not say about itself. It names no failure modes — none of section 7's dropped jobs, sixty-day disablings or notices to one person — and its silence on them is a silence, not a reassurance. And the free scheduler it relies on is the one documented above.

The second half is *baked data*, from his post of 28 July 2021: "Baked Data: bundling a read-only copy of your data alongside the code for your application, as part of the same deployment". Against the ordinary arrangement — "Most dynamic websites keep their code and data separate: the code runs on an application server, the data lives independently in some kind of external data store" — the baked site carries its data with it, built once and shipped whole. His stated benefits are the no-admin case in a practitioner's words. "Inexpensive to host. Anywhere that can run application code can host a Baked Data application—there's no need to pay extra for a managed database system." "Easy to scale. Need to handle more traffic? Run more copies of your application and its bundled data." "Difficult to break. Hosting server-side applications on a VPS is always disquieting because there's so much that might go wrong—the server could be compromised, or a rogue log file could cause it to run out of disk space." With baked data, "the worst that can happen is that you need to re-deploy the application—there's no risk at all of data loss". His own site "is automatically deployed once a day by a scheduled action", built "inside GitHub Actions"; a larger site he describes keeps its content in "a ~22MB SQLite database file".

And he draws the boundary himself: "Since every change to the data results in a fresh deployment this pattern doesn't work for sites that change often—but in my experience many content-oriented sites update their content at most a few times a day." That sentence is the live-versus-nightly line in the originator's words. A dashboard whose sources publish monthly, weekly or daily — which is every public data source in section 6's table except the flood feed, which is real-time — sits comfortably inside it. A dashboard that wants to show what is happening *now* does not, and must accept the chain arithmetic of section 7 for whichever panels are live.

The map has its own baked form. PMTiles "is a single-file archive format for pyramids of tiled data." "A PMTiles archive can be hosted on a storage platform like S3, and enables low-cost, zero-maintenance map applications." It works because "PMTiles readers use HTTP Range Requests to fetch only the relevant tile or metadata inside a PMTiles archive on-demand" — the browser asks for the bytes it needs from one large file, and no tile server exists to be rate-limited, re-priced or switched off. Its limit is the same as baked data's: "PMTiles is a read-only format." "It is not possible to update an archive in-place without re-writing the entire file, similar to CSV, JSON and Parquet." Section 10 returns to what this means for a map's supply and vintage. The same move is available for the data itself, where a query engine compiled to run in the browser — "a full DuckDB engine run entirely inside any browser on any device, with no server to send queries to and no data leaving the user's machine" — "reads only the bytes a query needs rather than downloading an entire file", within limits a phone will feel: "By default, the WebAssembly client only uses a single thread" and "WebAssembly limits the amount of available memory to 4 GB and browsers may impose even stricter limits" — and at a cost in script that section 9's budget will not allow on most pages.

### What the free hosts allow

A baked page needs somewhere to sit, and the free tiers of the static hosts are where small studios put such things. Their limits, as displayed on 6 October 2026 and certain to change, are the edges of the architecture. Cloudflare's Pages: "The maximum file size for a single Cloudflare Pages site asset is 25 MiB", "up to 20,000 files on the Free plan", "500" builds a month, and — the sentence that makes the whole approach free at the point of reading — "On both free and paid plans, requests to static assets are free and unlimited." GitHub's Pages: "Published GitHub Pages sites may be no larger than 1 GB", "a soft bandwidth limit of 100 GB per month", "a soft limit of 10 builds per hour" — and a condition of use that a studio must read: "GitHub Pages is not intended for or allowed to be used as a free web-hosting service to run your online business, e-commerce site, or any other website that is primarily directed at either facilitating commercial transactions or providing commercial software as a service (SaaS)." Its scheduler is "free for self-hosted runners and for public repositories that use standard GitHub-hosted runners", with "2,000" minutes a month for private ones. Netlify's free tier is now metered in "credits" — "300 credit limit / month" — and "When a site reaches its monthly usage limits, it enters a paused state until the start of the next billing cycle." Paused: a popular month switches the page off until the next one.

Two things follow. A single baked data file or map archive larger than 25 MiB cannot be a page asset on the first host and needs object storage, which has its own price list; and the 1 GB and 100 GB figures on the second are generous for figures and are what a wide tile extract or a full-resolution boundary file would test, as section 10 shows. And one thing could not be established: whether any availability commitment attaches to these free tiers at all. The relevant service agreement page returned an error when fetched, and so the chapter does not say either way. A studio relying on a free host should assume it has signed nothing.

### Live against nightly, in money

The cost difference between a page that fetches when read and a page that is baked is not subtle, and one worked case makes it visible. Mapbox, one of the commercial map services, charges by *map load*: "A map load is counted every time Mapbox GL JS initializes on a webpage or in a web app", and "A map load includes unlimited Vector Tiles API and Raster Tiles API requests." The free allowance is "Up to 50,000" loads a month; the next band, to 100,000, is "$5.00" per thousand, then "$4.00", then lower. Its geocoder is free to "Up to 100,000" requests a month and then "$0.75" per thousand.

The arithmetic is the chapter's own. A dashboard whose map initialises 60,000 times in a month is 10,000 loads over the allowance, at $5 per thousand: $50 that month, every month, rising with success. The same map served from a PMTiles archive on a static host costs nothing in fees at any number of views, within the size and bandwidth limits above. Because billing is per initialisation and not per visitor, a page that rebuilds its map on every view pays on every view; a page that is popular because it works pays most. None of this is an argument against the commercial service, which buys a great deal — current tiles, a geocoder, a styled basemap, someone else's operations. It is the arithmetic that a studio should do with its own expected numbers before choosing, and the service record, in section 12, has a line for it.

### The cost that does not go away

Everything above reduces money and refresh labour. None of it reduces the other cost, and the practitioner literature agrees that the other cost is the larger one.

The measured evidence is from government and it is about spending, not software lifecycle: the United States' audit office found that of "over $90 billion" planned federal IT spending in one year, "About 80 percent of this amount is used to operate and maintain existing IT investments, including aging (also called legacy) systems", and "As they age, legacy systems can be more costly to maintain, more exposed to cybersecurity risks, and less effective in meeting their intended purpose." The often-repeated claim that sixty to eighty per cent of a system's lifetime cost is maintenance could not be traced to an open primary source, and the chapter does not assert it; what it asserts, on the audit's evidence and on practitioner authority, is that operating a thing costs more than making it. Nygard: "Systems spend much more of their life in operation than in development—at least, the ones that don't get canceled or scrapped do." Google's engineering handbook: "Within Google, we sometimes say, 'Software engineering is programming integrated over time.'" "Over a span of a decade or more, most program dependencies, whether implicit or explicit, will likely change." And the law named for Hyrum Wright, which section 6's platforms illustrate from the other side: "With a sufficient number of users of an API, it does not matter what you promise in the contract: all observable behaviors of your system will be depended on by somebody." A dashboard is a user of interfaces and, once others cite it, an interface itself.

The chapter has its own small evidence for the rate at which this happens, and it is first-hand. In one research run of a single day, the brief's "stable" sources had drifted under it: a dashboard product renamed; a vendor's defining page moved to a new domain and one of its "five pillars" renamed, with the page modified eleven days before it was read; a free host's tier re-denominated in credits; a cloud provider's reference library moved; a pattern's definition rewritten; a file format's tagline reworded; a workbook chapter's address dead; a scheduler's documented behaviour changed. By the research agents' own count, roughly a quarter of the URLs and phrasings in their brief were no longer as expected — some had moved, and some had never been quite as remembered. That is what "maintenance" is: not fixing what broke, but noticing what moved.

The person who notices is the resource, and the evidence on how thinly that resource is spread is as follows. Nadia Eghbal's 2016 report for the Ford Foundation: "Free, publicly available source code is the infrastructure on which all of digital society relies." "However, few realize that public code is built and maintained almost exclusively by unpaid volunteers, and the enormous amount of work required is in desperate need of institutional support." The *truck factor* — "the minimal number of developers that have to be hit by a truck (or quit) before a project is incapacitated" — was estimated for 133 popular open-source projects by Avelino and colleagues in 2016 †, who "find that the majority of our target systems (65%) have TF <= 2"; their own validation was weaker than the headline, with about half of surveyed developers agreeing with the estimates, and the figure is given as the best open measurement rather than a settled one. A studio's dashboard, built by one or two people, has a truck factor of one or two by construction, and section 7 showed that its alarm may have a truck factor of one as well. The reliability engineers' definition of the work that remains is *toil* — "the kind of work tied to running a production service that tends to be manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, devoid of enduring value, and that scales linearly as a service grows" — and their rule is that "At least 50% of each SRE's time should be spent on engineering project work that will either reduce future toil or add service features." For a dashboard built as this section describes, the chapter's expectation — an expectation, not a finding, and one the prototype should test — is that the toil is near zero and the attention near one ticket a month. That is "no admin", as far as it goes, and it does not go further.

### What section 8 settles

A dashboard can be built so that nobody tends its refresh: its data fetched by a scheduled job that commits what it finds, baked into a static page with its date on the face, served from a host that charges nothing per view, with each series in its own compartment, the last good copy kept when a fetch fails, and an independent heartbeat that raises a ticket to a role when the data's date stops advancing. Every piece of that is documented, open and in use. What cannot be built is the absence of the person who reads the ticket, re-points the job when an interface moves, and decides, when a source closes, whether the series ends or finds a new supply. The architectures reduce that person's work to reading and deciding. They do not reduce it to nothing, and a plan that says they do has not read section 7.

## 9 · The phone that will actually open it

A dashboard that is built and baked as section 8 describes still has to arrive, and it arrives on whatever device the reader has in their hand. This section is about that device, and about the evidence that the device in the builder's hand is the wrong one to design for. It leans on four kinds of source — the search company's own thresholds, the yearly crawl of the web, one practitioner's budget model, and the UK regulator's surveys — and it says what each is and is not.

### The thresholds, and where they came from

The most widely used yardsticks for whether a page is fast enough are Google's Core Web Vitals, three numbers that, in the company's words, "will be surfaced across all Google tools" and that site owners therefore treat with great seriousness. "To provide a good user experience, LCP should occur within 2.5 seconds of when the page first starts loading" — the largest thing on the screen has appeared within two and a half seconds. "pages should have a INP of 200 milliseconds or less" — a tap is answered within a fifth of a second. "pages should maintain a CLS of 0.1. or less" — the page does not jump about as it loads (the stray full stop is on the page). And the measurement rule: "a good threshold to measure is the 75th percentile of page loads, segmented across mobile and desktop devices." Not the average, and not the best: the experience at the boundary of the slowest quarter of visits. "By using the 75th percentile, we know that most visits to the site (3 of 4) experienced the target level of performance or better."

A builder should know where the two and a half seconds came from, because it is not a human-factors constant. The engineers who set it have written up their reasoning, and part of it is achievability: "To confirm that a threshold is achievable, we require that at least 10% of origins meet the 'good' threshold"; "We find that the 1.5 and 2 second thresholds are not consistently achievable, while 2.5 seconds is consistently achievable." Their own table gives 3.5 per cent of phone origins meeting one second and 42 per cent meeting two and a half. The threshold, in other words, is partly what a tenth of the web could already do, chosen with an eye to the attention research but set where sites could reach it. That does not make it wrong as a budget. It makes it a floor and not a target, and a page that just clears it is a page in the better half of a slow field.

### How heavy the web is

The HTTP Archive's yearly *Web Almanac* crawls millions of pages with an open method, and its medians are the baseline a dashboard competes against. "In October 2024, the median page weight for a mobile page was 2,311 KB." Of that, JavaScript on mobile pages is "558 KB"; images, "900 KB"; "The median page makes 71 requests on desktop and 66 on mobile." The mobile median has grown "357%, or 1.8 MB" over the ten years to 2024. The 2025 edition, published in January 2026 and measuring home pages, has it still rising: "The median mobile home page is 2.6 MB, growing 8.4% from 2.4 MB in 2024."

And most of that web fails its own yardstick on phones. The 2024 performance chapter: with the current responsiveness metric, the share of websites with good Core Web Vitals on mobile "drops to 43%". The 2025 chapter, looking back, puts 2024 at 44 per cent and 2025 at 48 — "Mobile Core Web Vitals have shown consistent year-over-year improvement, increasing from 36% in 2023 to 44% in 2024, and reaching 48% in 2025" — and the two editions disagree by a point about 2024, so each should be cited with its own year. Desktop is better and not good: 54 per cent in 2024, "only to 56%" in 2025. The sentence from the 2024 chapter's introduction that the rest of this section rests on: "Web performance is tied to what devices and networks people can afford."

### Whose phone

Alex Russell's yearly essay on what he calls the performance inequality gap is the one source that turns device and network distributions into a budget in bytes, with its working shown, and it is a practitioner's model rather than a measurement: a reasoned estimate from global market data by one author, and the budgets are its outputs. Its 2024 edition fixes the device to design for as "a device sold new, unlocked for the mid-2020 to mid-2021 global ASP of ~$350-375", of which "Representative examples from that time period include the Samsung Galaxy A51 and the Pixel 4a" — a mid-range phone three or four years old when he wrote, in 2024 — on a network of "7.2Mbps down, 1.4Mbps up, and 94ms RTT". The budget that follows for a page that must become usable within five seconds on that phone: "~1.3MiB of compressed content to get interactive in five seconds. A page fitting this budget can afford: 650KiB of HTML, CSS, images, and fonts; 650KiB of JavaScript". A page built from markup rather than script gets more room — "Conservatively, to load in five seconds over two connections, we should try to keep content under 2.5MiB, including: 2.4MiB of HTML, CSS, images, and fonts, and 100KiB of JavaScript" — because markup is cheap for a phone to process and script is not. At three seconds, his figures roughly halve.

Then the sentence that names the blind spot, and the evidence for belief seven: "Devices within the envelope of our attention are 15-25% as fast as those carried by programmers and their bosses — even in wealthy markets." The phone the builder tests on is four to nearly seven times the phone at his seventy-fifth percentile — a global price distribution, not a UK survey, and the nearest thing the research found. A page that feels instant in the studio can take several times as long in the reader's hand. And the trend is the wrong way: "Inequality is growing faster than the bottom-end can improve."

Set his budget against the Almanac's median and the problem is visible without further argument. The median mobile page is 2.3 to 2.6 MB with over half a megabyte of script; the budget for the seventy-fifth-percentile phone is 1.3 MB with 650 KB of script, or 2.5 MB with a hundred. The median page is over budget on that phone. A dashboard with a map, section 10 will show, starts a long way over before it has drawn anything.

### The figure everyone quotes, with its label on

"53% of visits are likely to be abandoned if pages take longer than 3 seconds to load." The sentence is from a Google advertising blog post of 8 September 2016, and it is cited here only so that it can be cited correctly, because the form in which it circulates — that 53 per cent of mobile visits *are* abandoned — is not on Google's page; the page says "likely to be". Its footnote gives the basis: "Google Data, Aggregated, anonymized Google Analytics data from a sample of mWeb sites opted into sharing benchmark data, n=3.7K, Global, March 2016". Vendor research, ten years old, an opt-in sample, method not published, from before the current metrics existed, and the Think with Google page that carried it now redirects elsewhere. The same post's "the average load time for mobile sites is 19 seconds over 3G connections" has the same standing. Use the figure with its age and origin attached, or do not use it. The chapter's recommendation is the second.

### What the UK's own standard asks

The GOV.UK Service Manual's position on all of this is older than the metrics and more demanding, and it is a standard that public services in the UK are built to. "Progressive enhancement is a way of building websites and applications based on the idea that you should make your page work with HTML first." "Only after this can you add anything else like Cascading Style Sheets (CSS) and JavaScript". "Government services should be functional using only HTML." "A user should be able to navigate through your service using only the HTML." And a rule that many dashboard frameworks break by default: "Do not build your service as a single-page application (SPA). This is where the loading of pages within your service is handled by JavaScript, rather than the browser."

The reason is not users who switch scripts off. The Manual lists the ways "CSS and JavaScript can fail to load or run", and almost all are involuntary: temporary network errors; browser extensions; "third-party supplier downtime, such as when using a content delivery network"; DNS failures; browser updates; other scripts on the same page; corporate firewalls; "mobile network providers changing content to speed up load times and reduce bandwidth usage"; antivirus software; insecure connections into which providers insert their own code (the list read once, except the two quoted items). The one public measurement behind the principle is GDS's own, from 2013, and it remains the only figure of its kind the research found: on the GOV.UK homepage, from "over half a million visits", "1.1% of people aren't getting JavaScript enhancements (1 in 93)" — of which, in the post's wording, "0.2% of all people's browsers who have disabled, or don't support, JavaScript and 0.9% of all people's browsers have JavaScript enabled but don't receive it". Four-fifths of the failures were not a switch turned off by the user, though some will have been browsers that could not run the script. One visit in ninety-three, on one well-built site in 2013 — the only such figure found —  arrived with the script missing; a dashboard whose figures exist only once the script has run shows that visitor nothing, and the figures were in a file the whole time.

The Manual's companion page on browsers and devices has softened since the research brief was written — testing in the listed browsers is now something a service "should" do, where the brief remembered a "must" — and the page carries no guidance on low-specification devices or slow connections — while keeping the principle: "Your service must be universally accessible. This means building it to work on every browser or device that your users access it on." And a finding from the research itself belongs here as evidence: two of the public data front doors this chapter needed — the Ordnance Survey's plans page and the national statistics office's geography portal — returned nothing to a reader without scripts: the first only "You need to enable JavaScript to run this app.", the second only its page title. To a reader without scripts, two public bodies' data services fail the Service Manual's own test, which is a measure of how far the default has drifted from the standard.

### Who is on the phone, and where

The regulator's figures close the section. On coverage, Ofcom's *Connected Nations* 2025: "4G coverage from at least one MNO now reaches 96% of the UK landmass", and "The MNOs met their 2024 obligations to deliver good geographic coverage to 88% of the UK landmass and have continued to maintain coverage at around this level over the past year." The two figures measure different things: 96 per cent is the landmass covered by at least one operator, 88 per cent what each operator was obliged to cover on its own. Urban areas have coverage "from at least one MNO exceeding 99%". These are the operators' predicted outdoor signal, not anyone's experienced speed, and "Indoor mobile coverage can vary depending on factors such as wall thickness, building materials used in construction and where in a building people are using their phone". The complement of the 96 per cent figure, about four per cent of landmass with no 4G from any operator, is the chapter's inference and not the regulator's sentence.

On who is holding the phone, Ofcom's *Adults' Media Use and Attitudes* 2025: "Around one in five (18%) of adults who go online do so only on a smartphone, not on any other device." And the gradient: "Among adults in a DE household, 30% reported only going online on a smartphone, compared to only 9% of those in AB households." Of those smartphone-only users, "49% of these users said they struggled to complete forms on a smartphone compared to doing so on a laptop or PC, while 35% agreed that it was difficult to compare products or services" — though "around three quarters of adults who use a smartphone only to go online reported never feeling disadvantaged by it (74%)". And "6% of adults continuing to not have access to the internet at home in 2024".

The arithmetic a builder should carry from this section is short. One online adult in five has no screen but the phone; in social grade DE households, three in ten. Their phone is, on Russell's model, a seventh to a quarter as fast as the builder's. The budget for them is his: about 1.3 MiB in all if half of it is script, or 2.5 MiB if almost none is, for a five-second load — and the page should show its numbers before any script has run, because about one visit in a hundred, on the one public measurement, never runs it. Chapter 2 said who the readers were; this section says what they are holding. A dashboard that fails on their device has, in the failure register's words, failed because "people dont use it", and they did not use it because it did not open.

## 10 · The map

Section 9 ended at a budget. A map spends it faster than anything else a dashboard can contain, and it brings with it three dependencies that no chart has: a tile supplier with a policy, a boundary file with a vintage, and a rendering library heavier than the rest of the page. This section reads each, and ends with what the map must have beside it. It says nothing about what the map should look like, which is a design decision and not this book's.

### Two kinds of tile

A web map is drawn from tiles — square pieces of the world at each zoom level, fetched as the reader pans — and there are two kinds. The older kind is a picture: a raster tile is an image rendered on a server and sent finished, and the phone only has to display it. The newer kind is data: "A Vector tile is the vector data equivalent of raster image tiles for web mapping" — "a space-efficient encoding format for tiled geographic vector data" in which the roads, buildings and names arrive as geometry and the phone draws them. The specification is Mapbox's, open — "the use of this spec in products and code is entirely free: there are no royalties, restrictions, or requirements" — and the vendor's own account of the trade is the one most builders meet: "Tiles are styled at runtime, allowing for many map styles on global data." "Vector tiles are small, enabling global high-resolution maps, faster map loads, and efficient caching." And, read as probable rather than confirmed because the verifying pass agreed too readily: "Rendering vector tiles in the browser or device requires more processing power compared to displaying pre-rendered raster images."

That last sentence is the trade, and it should be read beside the two vendors' claims that vector maps load faster, which are claims about bytes on the network and not about work on the device; section 9 says which way the device half cuts for the phone most readers hold. Vector tiles move the styling and the drawing from the server to the device; raster tiles move them to the server. A vector map can be restyled to the studio's palette, rotated, labelled in the house face — everything a styled map offers — at the price of a rendering library and a working graphics processor on the reader's side. The Ordnance Survey offers both: a vector service that is "a customisable basemap" that "Loads maps quicker than traditional raster-mapping services", and a raster service of "pre-rendered raster tiles". The choice between them is a performance decision disguised as a styling one.

### Four ways to get tiles, and what each promises

Whoever draws the tiles, someone has to serve them, and the research found four models of supply, each with its own promise and its own way of ending.

*Donated and unguaranteed.* The OpenStreetMap Foundation's tile servers are the free default in thousands of tutorials, and the Foundation's policy — rewritten since the research brief was framed, in softer and more operational language — separates the data from the servers in its first lines: "OpenStreetMap (OSM) data is free for everyone to use. Our tile servers are not: they are funded by donations and sponsorship, and capacity is limited." Then: "Availability is best-effort: there is no SLA or guarantee." "We may block access, without notice, if your usage degrades the service." The client must "Honour server caching headers (Cache-Control, Expires, Etag). If your cache cannot read them, cache each tile for at least 7 days", must send "a clear, unique User-Agent string that names your app", and must not bulk download — "any pre-emptive fetching of tiles other than those a user is actively viewing" — or use the tiles offline: "Offline use is not permitted on tile.openstreetmap.org." This is belief four exactly. The map data is free; the map service is a gift with conditions, and the Foundation says plainly that a project which cannot meet them should "use an alternative OSM-derived service … or run your own".

*The national mapping agency.* The Ordnance Survey's Data Hub serves tiles behind a key, with the rate limits section 6 tabled — 600 transactions a minute live, 50 in development, 429 beyond — and with a branding condition: "Our logo should be positioned in the embedded map window across all your digital applications", and the copyright statement, terms and error-reporting link likewise. Its free allowances could not be read, because the plans page is script-only, and the chapter does not print them from memory. What it does offer, and what matters most for section 8's architecture, is a basemap that can be downloaded whole: OS Open Zoomstack is "an open vector basemap showing coverage of Great Britain from a national level, right down to street detail", "updated every six months in June and December", available "in just one single file" and "Free to use for everyone". Neither page reached names the Open Government Licence, so the licence basis is recorded as unverified. A basemap in one file on a six-month cadence is a basemap that can be baked.

*Metered and commercial.* Mapbox and MapTiler sell tiles by the unit, and section 8 did the arithmetic for the first: free to 50,000 map loads a month, then $5 per thousand, a load being each time the library "initializes on a webpage", with, in the company's glossary, "The maximum session length for a map load is 12 hours." MapTiler's free plan allows "100k/month" requests and "5k/month" sessions, is "Suitable for testing, PoC, prototyping, personal, or non-commercial use", puts the "MapTiler logo on the map", and — the same clause as the free static host — "On a FREE plan service will pause until the next month without an upgrade to paid plans." Its paid tier is "$30/month" for "500k/month" requests. A metered supplier promises current tiles and someone else's operations in exchange for a bill that rises with the dashboard's success and a map that stops when the free month runs out.

*A file you host yourself.* Protomaps' PMTiles, from section 8: "a single-file archive format for pyramids of tiled data", hosted "on a storage platform like S3", read by range requests so that no tile server exists. The project's own basemap is "available as a single PMTiles archive, distributed as an Open Database License Produced Work (OpenStreetMap attribution required)"; "A full planet file is roughly 120 gigabytes, including zoom levels from 0 to 15", and "Each additional zoom level roughly doubles the size of the file" — so a studio extracts the city it needs, which is a small fraction of that, and the file sits on a static host inside the limits section 8 listed, or on object storage if it is over 25 MiB. The promise here is the inverse of the others: no supplier can rate-limit, re-price or close it, because there is no supplier at read time; in exchange the studio owns the vintage — "PMTiles is a read-only format" — and must rebuild the file to move it. The project's own "read-only" is confirmed at source; even the download is only best-effort: "Please note that URLs may change and hotlinking to these downloads are discouraged."

Four supplies; four endings. The donated servers may block without notice; the agency's key has a rate and a logo; the metered service has a bill and a pause; the file has a date. The service record, in section 12, has a line for which one the map uses and what it has agreed to.

### The boundary file: the size is the decision

A place dashboard draws boundaries — wards, districts, output areas — and the boundary file is the one piece of a map whose weight is entirely in the builder's hands, because the national statistics office publishes each boundary at several resolutions and the choice of suffix is the performance decision.

The office's own page lists four: "(BFC) Full resolution - clipped to the coastline (Mean High Water mark)"; "(BFE) Full resolution - extent of the realm"; "(BGC) Generalised (20m) - clipped to the coastline"; "(BUC) Ultra Generalised (500m) - clipped to the coastline" — "designed to be used in different contexts (such as analysis or visualisation)". The portal publishes a fifth that the explainer omits: "(BSC) Super Generalised (200m) - clipped to the coastline (Mean High Water mark)." The portal's size fields for the December 2024 local authority districts — indicative only, since what the field measures is undocumented — give the full-resolution file at about 128 MB and the 200-metre file at about 1.7 MB in the same record type: roughly seventy-five to one, by the chapter's division. Against section 9's budget of 1.3 to 2.5 MiB for everything, the full-resolution national file is a hundred times the whole page, and even the 200-metre national file is more than the page can afford. The answer is not the national file at all: a map of one district needs only its own boundaries, cut from the generalised product, and the figure drawn on top of them is the same whichever resolution carries it.

Two further facts about boundary files belong in the service record. They are dated products — the record is "as at December 2024" — and the geography underneath them moves: "Electoral ward and electoral division boundary changes are usually enacted on the first Thursday in May each year, to coincide with the local government elections in the UK", and the census geographies were redrawn in 2021 "by merging or splitting 2011 LSOAs to ensure that population and household thresholds were met". A dashboard pins a boundary vintage the way it pins a data vintage, and says which, because a figure for a ward that no longer has that shape is a figure for a place that no longer exists. And they carry a licence with two owners: "Source: Office for National Statistics licensed under the Open Government Licence v.3.0" and "Contains OS data © Crown copyright and database right [year]" — the boundaries "Contains both Ordnance Survey and ONS Intellectual Property Rights."

When the published generalisation is not enough, the builder simplifies, and should know what the tools do to small places. Mapshaper, the usual instrument, "supports Douglas-Peucker simplification and two kinds of Visvalingam simplification": the first "Guarantees that simplified lines stay within a fixed distance of the original" but "tends to introduce visible spikes at high simplification"; the second "Iteratively removes the point that forms the triangle of smallest area with its two neighbors", and its weighted form is the default "because it produces generalizations that look good enough for most web maps, even if they fall short of true cartographic quality." The warning is one sentence: "At high simplification, small polygons can disappear entirely". The smallest areas are the first to vanish, and in a city the smallest areas are usually the densest. Shared borders survive simplification together "but only if their vertices are perfectly aligned to begin with", which is why the topology-preserving format exists: TopoJSON "encodes topology", stitching geometries "from shared line segments called arcs", and is "substantially more compact than GeoJSON, frequently offering a reduction of 80% or more even without simplification."

### The library

Before a single tile arrives, the map has to be drawn by code the phone downloads, and here the raster-versus-vector trade becomes a number. Leaflet, the standard raster-map library, describes itself as "Weighing just about 42 KB of JS" gzipped. MapLibre GL JS, the open vector renderer — "an open-source fork of mapbox-gl-js, before their switch to a non-OSS license in December 2020", "licensed under the 3-Clause BSD license", drawing by "GPU-accelerated vector tile rendering" — is reported by a third-party measurement at about 287 KB gzipped for a recent version (the version number could not be matched to the project's own releases). The vector renderer alone is about seven times the raster library, and about 43 per cent of Russell's entire JavaScript budget for his seventy-fifth-percentile phone, by the chapter's arithmetic, before any tile, boundary or figure has been fetched. A vector map on a phone is a decision to spend over two-fifths of the script budget on the ability to restyle.

### What nobody has measured

The research looked for a measurement of what a map view costs a phone in bytes — a typical city basemap at a typical zoom, tiles and all — and found none. The suppliers bill by load, session or request, not by byte; the OpenStreetMap wiki's aggregate — "during August 2026 the tile.openstreetmap.org CDN served approximately 1.7 PB per month (outbound) and 135 billion requests per month" — divides to about 12.6 KB per request by the chapter's arithmetic, an average over every response and not a per-view figure; the tile encoder's 500 KB ceiling per tile is a limit and not a measurement. This is a gap in the published record `[Further research]`, and it is a gap the prototype can fill with its own numbers in an afternoon `[Prototype flag]`: open the page on the baseline device, on the baseline network, and count. Until then, the plain statement is that nobody official knows what a map costs the median reader, and the library figure above says the answer starts high.

### The twin

A map is also the one element of a dashboard that the accessibility standards use as their worked example, twice. The success criterion on dragging: "All functionality that uses a dragging movement for operation can be achieved by a single pointer without dragging, unless dragging is essential or the functionality is determined by the user agent and not modified by the author"; the example, "A map allows users to drag the view of the map around, and the map has up/down/left/right buttons to move the view as well." The criterion on gestures: "All functionality that uses multipoint or path-based gestures for operation can be operated with a single pointer without a path-based gesture, unless a multipoint or path-based gesture is essential"; the example, "A website includes a map view that supports the pinch/spread gesture to zoom into the map content. As a single-pointer alternative, the map also includes plus/minus buttons to zoom in and out." Pan and zoom buttons are, in effect, the cited way to conform. The web accessibility tutorials class maps among the complex images — "maps showing locations or other information such as weather systems" — for which "a two-part text alternative is required", the second part being "a textual representation of the essential information conveyed by the image". And the cross-government guidance on colour names the specific difficulty: "Maps provide a particular challenge for accessibility as colours are not presented in an order. This can make it difficult to match colours against a legend when there are insufficient contrast ratios between sequential colours." Its instruction for maps: "make sure the data is available in another format such as a downloadable data table". Its palette tops out at five sequential blues — "Where 5 sequential colours are required, the full set of blue colours shown in the palette table can be used" — because "The more categories you use in a sequential colour palette, the less accessible the colours become".

The map, then, needs a twin: a table of the same figures, by the same places, reachable without the map and before the script. The twin is not an accessibility afterthought. It is the version of the page that the one visit in ninety-three receives, that the slowest quarter of phones receives first, that a screen reader receives, and that survives the day the tile supplier blocks without notice. A dashboard whose map has no twin has made its most expensive element its only one.

## 11 · The studio's own positions, tested

The chapter has so far argued from its sources. This section turns the same sources on the studio's own standing positions — the ones in version 1 of this book, in the Decision Book, in the design system and in the house framework — and records, for each, whether the evidence strengthens it, qualifies it, counterbalances it, or leaves it a choice. Chapter 0 calls this the symmetry test, and it is done here before the truths are written so that the truths are not the positions restated.

**"No admin."** The studio's phrase. Section 7 and 8 support the architecture it points to and narrow what it can mean: nobody tends the refresh; someone reads the ticket, re-points the job when an interface moves and decides what happens when a source closes. The UK's own dashboard guidance says in terms that running one is "not an appropriate project for a single person" (section 3). A small studio that intends to run one should write down whose job the ticket is and who is second. *Qualified.*

**"Staleness degrades visibly."** Version 1's Chapter 8 held that a stale series should look stale on the face. The protocol (`stale-if-error`), the cloud provider's anti-pattern list ("Invalidating or emptying local state as a result of a failed refresh") and the workbook ("Stale data is almost always better than incorrect data") agree that the last good copy is kept and shown with its age. Where the chapter adds to the position: the age shown must be the data's, not the page's (section 4); and "visibly degrades" must stop short of "silently disappears". The honest amputation of a series that has closed — which version 1 also allowed — must meet the retirement procedure of section 3: notice, an archive of what was shown, and the series' last state kept reachable. *Strengthened, with two conditions.*

**"Freshness is first-screen furniture."** Chapter 1's audit put the date on the face. Section 4 says the single date is not enough: a reader needs the period the data describes, the date it was published, the date it was last revised and the declared frequency, because a date without a frequency does not say whether the figure is late. The first of these is official — "Always make it clear which dates or period your dashboard is relevant to" — and the other three are the studio's extension of it. No design system reached (GOV.UK, Carbon, PatternFly) says a dashboard should show its last refresh; the practice is found in one product's documentation (the stamp "in the bottom left corner of the report") and a 1994 heuristic ("The design should always keep users informed about what is going on"). The four-part statement is the studio's, supported by the catalogue standards and the official rule on the period, and thin on display precedent; the chapter holds it. *Strengthened and extended; display precedent thin.*

**"Living series", and the house framework's "maintained … or extractive".** The framework quoted in Chapter 1 — confidential; cited, not reproduced — holds that a map not "actively maintained and ethically governed… risks becoming outdated or extractive as soon as it is published". Sections 5 and 6 add the supply side: the open licence "does not guarantee the continued supply of the Information", and the streaming and social platforms' interfaces have each been narrowed within five years. A living series is a promise the studio makes on top of supplies that promise nothing. The position stands; the chapter's addition is that "maintained" has a service record behind it (section 12), with a named role, a retirement plan and a successor. *Strengthened; made concrete.*

**The observatory.** Version 1's Chapters 1 and 8 made a long-running civic data observatory the founding failure: a maintained container over unmaintained contents. Section 7's standards cut both ways. If the observatory is judged by them — does it show the data's date and frequency, does it keep the last good copy, does its alarm go to a role — then so is the studio's own plan, and the plan must meet the sixty-day disabling, the notification to one person and the dropped job on the free scheduler before it is entitled to the comparison. *Counterbalanced: the standard applies to us first.*

**The ambition to read ticketing and streaming interfaces.** The Decision Book and the prototype's own questions assume these can be called. Section 6 reports what the terms permit, and the chapter stops there: it is a `[Prototype flag]` and a Chapter 10 question whether to proceed, and the terms should be open on the table when it is decided. The chapter's one methodological contribution is the distinction between a dependency on *supply* (the public interfaces: permitted, unguaranteed) and a dependency on *terms* (the platforms: guaranteed nothing, and addressed clause by clause). *A choice, now with its evidence attached.*

**"A map is the face."** The design system makes the map the recognisable surface of the Vibe Map. Section 10 does not dispute it; it prices it. A vector map costs over two-fifths of the script budget of the phone that three in ten online adults in DE households rely on, before tiles; its supply has four endings; its boundaries have a vintage and a size; and the standards require a non-map twin. The face can be a map; the page cannot be only one. *Qualified.*

**"Built in the open, on free tools."** The studio's repository and hosting assume the free tiers. Section 8 lists their edges on the day read: 25 MiB per asset; 1 GB per site; a paused state at the credit cap; schedules dropped under load and disabled after sixty quiet days; and no availability commitment that could be established. The free tier is a design constraint with a date on it, not a neutral default. *Qualified.*

## 12 · What holds true regardless

Seven truths, in the chapter's own words, each with its grounds; then the record that sits beneath them. They are stated for any dashboard that intends to be read next year; the nightlife case in section 14 is one application.

**1 · Freshness is a declared lifetime, not a stamp.** A dashboard states, for each series, the period the data describes, the date it was published, the date it was last revised and the frequency at which it is expected to move; "last updated" alone names none of these. An absent date, by the catalogue standard's own admission, means one of three things and a reader cannot tell which. The page's own freshness lifetime — the age at which what it shows is to be treated as stale — is written down and compared against the data's date on the page itself, in words a reader can read. *(Sections 3, 4, 7; DCAT 3; RFC 9111 §4.2; the Analysis Function's dashboards guidance; the SRE workbook's freshness objectives.)*

**2 · A dashboard is a service, and "no admin" moves the job, it does not remove it.** The UK's own guidance calls a dashboard a digital service that is continually managed and "not an appropriate project for a single person". Automation replaces the refresh; it does not replace the person who reads the ticket, re-points a job when an interface moves and decides what happens when a source closes. That person is a named role with a successor, and the signals they receive are sorted into alerts, tickets and logs so that attention is spent once. *(Sections 3, 7, 8; the Analysis Function; the SRE book on monitoring and toil; Eghbal; Avelino et al.)*

**3 · A dependency's terms, limits and notice are part of the design, and an open licence gives reuse, not supply.** Every interface a dashboard calls is a service run by someone else, on terms that may be revised without notice, with a stated limit that is the real limit and a history of closing that is the best guide to its future. The Open Government Licence permits everything and promises nothing about continuing to exist; the platform terms forbid, clause by clause, the things a dashboard does. The dashboard records, for each dependency, which kind it is — supply or terms — and what notice it has been promised: twelve months by contract with exceptions, six as an aspiration, or none. *(Sections 5, 6; GDS; RFC 6585; the OGL; the platform terms and histories.)*

**4 · Availability is bounded by the weakest thing the page needs at the moment of reading, so the work is done before the reader arrives.** A page that fetches live from ten dependencies has given itself days a year of showing nothing. Fetched the night before and baked, the page depends on its host alone, the night's job can fail without the reader seeing it, and the last good copy is kept and shown with its age — which is what the protocol already does when allowed and what the cloud provider's own anti-pattern list requires. A series that fails floods one compartment, not the page. *(Sections 7, 8; Treynor et al.; RFC 9111, RFC 5861; Fowler; AWS REL05-BP01; Willison.)*

**5 · Scheduled jobs fail quietly, so the effect is watched, not the job, and the ticket goes to a role.** The oldest scheduler, on a reading of its manuals, has no notion of failure to report; the free one documents that it delays, drops, disables after sixty quiet days and notifies one person. The watch is therefore independent of the scheduler, checks that the data's date advanced, and raises a ticket to an address the studio reads. Refresh jobs are built to be run twice safely, and to be skipped rather than doubled. *(Section 7; the cron manuals; GitHub's documentation; the SRE book on cron; heartbeat monitoring practice.)*

**6 · The phone sets the budget, and the page shows its figures before any script has run.** The phone to design for is a seventh to a quarter as fast as the builder's, on a network of a few megabits; one online adult in five, and three in ten in social grade DE households, has no other screen; about one visit in a hundred, on the one public measurement, arrives without the script, four-fifths of them involuntarily. The budget is Russell's — about 1.3 MiB with half of it script, or 2.5 MiB with almost none — measured at the seventy-fifth percentile, and the figures are in the HTML. *(Section 9; Russell; the Web Almanac; GDS; Herlihy 2013; Ofcom.)*

**7 · A map is a licence, a tile supply and a vintage, and it has a non-map twin.** A map brings a supplier with one of four endings, a boundary file whose resolution is chosen and whose date is pinned, and a rendering library that can take over two-fifths of the script budget before a tile arrives. The same figures, by the same places, are reachable as a table without the map and before the script; pan and zoom have buttons; the colour ramp is short. *(Section 10; the OSMF policies; OS; Mapbox and MapTiler; Protomaps; the ONS boundary products; WCAG 2.5.1 and 2.5.7; the Analysis Function on colour.)*

### Beneath the dashboard: the service record

The truths are kept by a record, as Chapters 4 to 7 each produced one — the provenance set, the recipe card, the claim record and the comparison record — and whether these five are one document is a question for the end of the book `[Revisit at end]`. The *service record* is written before the dashboard launches, by the person who will keep it, and it answers ten questions. Each line is short; the point is that each is answered, and that the last — what is not promised — is answered too, because section 7 showed that a year of silent reliability makes a promise whether or not one was written.

| Line | What it states |
|---|---|
| 1 · Freshness promise | For each series: period described, published, last revised, declared frequency; the page's own freshness lifetime, in days, and where it is shown |
| 2 · Dependencies | Each interface, file and host the page needs — at build time and at read time, marked which — with its stated limit, its terms (supply or terms), the notice it has promised, and the date its page was read |
| 3 · Refresh mechanism | What runs, where, on what schedule, off the hour; what it fetches and what it commits; that it can be run twice safely |
| 4 · Watch and alarm | What checks that the data's date advanced, independent of the job; where the ticket goes — a role, not a person — and who is second |
| 5 · Availability target and degraded mode | The objective in the data's terms ("the oldest figure is never more than N days behind, 11 months in 12"); what the page shows when a fetch fails, series by series; when the degraded mode was last exercised |
| 6 · Budget and baseline device | The byte budget; the device and network it was tested on; what the page shows before any script runs |
| 7 · Map supply, licence and vintage | Which of the four supplies; what has been agreed to (cache, attribution, logo, offline, non-commercial); the boundary product and its date; the renderer and its weight; where the twin is |
| 8 · Retirement plan and archive | How a series ends when its source closes: the notice given, what is archived, where the last state stays reachable; how the whole page ends |
| 9 · Owner and successor | Whose job the ticket is; who takes it when they leave; where this record lives |
| 10 · Not done | What the record declines to promise, and why |

**A filled example, invented for the method.** *1.* Series A: monthly, period = calendar month, published ~6 weeks after period end, revised for 3 months; page lifetime 50 days; shown beside the figure. *2.* Build time: statistics API (beta; 200/min; no notice stated; read 6 Oct 2026), boundary file BSC Dec 2024 (OGL + OS copyright). Read time: static host only (25 MiB/asset; no availability commitment established). *3.* Scheduled job, 03:17 UTC daily, commits fetched files to the repository; idempotent. *4.* Heartbeat monitor pinged after the data's date is confirmed to have advanced; ticket to `data@` (role); second: named. *5.* Newest figure published ≤ 50 days ago, 11 months in 12; on fetch failure each series keeps its last good copy and shows its age in words; degraded mode last exercised: date. *6.* 900 KB total, 60 KB script; Galaxy A51-class device on 7 Mbps; all figures and the table render without script. *7.* Self-hosted PMTiles extract, OSM attribution; district boundaries cut from BSC, December 2024; MapLibre 287 KB gz — *flagged against a 60 KB script line; raster fallback under test*; twin table reachable from the face. *8.* On source closure: notice on the face for 6 months, series archived with last state at a fixed address, then removed from the face. *9.* Owner: role; successor: role; record at `/service`. *10.* Not promised: any live figure; any uptime percentage for the host; any platform-derived series.

The example is a shape and not a plan; the prototype's record will have different lines in it, and some of them will say "not yet".

## 13 · Where the choice is yours

The truths bind; these do not. Each is a decision the evidence informs and does not make, and Chapter 0 asks that they be recorded as choices so that nobody mistakes a studio preference for a finding.

*Live or baked, panel by panel.* The chapter's architecture is nightly, and Willison's own boundary — "at most a few times a day" — covers every public data source in section 6's table but the real-time flood feed. A studio may still want one live element: a count that moves while the reader watches. That element accepts the chain arithmetic of section 7, with its own dependency line in the record, and the rest of the page does not depend on it. How much liveness is worth that cost is the studio's to weigh.

*Vector or raster.* Section 10 priced the vector renderer at over two-fifths of the script budget and the raster library at a seventh of that. The vector map can be restyled to the house; the raster map cannot. Whether the recognisable face is worth the budget — and whether a raster fallback for the slowest quarter of phones is an acceptable version of it — is a creative decision and this book does not make it. It does require that the twin exist either way.

*Which of the four tile supplies.* Donated, agency, metered or self-hosted; each has an ending and a cost, and the right one depends on the studio's traffic, money and tolerance for owning a vintage. The record states which was chosen and what was agreed to.

*Which objective, and how large a budget.* "Eleven months in twelve" is the chapter's example. A studio may promise more and spend more attention, or less and say so. The reliability engineers' advice is to set it no higher than readers can tell, and to write it down before the year in which it is silently exceeded trains readers to expect the excess.

*The notice the studio gives when it retires a series.* The UK's aspiration is six months for a live interface; the contractual best case is twelve with exceptions; most of the studio's own suppliers promise none. What the studio promises its own readers is its choice, and the chapter's only requirement is that it be written in line 8 before the first series needs it.

*Whether to call the platforms at all.* Section 6 reported the terms. The decision is the prototype's and Chapter 10's, and it is listed here so that it is not mistaken for something this chapter settled.

*Who reads the ticket.* A role, the chapter says; but which role, with what second, at what hours, is an organisational choice the studio makes about itself.

## 14 · A nightlife illustration — method only

What follows shows how the method would be applied to a dashboard about where people go at night; it draws no conclusions about any place, names no venue and proposes no design. It is the service record's ten questions asked of a hypothetical page.

Suppose a studio proposes a page about a district — some monthly series by small area, a map because the design system asks for one, and one panel fed from a platform. Before any of it is built, the record is opened.

*Line 1.* Each monthly series is traced to its publisher's calendar: the period it describes, the lag to publication, how long it is revised. The platform panel is a different animal — daily or hourly, from a platform — and gets its own line with its own lifetime. The page's freshness lifetime is set from the slowest monthly series' frequency, counted from its publication date, plus a margin; and the four parts that will appear beside each figure — period, published, last revised, how often — are drafted now, in whatever words the designers choose.

*Line 2.* The build-time dependencies are listed with their pages open: the statistics interface with its beta label and 200 a minute; the geocoder with its four requests a minute for scheduled scripts; the boundary file with its date and two owners. The read-time dependencies are the host and, if the map is live-tiled, the tile supplier — and here the studio sees that it has a choice to make about line 7 before line 2 is complete. The platform panel's dependency is a platform whose terms forbid caching beyond "reasonable periods", reserve the right to block calls "not primarily in response to direct user actions", and may change "at any time". That is a dependency on terms, not supply, and it is written down as such; whether it is acceptable is the prototype's question, and the record leaves the line open rather than answering it.

*Line 3.* A scheduled job, at seventeen minutes past three, that fetches each monthly series, commits what it finds, and is safe to run twice. The job is in a public repository, so the studio writes beside it: *may be disabled after sixty days without activity, and whether the job's own commits count is unconfirmed; the watch in line 4 is what catches this either way.*

*Line 4.* An independent heartbeat, pinged after the job has confirmed that at least one series' date advanced, raising a ticket to a shared address, with a named second. The studio notes that the scheduler's own notifications go to whoever last edited the schedule, and routes them to the same address.

*Line 5.* "The newest monthly figure on the page was published no more than fifty days ago, eleven months in twelve." When a fetch fails, each series shows its last good figure with its age in words; the platform panel, if its platform refuses, shows the last successful fetch and its time — for as long as the terms recorded in line 2 allow it to be kept — and never nothing. The degraded mode is exercised on launch day by breaking the job on purpose, and the date is written down.

*Line 6.* The page is opened, before launch, on a four-year-old mid-range phone on a throttled connection. The budget is written down. The figures and the table are in the HTML and appear before the map's script has been fetched.

*Line 7.* The map's supply is chosen with its ending in view, and the boundaries are the district's own, cut from a generalised product, dated, with their two-owner attribution. The renderer's weight is measured against the budget; if it is most of the script allowance, the studio decides — a creative decision, recorded as one — whether the restyled face is worth it or whether a raster version serves the slowest quarter. The twin table exists at launch, not later.

*Line 8.* The studio writes, before anything has closed, what happens when a monthly source retires or the platform withdraws: the notice shown on the face, the archive address at which the series' last state remains, and the day the tile comes off. It writes the same for the page as a whole.

*Line 9.* A role, a second, and the address of this record.

*Line 10.* Not promised: a live count of anything; an uptime figure for the free host; any series derived from a platform whose terms forbid deriving it.

The record is published beside the page. Nothing in it says what the district is like, and nothing in it is a design; where it names a form — a table, a set of words beside a figure — the form is the designers' to choose. It is the method of keeping the promise the page's first screen makes the moment it appears: that what it shows is current, and that someone is keeping it so.

## What proves a break works

The test for whether a departure from one of this chapter's rules has cost anything is the same shape as the earlier chapters', with a different clock: **take one of the page's sources away, or stop its refresh, and open it on the baseline phone a week later.** If a reader who was not in the room can tell, from the face alone, how old each figure is, which series is missing and why, and that someone knows — the break has cost nothing. If the page is blank, or shows a date that is the page's rather than the data's, or shows last month's figures as this month's with no sign, the break has failed, however well the page loaded.

For a departure that adds a live element, the test is the chain arithmetic done with real numbers and written in the record. For a vector map, it is the renderer's weight counted against the script budget on the baseline device, with the twin table present. For a platform dependency, it is the clause that permits what the dashboard does, quoted in the record beside the series. For a studio of one, it is the name of the second.

## Looking back

Against Chapters 0 to 7, the archived version 1, the Decision Book and the design system.

- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's "freshness and provenance are first-screen furniture" and **extends** it: the furniture is four things, not one (period, published, revised, frequency), and the official vocabulary separates timeliness from punctuality. **Counterbalances** Chapter 1's observatory: the standard by which a long-lived civic page is admired — kept copy, data's date, alarm to a role — applies to the studio's own plan first. `[Counterbalance → Ch 1]`
- **Strengthens** Chapter 2's finding that each reader has official methods, and adds the operations reader's: service levels, error budgets and the three signals. **Opens a thread**: Chapter 2's smartphone-only readers are now a number — one in five, three in ten in DE households — and a budget.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 3's "show the shape" by pricing it: the map that shows the shape of a place costs half the script budget before a tile, and the table is the shape's twin. **Counterbalances** any Chapter 3 rule that assumes a script is present: the figures are in the HTML first. `[Counterbalance → Ch 3]`
- **Strengthens** Chapter 4 throughout. The provenance set's "revision status" becomes three dates and a frequency; its platform caveat becomes a dependency on terms; its "a zero that is a null" becomes "Invalidating or emptying local state as a result of a failed refresh". **Opens a thread**: line 2 of the service record and the provenance set's source lines overlap; one may contain the other. `[Revisit at end]`
- **Strengthens** Chapter 5's rule that a measure's recipe is kept: the recipe now has a refresh schedule and a retirement plan. **Opens a thread** for Chapter 5's Goodhart section: a dashboard that is reliable for a year is itself a target that trains its readers (the synthesised outage).
- **Strengthens** Chapter 6's platform-liquidation caution with the platforms' own terms (store, aggregate, derive, combine: each forbidden somewhere) and its small-number rules with the police interface's snapping, which is a disclosure method with a map-shaped side-effect. **Counterbalances** Chapter 6's "contributed to" ladder from the other side: a claim earned by a tested theory of change still has to be re-earned when the source behind it closes. `[Counterbalance → Ch 6]`
- **Strengthens** Chapter 7's "a change read against its own history" with the archive: the history is the version history of fetches, and a base month is a committed file. **Strengthens** Chapter 7's season rule from the operations side: the statistics office's blocks vary "depending on release schedules and observed demand", so the morning the comparison most wants new data is, on the chapter's reading, the morning it is most likely to be refused.
- **Strengthens** version 1's Chapter 8 in two positions (freshness as a display element; staleness degrades visibly) and **qualifies** both: the date shown is the data's, not the page's; and visible degradation stops short of silent disappearance, with a retirement procedure behind it. **Weakens** version 1's "no admin" as a slogan into a sentence with a person in it. **Counterbalances** version 1's Chapter 1 observatory as above.
- **Opens a thread** for Chapter 0: five records now sit beneath a dashboard's numbers; "Fixed", "Fixed as a procedure" and "Fixed as a stance" are nearly equal in number, with almost nothing Adaptable; and the chapter has used "Standard" for protocol specifications, licences and platform terms, which Chapter 0's legend should admit or rename.
- **Opens a thread** for Chapter 9: any live measure of a vibe accepts the chain arithmetic and the platform terms; the chapter should begin with that cost known.
- **Opens a thread** for Chapter 10: the platform clauses read here as technology are also clauses about consent and about whose behaviour a derived measure describes.
- **The Decision Book.** Chapters 8 and 15 were read, with the rule register. **Agrees:** DTA-05 and DTA-06 (boundary vintage with every geographic figure; no cross-vintage comparison without a stated lookup) are this chapter's line 7; BRF-15 ("Put version and date on the face of anything that will be superseded") and BRK-18 (a derived artefact names its version) are truth 1 in the Decision Book's own words; DSH-13 ("Choose tooling against the accessibility requirements at project start") is the chapter's "budget before you build"; DSH-15 ("Gaps render as gaps in every filter state") is the compartment rule; DSH-03 and DSH-18 (default view answers unaided; explanation beside, not in tooltips) are what survives without script. **Gaps:** the Decision Book has no rule on freshness display (period, published, revised, frequency), on dependencies and their terms, on the refresh mechanism, the watch, the alarm or the role that reads it, on an availability objective or degraded mode, on a byte budget or baseline device, on progressive enhancement or the single-page application, on tile supply and licence, or on retirement and archive. DSH-21 lists "empty and error states" as Decorative; this chapter holds that the degraded state — last good copy, age in words, which series is missing — is a Fixed procedure and not a decoration. Logged as a candidate chapter: *Running it* (or an extension of Chapter 15).
- **The design system (Style Guide v1.5).** **Agrees:** "Every chart carries a provenance code, reference period, geography and boundary vintage" is line 7 and part of line 1; the seven tests' "Phone — understandable quickly on a small screen?" and "Screen-reader — does the meaning survive without the visual treatment?" are section 9 and the twin. **Gaps:** no component for the four-part freshness statement; no component for the degraded state of a tile; no stated byte budget, baseline device or script-free rendering requirement; no map component at all, and so no rule for the twin, the pan and zoom buttons, the tile attribution or the logo conditions a supplier imposes; the running foot carries "v1.5 · date" for the document and nothing for the data. These are design decisions and are logged, not made.

## Design system (Style Guide v1.5) and Decision Book — conflict log

**Agrees.** The provenance code on every chart — "reference period, geography and boundary vintage" — is the geography half of this chapter's line 7 and the period half of line 1. DTA-05 and DTA-06 are the boundary rules as stated. BRF-15 ("Put version and date on the face of anything that will be superseded") and BRK-18 (name the version) are truth 1 in the house's own words, and the running foot already carries "v1.5 · date" for the document. DSH-13 (choose tooling against the requirements at the start) is the budget rule. DSH-15 (gaps render as gaps in every state) and DSH-03/DSH-18 (the default view answers unaided; explanation beside, not in a tooltip) are what this chapter needs to survive without script. The seven tests' "Phone" and "Screen-reader" lines are section 9 and the twin.

**Conflicts.** One direct. DSH-21 classes "empty and error states" as Decorative, "free within the rules above"; this chapter holds that the degraded state — the last good copy kept, its age in words, which series is missing and since when — is a Fixed procedure (AWS REL05-BP01; RFC 5861; the SRE workbook), and that a tile which blanks on a failed fetch is a defect and not a style. The rule register needs the state split: how the degraded tile *looks* is Decorative; *that* it shows the last good copy with its age is Fixed. Two tensions. The design system's recognisable face is a styled vector map, and this chapter prices that at over two-fifths of the script budget of the seventy-fifth-percentile phone before a tile arrives; whether the face is worth the budget is a creative decision, but the system needs a raster or static fallback state and a twin-table component before the decision can be made with its cost visible. And the design system has no component for the four-part freshness statement (period, published, revised, frequency) and no running-foot slot for the data's date as distinct from the document's; the chapter asks for both and leaves their form to the designers. **Logged for the design system:** a freshness component; a degraded-tile state; a map component with twin, buttons, attribution and supplier-logo slots; a stated byte budget and baseline device; a script-free rendering requirement. **Logged for the Decision Book:** a chapter on running a dashboard — freshness, dependencies and terms, refresh, watch and alarm, objective and degraded mode, budget, tile supply, retirement, owner — or an extension of Chapter 15 that carries them.
