## Before you start

This is the ninth chapter of a research book written for Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio led by Carly Heath, about how dashboards work and why. The book exists to support the Vibe Map — a planned public, non-commercial, interactive account of the people, activity, value and support systems that sustain a place's nightlife culture, built first for the West of England. You do not need to have read Chapters 1 to 8 to read this one, which is the book's central chapter and the one its title question comes to rest in. Six things from the earlier chapters recur: the working definition — *a dashboard is a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader*; Chapter 4's rule that a platform's data describes the platform's users; Chapter 5's first instruction, *define the construct before the proxy*, and its finding that "validation by recognition" splits into legitimacy (who chooses what counts) and validity (what the evidence supports); Chapter 6's first rung, that behaviour is evidence of value and a dashboard says who is in the record; Chapter 7's rule that a change is read against its own history; and Chapter 8's price for any source that is live — a dependency with terms, a chain of availability, and a budget on the phone. Where this chapter refers to Carly, it means the studio's founder; where it refers to the Vibe Map, it is illustrating, not specifying. The studio's rulebook for design decisions is the Decision Book; its visual rules are in a design system (Style Guide v1.5).

Two conventions, explained fully in Chapter 0. Every rule carries a **kind** — *Standard*, *Evidence*, *Reading*, *Practice* or *Nightlife Culture* — and a **tier**: *Fixed*, *Adaptable* or *Decorative*; "Fixed as a stance" marks a rule the studio adopts whose source is a reading or a practice, and "Fixed as a procedure" fixes the steps and not the parameter. Flags in square brackets mark loose ends: `[Further research]`, `[Prototype flag]`, `[Validate with users]`, `[Revisit → Ch n]`, `[Revisit at end]`, `[Rich reference]` and `[Counterbalance → Ch n]`. A dagger (†) marks a source read at the authors' abstract or summary only; "secondary" marks a quotation reached through another named document; "(read once)" marks a quotation returned on a single reading and not confirmed by a second; "reconstructed" marks an abstract rebuilt from an index record because every copy of the text was closed. The *symmetry test* (Chapter 0) means that every standard this chapter applies to others' methods it applies, in section 14, to the studio's own. The *failure register* is the brief's list, in Carly's words, of the ways a dashboard fails — among them, for nightlife, "if they do not recognise their place within the front-end interface", and "it fails if its an aggregation of 'the biggest'".

Because this chapter is about measurement, it uses a handful of measurement words in one sense each, and gathers them here. A *construct* is the thing one is trying to measure, named in words before any number is attached; a *measure* is the question, scale or procedure that produces the answers, and its result is the distribution of those answers; an *item* is one question in it; "instrument" is used only as a synonym for a measure in the psychometric sense, never for the dashboard. A *register* is one of three senses in which the word "vibe" is used — as something *made*, something *perceived*, something in *excess* of both — and section 1 says where the three come from; the brief's *failure register*, a different sense, is the list of ways a dashboard fails, named below. *Recognition* means the people a measure describes agreeing that it describes them, and section 12 says exactly what kind of evidence that is. The *face* of a panel is its visible surface — what a reader sees without opening anything. Following the soundscape researchers, a *descriptor* is a measure of how people perceive a place, an *indicator* is a measure used to predict a descriptor, and an *index* is a single value derived from either. *Validity* belongs to an interpretation of a number for a use, never to the number itself; *reliability* is whether the same measure gives the same answer twice. *Participatory* means that the people of a place contribute the measurement, and section 5 calls that family participatory for that reason; *co-produced* is reserved, following the definition in section 6, for the case where they also share in what is done with it. *Co-presence* is two people being in the same place at the same time; a *tie* is a relationship between them; section 7 is about why the first is not the second. A *trace* is a record of behaviour left for some other purpose and read for this one. A dashboard's parts are *panels*. The *brief* is Carly's written commission for this book; the *house framework* is the studio's own Vibe Setting Framework, unpublished and confidential, which this book cites and never reproduces; the *data contract* is the prototype repository's own rules for its data. Where "the fetch tool's reader" is mentioned, it means the text-only reader this book's research used to open pages.

A note on this chapter's sources. The research agents again had no web search, and this chapter's literature is the least open in the book so far: most of it sits in journals that refused the tools, so a larger share than usual was read at the authors' abstracts (marked †), through a named reproduction (marked secondary), or not at all (listed as unreached in the sources, with nothing quoted from them). Where a founding text could not be opened — Böhme on atmosphere, Anderson on affect, Lewicka on place attachment, Jacobs's own four conditions — the chapter says so and uses what a reachable source quotes of it, marked. ## The question

**How might one measure a vibe — a felt quality of a place: its atmosphere, its welcome, its liveliness, the sense that something is happening here — in a way that is evidence and not decoration; by which families of method; what does each need and what can each claim; and how is any such measure validated, above all by whether the people who make the place recognise themselves in it?** The book does not answer this for any place. It sets out the methods, their requirements and their failure modes, so that the prototype can choose among them knowing what each costs and what each can say.

---

## The 60-second version

> "Vibe" is not one construct. The literatures that have tried to measure a felt quality of place split it three ways, and the three have different methods and different validation histories. As something *perceived*, it has an international standard: the soundscape series defines its object as the "acoustic environment as perceived or experienced and/or understood by a person or people, in context" (secondary), asks people in situ to rate eight attributes — "Pleasant, Chaotic, Vibrant, Uneventful, Calm, Annoying, Eventful, and Monotonous" — and whose analysts argue for reporting the spread of answers rather than a median; "vibrant" is not a primary dimension but a derived diagonal between pleasant and eventful, and the one study of how people use the scales found "a general non-equidistant interpretation of the scales, particularly on the vibrant-monotonous direction". As something *made* — by lighting, sound, welcome, staging — it has a qualitative protocol whose author calls it "extremely difficult to be analytically precise". As something in *excess* of both, its theorists offer no number, and the one dancefloor ethnography reached warns that felt togetherness "may hide exclusions and injustices".
>
> Four families of method could evidence any of the three, and each has a known failure. **Participatory methods** — maps people mark, self-placed distributions, stories of change — are strongest on legitimacy and weakest on who turns up: in internet mapping "Mapping effort was highest in sampling groups whose livelihoods were closely related to the purpose of the study"; the most-significant-change method's own manual lists understanding "the average experience of participants" among the things it does less efficiently than other methods; a visible self-placement narrows as people see each other's — "even mild social influence can undermine the wisdom of crowd effect" — and an open vote records the organising capacity of whoever arrives ("Boaty McBoatface topped the list with over 124,000 votes"). **Relational measures** — who is there with whom — rest on a duality the founders named, and on a distinction they also named: co-presence is not a tie. The strongest test found, across six datasets from five settings with both kinds of data, concluded that co-presence "is not easily usable to build in a reliable and systematic fashion surrogate contact data" for simulating contagion. **Traces of behaviour** explain a great deal of variance in a proxy its authors did not validate — "as much as 77% of the variability of district activity is explained by simple structural and static features", where activity is phone connections and "has been assumed to be a good proxy for city's vitality" — and the people in the trace are not the people in the place. **Signals read as feelings** — text sentiment, images, sound, song features — track surveys weakly or inconsistently unless fitted to them (dictionary methods "yielded inconsistent county-level well-being measurements"; the founders of the hedonometer wrote "we quantify only how people appear to others"), and the authoritative review of faces found "no unique mapping between a configuration of facial movements and instances of an emotion category" — a finding the EU has since written into law as the reason for a prohibition.
>
> The method closest to asking the person at the moment is experience sampling by location, and the US National Academies say momentary methods "are often regarded as the gold standard for capturing experiential states" while recommending they stay "experimental" in official surveys. The official precedent for building a felt measure properly is the national wellbeing programme: four questions on a 0–10 scale after "an extensive programme of question testing" (read once), reported as bands as well as means because "relying only on average ratings might mask important differences in how people rate their well-being", not published "if the sample size they are based on is fewer than 50", within a framework developed from "over 34,000 responses" to a public question about what matters.
>
> Validation of a new felt measure is argued, not declared: "Validity refers to the degree to which evidence and theory support the interpretations of test scores for proposed uses of tests", from five named sources of evidence, and "Face validity is at best a very weak kind of evidence". So "the scene recognises itself" is necessary and not sufficient: it is content and response-process evidence from the people described, plus legitimacy about who chose what counts, and it still owes the other three. When the felt and the counted disagree, that is a reportable result — "Discordance occurs if the qualitative and quantitative findings are inconsistent, incongruous, contradict, conflict, or disagree with each other" — and the method pre-commits to reporting it.
>
> So the canonical truths: a vibe is named as a construct and a register before it is measured, and the three registers are never collapsed into one number; a felt measure is a distribution of people's answers, reported with its spread and its sample, never a point alone; who was asked, who answered and who was missing are part of the measure; participation is evidence of what participants placed, not of the place, until selection is accounted for, and it becomes co-production only when what it changed is shown; co-presence is not a tie and a trace is not a feeling — each is read for what it records, and a derived signal is validated against the people before it stands for them; a new felt measure is validated from five sources of evidence, and recognition by the people described supplies two of them and not the other three; when the felt and the counted disagree the disagreement is reported, not resolved by preferring the number. Beneath any felt measure sits a *measure record*, and the measure's first validation is a conversation with the people it describes.

## 1 · What the word is asked to carry

The brief asks: "Instead of measuring 'feeling of safety', why are we not instead measuring the vibe that night time communities create?" Before a method can be chosen, the chapter has to say what the word is being asked to carry, because the literatures that have tried to measure something like it do not agree on what it is, and their disagreement is the first finding.

### Three senses, from three literatures

Version 1 of this book split the vibe into three registers and held that they are never collapsed. The split is re-tested here against the sources rather than assumed, and it holds, with one correction: the perceived register's word "vibrant" turns out to be a derived quantity, not a measured one (section 3).

The first sense is atmosphere as something **made**. Gernot Böhme's founding paper could not be opened; what the one reachable study of his method quotes from it is "aesthetic work", which Böhme describes as "'making' atmospheres through work on an object" (secondary, via Michels). Michels, taking this into an empirical programme, writes that "Aesthetic work can be described as a process through which material components are selected and shaped" — in a venue, to take the chapter's own examples, the set, the lights, the sound, the door, the welcome. In this sense a vibe is produced by identifiable people doing skilled work, and the work is observable.

The second sense is atmosphere as something **perceived**. The International Organization for Standardization's soundscape series defines its object as the "acoustic environment as perceived or experienced and/or understood by a person or people, in context" — the wording is reached here through a protocol paper that quotes it, since ISO's own text is closed (secondary) — and the definition's weight falls on *perceived* and *in context*: the thing measured is not the sound but people's experience of it where they stand. Section 3 shows that this is the one sense with an instrument, a standard and a validation history, and that one of its eight attributes is "Vibrant".

The third sense is atmosphere as something in **excess** of both. Ben Anderson's 2009 paper was also closed; what Michels quotes of it is that atmospheres "emanate from the assembling of the human bodies, discursive bodies, non-human bodies, and all the other bodies that make up everyday situations" and are "always in the process of emerging and transforming" (secondary). This is the sense in which a room has a feeling that no one person made and no one rating captures. Its theorists do not offer a measure, and the chapter does not invent one for them.

Two further literatures use the word's neighbours and are read for their methods. Urban studies has **vitality**, from Jane Jacobs's four conditions — "(1) mixed use, (2) small blocks, (3) aged buildings, and (4) a sufficient concentration of buildings" (secondary, via Sung et al.; the book itself was unreached) — operationalised in Seoul as pedestrian activity, in Barcelona as a map of the conditions, and in six Italian cities as mobile-phone connections; section 8 reads the Italian study. Marketing has **atmospherics**, from Philip Kotler's 1973 coinage (reached only in tertiary form and not quoted), Mary Jo Bitner's "servicescape" framework for "exploring the impact of physical surroundings on the behaviors of" customers and employees (†), and a line of scales for stadiums, festivals and destinations. Destination-image research made, thirty years ago, a point this chapter will return to: the components of a place's image are "attribute-based images, holistic impressions, and functional, psychological, unique and common characteristics" (†), and "a combination of structured and unstructured methodologies" is needed to reach them — the holistic and the unique parts do not come through rating scales alone.

### The construct before the proxy

Chapter 5's first instruction was to define the construct before the proxy, and this chapter's sources show what happens when the order is reversed. Thomas Frank's 2012 essay on the planning word "vibrant" found that "no one seems to be exactly sure what vibrancy is", and quotes the funder consortium that had made "Vibrancy Indicators" its measure of success saying, on its own website, "While we are not able to measure vibrancy directly" — and continuing, in Frank's quotation, that "we believe that the measures we are assembling, taken together, will provide useful insights into the nature and location of especially vibrant places within cities" (Frank quoting ArtPlace; Markusen quotes the same sentence with a different last word, and the funder's page was not sought). Ann Markusen, the same year, named the problem: "A fuzzy concept is one that means different things to different people, but flourishes precisely because of its imprecision", and "In any particular place, changes in the proposed indicators will not be attributable to the creative placemaking intervention alone" (read once). A word that flourishes because it is imprecise is a word that any indicator can be attached to and none can be held to.

The soundscape researchers' vocabulary is the chapter's corrective. Francesco Aletta and colleagues distinguish *descriptors* — "measures of how people perceive the acoustic environment" — from *indicators*, "measures used to predict the value of a soundscape descriptor", and from *indices*, "single value scales derived from either descriptors or indicators that allow for comparison across soundscapes" (the first two at source; the third secondary, via Mitchell et al.). Read across the four families below, the distinction does most of the work: the participatory and experience-sampling methods produce *descriptors* — people's own reports; traces, signals and amenity counts produce *indicators* at best — things that may predict a descriptor, and that have to be shown to before they stand for one; and anything that collapses either into one number is an *index*, with Chapter 5's price list attached. A vibe, measured, is a descriptor. Everything else stands for it on evidence or does not stand for it.

### Why not "feeling of safety"

The brief's contrast case is worth one paragraph of method, because it is not only a different construct. The official measure of feeling safe — the crime survey's question about walking alone after dark — could not be reached in its current wording (the questionnaires returned errors; the chapter does not print it from memory). What could be reached is the official statistics office's own caution that "individuals' perceptions of crime on a national level do not typically match well to reality", and the criminological record of testing the question inside the official survey. Farrall and colleagues' 1997 methodological study "raised the possibility that the fear of crime has been significantly misrepresented", concluding that "our understanding of the fear of crime is a product of the way it has been researched rather than the way it is" (†). Gray, Jackson and Farrall, running new frequency-and-intensity questions beside the old ones in the 2003–4 survey, found that "few people experience specific events of worry on a frequent basis", that the old questions "magnify the everyday experience of fear", and that worry about crime "is often best seen as a diffuse anxiety about risk rather than any pattern of everyday concerns over personal safety" (†). The office's own note records that the feeling-safe questions "are not included on the year ending 2017 and year ending 2018 questionnaires". So the contrast case is a warning rather than a foil: a single felt item, asked for decades, was shown, by new questions run beside it in the official survey, to "magnify" what it reported. A vibe question asked without the same tests would be in no better position, whatever its intention.

## 2 · Why people believe what they believe

### Belief one: a vibe cannot be measured, so it is decoration

The register in which it cannot be counted is one of three. The perceived register has an international standard, an eight-item questionnaire and a protocol that collects "a minimum of 100 responses" per location. What cannot be done is to measure the three as one. Section 1 and 3.

### Belief two: a vibe can be measured, so give it a score

The standard's own analysts argue against collapsing a place to a median and for reporting the "percent of people likely to perceive" it one way or another; the national wellbeing measure reports bands because averages "might mask important differences". A felt measure is a distribution or it is misleading. Section 3 and 11.

### Belief three: ask the people and you have the answer

Ask which people. In participatory mapping, effort is highest among those whose livelihoods depend on the result; people mark near home; the recruitment route that raises response lowers data quality; and "those who choose to participate are frequently quite unrepresentative of any larger public". Section 5.

### Belief four: if they came together, they are connected

Co-presence is "(by definition) much denser than" contact, and surrogate networks built from it are "only partially able to identify the most central nodes". Who is in the room together is first a fact about the pool. Section 7.

### Belief five: the data already knows — count the phones, the check-ins, the posts

A trace is evidence of what it records. The best-known vitality model explains 77 per cent of a proxy its authors call "assumed"; geotagged posts are "approximately 1%" of posts and skew by income, age, urbanity and ethnicity; perceived and objectively measured neighbourhood variables "exhibited low agreement". Section 5 and 8.

### Belief six: sentiment analysis measures feeling

Its founders say it measures "how people appear to others". Dictionary methods were "inconsistent" at county scale; the models that worked were trained on the survey they were meant to replace; the face is "neither reliably expressed through nor perceived from a common set of facial movements". Section 9.

### Belief seven: recognition is validation

"Face validity is at best a very weak kind of evidence." Recognition by the people described is necessary — it is response-process and content evidence, and it is legitimacy — and it supplies two of the five sources of validity evidence, not the other three. Section 12.

### Belief eight: when the number and the feeling disagree, the number is right

Mixed-methods doctrine names the disagreement *discordance*, treats it as a result, and lists "challenging the validity of the constructs" among the responses. Section 13.

## 3 · The perceived register: the one with an instrument

Of the three senses, one has been turned into a standard, and the chapter reads it closely because it is the nearest thing that exists to a tested way of asking people how a place feels.

### The standard and its lineage

ISO 12913-1:2014 "provides a definition and a conceptual framework of soundscape" and "explains factors relevant for measurement and reporting in soundscape studies". Part 2 (2018) "specifies requirements and supporting information on data collection and reporting"; its methods, as the protocol paper describes them, are "soundwalks combined with questionnaires (Methods A and B) and narrative interviews (Method C)" (secondary), and its questionnaire asks respondents to rate the present sound environment on eight attributes — "Pleasant, Chaotic, Vibrant, Uneventful, Calm, Annoying, Eventful, and Monotonous" — on a "5-point Likert scale" from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Part 3, on data analysis, was withdrawn on 8 May 2025 and replaced by a 2025 second edition; anything citing the 2019 analysis standard as current is out of date, and the chapter could read neither edition's text.

The eight attributes are not arbitrary. They come from a 2010 listening experiment in which "One hundred listeners measured 50 excerpts of binaural recordings of urban outdoor soundscapes on 116 attribute scales", yielding "three components: Pleasantness, eventfulness, and familiarity, explaining 50, 18 and 6% of the total variance, respectively" (†) — the listeners being "100 students" in Sweden, a later paper notes. Three components, of which the third was dropped downstream; the two that remain are placed at right angles, and the eight attributes sit around them at forty-five-degree intervals. That geometry descends from James Russell's 1980 circumplex of affect — "affective concepts fall in a circle in the following order": pleasure, excitement, arousal, distress, displeasure, depression, sleepiness, relaxation — built on "36" and "34" undergraduates and "343 male and female students" at one Canadian university, which Russell himself said "accounts for a substantial proportion, but not all, of the variance in self-reported affective states"; and from his 1981 study with Ward and Pratt of "105 commonly used adjectives descriptive of the affective quality of molar physical environments", rated by "323 subjects", which found two bipolar factors of "pleasing and arousing quality" (†). "Vibrant", in other words, is the great-grandchild of "exciting": the pleasant-and-aroused corner of a circle drawn around student ratings in 1980.

### What the measure does with the answers

The analysis standard (2019 edition; formulae reached through the authors' software tutorial, secondary) turns the eight ratings into two coordinates. Pleasantness is the sum of (pleasant − annoying), of cos 45° × (calm − chaotic) and of cos 45° × (vibrant − monotonous), scaled to lie between −1 and 1; eventfulness is (eventful − uneventful) plus cos 45° × (chaotic − calm) plus cos 45° × (vibrant − monotonous), scaled the same way. Two things follow that a builder should see before borrowing the word. First, "vibrant" has no coordinate of its own: the vibrant–monotonous difference loads positively on *both* axes, so vibrant is, by construction, the diagonal where pleasant meets eventful. A place can land on the vibrant diagonal by the arithmetic even where few respondents endorsed the "Vibrant" item itself. Second, the arithmetic assumes that respondents treat the five points of each scale as equally spaced and the eight attributes as evenly placed around the circle — and the one study that tested this, on "984 soundscape assessments across 11 urban locations in London", found that "it is still unclear whether the metrics of the scales are consistently interpreted during a typical assessment task" and that "The overall results show a general non-equidistant interpretation of the scales, particularly on the vibrant-monotonous direction". The weakest axis of the standard instrument is exactly the one the brief's word sits on.

The protocol's authors have made the argument that matters most for a dashboard. Against describing a location by a single average, they compute the coordinates "for each individual response" — "rather than calculating the median response to each PA in the location, then calculating the circumplex coordinates" — and urge speaking of the "percent of people likely to perceive" a place as pleasant or vibrant. The standard's recommendation, as its analysts report it, is the median; theirs is the distribution. A place does not have a vibe score; it has a spread of people's perceptions, and the spread is the finding.

### How it is collected, and by whom

The Soundscape Indices protocol is the fullest worked example of the perceived register in the field. It pairs "a Recording Stage to collect audio-visual recordings" with "a Questionnaire Stage to collect in situ soundscape assessments via a questionnaire method paired with acoustic data collection", and "Typically, a minimum of 100 responses are collected at each location during multiple 2–5-h sessions" — no rationale for the hundred is given — with recordings "taken next to each respondent", building a database of "nearly 4000 participants' responses from 59 locations in 10 cities and provinces across the UK, China, Spain, and Italy". Recruitment is to "Approach participants and ask if they would be willing to take part in a research study": the people asked are the people present, which is a strength (they are the ones experiencing the place) and a selection (section 5 returns to who is present). And "there are questions that cannot be practically addressed in situ". Translation is its own validation programme: the standard "does not provide official translations in languages other than English" (secondary), a dataset of "17,441 samples, including 645 participants, for 27 recordings, in 19 languages" exists to supply them, and the Thai team's warning generalises beyond translation — small expert panels "could lead to translations with psycholinguistic meanings that, in a non-expert setting, deviate or distort from that of the source language" (†). A word that means one thing to the people who chose it may mean another to the people asked.

### Other perceived constructs that were tested before they were counted

The soundscape standard is not alone. The environmental psychology of *place attachment* offers the cleanest case of a felt bond with a place being tested before being counted. Scannell and Gifford's framework — "a three-dimensional, person–process–place organizing framework", whose psychological dimension "includes the affective, cognitive, and behavioral components of attachment" — is offered to "create operational definitions for quantitative studies, guide semi-structured interviews for qualitative studies", with the instruction that "One approach would be to develop a place attachment measurement instrument, and explore the factors that emerge". Williams and Vaske did that. Their twelve-item measure splits into "Place dependence (a functional attachment)" — "the importance of a place in providing features and conditions that support specific goals or desired activities" — and "Place identity (an emotional attachment)" — "the symbolic importance of a place as a repository for emotions and relationships that give meaning and purpose to life" — with example items like "I feel 'X' is a part of me." Across four samples (students rating recreation sites; "Shenandoah National Park (n = 2005)"), "The Cronbach alphas for both place identity (range = 0.84 to 0.94) and place dependence (range = 0.81 to 0.94)" supported the two dimensions, and the analysis "suggested that each attachment dimension can be reliably measured with as few as four questionnaire items". Then the candour that a builder should copy: "A general limitation of previous on-site place attachment studies is the relatively low variance and high scores observed on attachment." Ask people who are already there how attached they are, and most say very. The ceiling is in the sampling, not the place.

The UK's own official felt-place item is simpler and less tested. The Community Life Survey asks "how strongly do you feel you belong to your immediate neighbourhood?", with "By neighbourhood we mean a few minutes walking distance from your home" — and, for satisfaction, "Please think of the area within 15 to 20 minutes' walking distance from your home". The question defines its place for the respondent, at two scales, in walking minutes, which is a discipline worth borrowing. Its 2024/25 headline — "62% of adults felt they belonged 'very strongly' or 'fairly strongly' to their immediate neighbourhood", from "177,749" respondents by "Address-Based Online Surveying (ABOS)" at an individual response rate of "19.86%" — is quoted as a method precedent and not as a finding about anywhere. As far as the fetch tool's reader could find, the technical report describes no cognitive testing of the belonging item and no mode-effect analysis; an absence claim is weaker than a presence claim, and it is recorded as such.

What the perceived register teaches, then, is this. It can be asked. When it is asked well, the question defines its place, uses a tested set of attributes, pairs the rating with the moment and the conditions, collects a hundred answers at a location, and reports the spread. When it is asked badly, it inherits an untested word, averages a room into a point, and measures the ceiling of whoever was already there.

## 4 · The made register and the excess register

The other two senses of the word have no standard, and the chapter's job for them is different: to say what methods their own literatures use, what those methods can claim, and what each refuses to claim.

### Atmosphere as made: a qualitative protocol that names its own limit

Michels's 2015 essay is the clearest statement of a method for the made register, and it begins by admitting the difficulty: "I suggest activating the notion of atmospheres as a heuristic device to empirically research affects", along three dimensions — "their spatio-materiality, their sensuality and their (in)stability" — while "it is extremely difficult to be analytically precise when empirically researching atmospheres". His three steps are the section headings of his method: tracing the material modulation of atmospheres, by "taking on the role of a 'sensory apprentice'" through "participant observation and (narrative) interviews"; exploring the sensual capacities of bodies, for which "I present an auto-ethnographic field note"; and accounting for the (in)stability of atmospheres, which "requires a researcher to 'zoom out' and to describe similar compositions of affective atmospheres at other sites". He adds that "decomposing atmospheres along these lines may not resonate with other ways of conceptualising" them.

What this gives a dashboard is, first, not a number but a description with makers named; if a number is attempted for this register, section 12 says it is a formative one. The made register is evidence about *who did what to the room* — the set, the sound, the light, the door, the welcome — and it is gathered by watching, asking and comparing across sites. Its product is description with named makers in it. Sumartojo and Pink's methods book, reached only at the publisher's description, proposes "a research-based way of understanding and intervening in atmospheres" that treats them as "dynamic and changing configurations that allow analytical insight into a range of topics when we think in, about and through them"; the description does not use the word "measure", and the chapter does not attribute a measurement claim to it. Tim Edensor's study of a seaside illumination (†; read for method only) adds the finding that matters for the next sections: "affective atmospheres are coproduced by visitors as part of a reiterative, festive, convivial, and playful social practice in familiar space", with "the anticipation of affect" part of the making. The atmosphere is not only staged by the people who run a place; it is made by the people who come, which is a direct argument for asking them.

### Atmosphere as excess: the warning that travels with it

For the third register the chapter has less to work with, because its founding paper was closed and its best ethnography is a book reached at its publisher's page. What Anderson's paper says in the fragments Michels quotes — atmospheres "emanate from the assembling of the human bodies, discursive bodies, non-human bodies, and all the other bodies that make up everyday situations" (secondary) — is a description of something that no single rating reaches, and the chapter reports it as that. The commonly quoted formula "singular affective qualities that emanate from but exceed the assembling of bodies" could not be verified at source and is not printed as a quotation.

Luis Manuel Garcia-Mispireta's *Together, Somehow* is the dancefloor ethnography the brief named, and it is read here for one methodological warning and nothing about any scene. The publisher's description says the book draws "on time spent in the minimal techno and house music subscenes" and reaches a concept of "stranger-intimacy"; and it says of the felt togetherness it studies that "this sense of belonging can be vague, fluid, and may hide exclusions and injustices", (two further phrases on the same page — that such spaces "exclude as much as they include" — are a reviewer's praise quoted there, not the description's, and are attributed as such). For a measure, the consequence is exact: a high score on felt togetherness is compatible with exclusion, because the people excluded were not in the room to be asked. "Who was not here" is a validity question for the excess register before it is an ethical one.

### What the three registers settle together

Put beside each other, the three literatures agree on more than their vocabulary suggests. Those who call a felt quality measurable — the soundscape standard, the place-attachment psychologists, the destination-image researchers — pair the scale with open questions, interviews or recordings of the moment. Those who treat it as resistant to number — Böhme through Michels, Anderson, Edensor — still name concrete methods: observation, interview, auto-ethnography, comparison across sites. Nobody in either camp proposes a single number for the three together. Version 1's rule that the three registers are displayed as three and never collapsed is strengthened by every source read, and section 14 records it as such. What the chapter adds is the vocabulary of section 1: the perceived register yields a *descriptor*; the made register yields a *description with makers named*; the excess register yields *testimony*, and a warning about who is missing from it.

## 5 · The participatory family

The first family of method asks the people of a place to contribute the measurement themselves: to mark a map, place themselves on a line, tell a story of change, take a photograph. The brief calls for it; the house framework, confidential and cited only, grew out of it; and version 1 made it the spine of its felt layer. This section reads the evidence on what such methods are evidence *of*, and it is more sobering than the enthusiasm around them.

### Participatory mapping: who marks, how much, and where

Public participation GIS — asking people to place pins, routes and areas on a map and answer questions about what they placed — is the most developed of the participatory methods, and its leading researcher has reviewed its own sampling effects. Greg Brown's 2017 review of twenty-six internet mapping studies (†) begins by noting that "systematic study of spatial data quality and sampling effects is limited", and then states what the studies show: "Mapping effort was highest in sampling groups whose livelihoods were closely related to the purpose of the study"; "there was greater mapping effort in household sampling groups compared to volunteer groups"; "participant domicile had strong effects on mapped results through spatial discounting" — people mark near home; and "The use of online Internet panels provides higher response rates but lower spatial data quality." Three findings a builder must carry: the people with most at stake mark most; everyone marks their own patch; and the recruitment route that raises the response count lowers the quality of what is marked.

The field's own leaders say the unmet need is proof that the mapping changes anything. Brown and Kyttä's 2018 synthesis of expert working groups (†) found "The most significant cross-cutting theme to emerge across all PM domains was the need to evaluate PM outcomes to provide evidence of success", and an earlier review of some thirty ecosystem-service mapping studies found "little evidence that mapped ecosystem data was used for actual decision support in land use planning" (†). Kahila-Tani and colleagues, reflecting on "over 200 real life planning cases in Finland (62%) and other countries (38%)", warn that "Planners lack the knowledge of usable tools to reach broader groups of participants, which can turn participation into a small-group elitist activity" and that "the collected data remains invisible and systematic analysis is often not realized" (†). A map that people marked and nobody used is the participatory version of the observatory in Chapter 8.

What a careful small-study looks like is also on record. Reed and colleagues' 2025 collaborative mapping (read for method only) ran three discussion events "with between two and seven self-identified lesbian people at each event (12 in total)", with participants "invited to annotate it using coloured post-it notes, stickers, pens, and highlighters", "prompt cards around tables", and "five expert interviews"; and its authors wrote the sentence every participatory map should carry: "We make no claim that the maps produced represent a coherent and fixed lesbian community." The maps "are always partial, always fragmented, and always in a process of becoming", and people without an entry point to community engagement "were likely not reached by this recruitment strategy". Twelve people, with the bound stated, is evidence about those twelve; twelve people presented as a community is not.

The tools themselves describe the method plainly. A mapping-survey product lets respondents "leave comments on the map by placing pins, or by drawing routes or areas", asks "follow-up questions on what they draw", and will "Generate heatmaps, dot maps, and maps of community-identified routes or areas." The heat map is the derived product of whoever chose to mark. It is a picture of participation before it is a picture of the place.

### Volunteered data can be good — when there is a right answer to check it against

The citizen-science literature is often cited to reassure, and it does, under conditions. Kosmala and colleagues' review (†) found that "a growing body of publications clearly shows that diverse types of citizen-science projects can produce data with accuracy equal to or surpassing that of professionals", that "Successful projects rely on a suite of methods to boost data accuracy and account for bias" — "iterative project development, volunteer training and testing, expert validation, replication across volunteers, and statistical modeling of systematic error" — and that "Each citizen-science dataset should therefore be judged individually, according to project design and application, and not assumed to be substandard simply because volunteers generated it". Haklay's comparison of OpenStreetMap with the national mapping agency found volunteered geography "can be fairly accurate: on average within about 6 m of the position recorded by the OS", with the weakness in coverage rather than accuracy (†). Every one of those reassurances rests on a ground truth: a species that was or was not there, a road that is or is not at that coordinate. Expert validation, replication across volunteers and modelling of systematic error all presuppose something to check against. A felt quality has no such truth outside the people asked, so the citizen-science evidence does not transfer; what transfers is the discipline — training, replication, modelling who contributed — and the knowledge that it was built for a different kind of question.

What does transfer whole is participation inequality. Haklay: "Participation inequality – the phenomenon that a very small percentage of participants contribute a very significant proportion of information to the total output – is persistent across Volunteered Geographic Information (VGI) and citizen science projects", "also known as the 1% rule or the 90-9-1 rule", with the rule that "When using and analysing crowdsourced information, consider the implications of participation inequality on the data and take them into account in the analysis" (†). The rule's origin is a 2006 practitioner heuristic — "In most online communities, 90% of users are lurkers who never contribute, 9% of users contribute a little, and 1% of users account for almost all the action" — whose author adds, under customer feedback, "you're getting an unrepresentative sample", and whose first step is "to recognize that it will always be with us." A participatory layer is made by the one per cent. That is not a reason not to build it. It is a reason to say so on its face.

### People can produce their own numbers — on two conditions

Robert Chambers's 2007 paper is the strongest statement that local people can generate statistics, and it should be read whole because it carries its own conditions. "Participatory approaches and methods can generate quantitative as well as qualitative data"; "Statistical principles can be applied to participatory numbers"; "Comparisons of costs and accuracy of questionnaires and participatory numbers have been decisively in favour of the latter"; "The extreme accuracy of most social mapping in a census mode has been repeatedly confirmed", "especially when there is triangulation between groups from the same community". The claims rest on cited field cases in international development rather than a systematic review, and the paper says where they fail: "Where groups are involved, and as is well known with focus groups generally, those who are most easily convened may be unrepresentative or dominated by one or a few people, or by one sort of person (for example, men in a mixed group of men and women)"; and of the field's earlier practice, "Much practice was abusive – imposed, routinised, insensitive, unimaginative, exploitative, and unethical", so that "People were alienated, data were unusable and unused, and the approaches misleadingly discredited". Two conditions follow from his own text — this is the chapter's reading — facilitation quality and whether anything changes: "Whether participatory statistics empower local people is sensitive to official attitudes and acceptance and whether these lead to changes in policy and practice that make a real difference." And his title's question has two senses — "who is the active agent in generating numbers" and "who matters and whose reality matters" — with "the mix and balance of extraction and empowerment" named as the open question. Section 6 is about his second condition.

The best-documented participatory method says in its own manual what it is not best suited to. The Most Significant Change technique "is a form of participatory monitoring and evaluation", participatory "because many project stakeholders are involved both in deciding the sorts of change to be recorded and in analysing the data"; it works by "the systematic selection of the most significant of these stories by panels of designated stakeholders or staff", and "Every time stories are selected, the criteria used to select them are recorded and fed back to all interested stakeholders". It is "monitoring-without-indicators": "MSC does not make use of pre-defined indicators, especially ones that have to be counted and measured." And its originators list where "the benefits may not justify the cost" — "While MSC can be used to address the following, there may be other less time-consuming ways to achieve the same objectives": to "capture expected change"; to "develop good news stories for public relations (PR)"; to "conduct retrospective evaluation of a program that is complete"; to "understand the average experience of participants"; to "produce an evaluation report for accountability purposes"; to "complete a quick and cheap evaluation". A story-of-change method is for the significant, selected and fed back; by its own manual it is a poor route to the average and to the accountability case. Photovoice's three founding goals — "to enable people to record and reflect their community's strengths and concerns", "to promote critical dialogue and knowledge about important issues", "to reach policymakers" (†) — contain no measurement goal at all.

### Self-placed distributions

Version 1 proposed the *human dot plot* — people placing themselves on a line or a grid, the distribution readable in bodies — as both a workshop form and a collection method. The literature on what a self-placement is evidence of comes from four directions, and none of them is the one the proposal hoped for.

The teaching literature's best-known people-as-data display is the living histogram, in which students stand in columns by height; and the statisticians who examined it found that the canonical result is an artefact. "The combined distribution of heights of men and women has become the canonical illustration of bimodality when teaching introductory statistics", yet "Examination of national survey data on young adults shows that the separation between the distributions of men's and women's heights is not wide enough to produce bimodality." The two humps that generations of students saw came from small bins, sampling variability — "it will not be unusual for samples to give two nonadjacent bins that appear taller than the others" — and people rounding to "apparently popular heights". A room of bodies shows a shape, and the shape can be in the room and not in the population.

The facilitation literature describes the spectrum line as a conversation, not a measurement. One practitioner guide: it is for "surfacing and visualizing divergent opinions within a group"; participants position themselves "closer to the end if their agreement is complete and towards the center if their agreement is mixed"; "The facilitator then 'interviews' people along the line, asking them why they are standing where they are"; and "listeners are encouraged to shift their position on the spectrogram as points are made which alter their thinking". Another: spectrum lines "can help to explore the different views or needs around an issue within the group", and the exercise "makes differences very visible", which "could be difficult for anyone who feels isolated in their opinions". Positions are public, explained aloud and expected to move. That is a good design for a conversation and a bad one for a measure, and the two uses should not be confused on a dashboard.

The measurement literature on the private version — a mark on a line, made alone — is better. A visual analogue scale, completed individually, "approximate[s] an interval-scale level" (†), and its authors "conclude that categorical scales only reach ordinal-scale level, and thus visual analogue scales are to be preferred whenever possible" (†). But a single mark is a single item, and the evidence on single items is cautionary: "the predictive validity of single items varies considerably across different (concrete) constructs and stimuli objects"; "under most conditions typically encountered in practical applications, multi-item scales clearly outperform single items in terms of predictive validity"; "Only under very specific conditions do single items perform equally well" — when the construct is "very concrete" and items would correlate above about .80. A vibe is not a concrete construct. One mark on one line is a weak measure of it, however vivid the room.

And the public version measures something the private version does not: the group's convergence. In a laboratory experiment with 144 participants and factual estimation tasks, "even mild social influence can undermine the wisdom of crowd effect": "knowledge about estimates of others narrows the diversity of opinions to such an extent that it undermines the wisdom of crowd effect in three different ways", and "The 'social influence effect' diminishes the diversity of the crowd without improvements of its collective error" (†). A randomised experiment on an online rating site found that "False positive entries led to inflated subsequent scores, whereas false negative initial votes had small long-term effects" (†; editor's summary). The dot-voting guides say the same from practice — "Group voting (often called group think): individuals think 'I'll just vote here since everyone else did'"; "Persuaded voting: one person campaigns for their preferred option" — and no empirical study of dot-voting bias as such was found; the general social-influence experiments are the evidence. A visible self-placement is a record of a room agreeing with itself, and that may be exactly what a workshop wants and exactly what a measure must not mistake for the spread of opinion.

### Who turns up, and what they can do to an open vote

The selection problem is not a detail of participatory method. It is its central limit, and the statistics of web surveys state it without softening. Bethlehem's review: web surveys "provide simple, cheap, and fast access to a large group of potential respondents", but "recruitment of respondents is often based on self-selection", "Both under-coverage and self-selection may lead to biased estimates", and the conclusion is "that under-coverage problems may solve itself in the future, but that self-selection leads to unreliable survey outcomes" (†). Coverage improves with time; self-selection does not, and weighting is a partial repair. Archon Fung's framework for participation puts it from the democratic side: "Though complete openness has an obvious appeal, those who choose to participate are frequently quite unrepresentative of any larger public"; "Individuals who are wealthier and better educated tend to participate more than those who lack these advantages"; and "Randomly selecting participants from among the general population is the best guarantee of descriptive representativeness."

What an open, repeatable vote collects is on the official record. In 2016 the UK's research council asked the public to name a polar ship: "The search for a name attracted over 7,000 suggestions"; "Boaty McBoatface topped the list with over 124,000 votes"; the ship was named "Royal Research Ship Sir David Attenborough", a name with "over 11,000 members of the public supporting it", and "in recognition of the public interest" the winning name went to one of the ship's "remotely operated sub-sea vehicles". The sponsor kept the decision and reported the vote as an input. In 2009 a magazine's online poll for the world's most influential person was won by a message-board founder with "16,794,368 votes and an average influence rating of 90 (out of a possible 100)"; its editor said, "I would remind anyone who doubts the results that this is an Internet poll." These are cases of method, not of nightlife: an unmoderated open vote records whoever arrives in whatever numbers they can muster, and the design answer is known. Deliberative polling begins from the other end: "A random, representative sample is first polled on the targeted issues", then "members of the sample are invited to gather at a single place for a weekend in order to discuss the issues", with "Carefully balanced briefing materials"; and its claim is counterfactual rather than descriptive — "The resulting changes in opinion represent the conclusions the public would reach, if people had opportunity to become more informed and more engaged by the issues." Recruit by sample first; deliberate second; say which claim is being made.

### What the co-produced family can claim

Drawing the section together: a participatory measure is evidence of what the participants placed, said and chose, by the people who turned up, in the conditions they turned up in. That is real evidence and often the only evidence of the perceived and excess registers that exists. It becomes evidence about the place when the selection is known and stated — who was invited, who came, who marked most, who was missing — and when the shape of the answers is reported as a spread and not a point. It becomes *co-production*, in the sense section 6 defines, only when the people who gave it share in what is done with it. And one finding, from a systematic review the research did not go looking for, closes the question of whether residents' ratings of a place agree with observers': across "85 relevant peer-reviewed studies published between 1990 and 2015", "Comparably defined variables exhibited low agreement and only 8.2% were associated with the same outcome" — 8.2 per cent of comparably defined pairs of perceived and objective variables —, and "The perceived neighborhood environment and objectively measured neighborhood environment are related but distinct constructs that account for unique variance" (†). The perceived place and the measured place are two things. A dashboard that shows one and calls it the other has made a category error, and a dashboard that shows both has a finding.

## 6 · "What this changed": feedback as method

Version 1 made "what this changed" a mandatory field on felt data — the display's proof that giving data visibly did something — and found no display precedent for it. This section reads what the participation, consultation and evaluation literatures say about reporting back, because the field turns out to be a method with evidence behind it and not only a courtesy.

### The ladder, and the rung a survey sits on

Sherry Arnstein's 1969 ladder is the text every participation guide cites and most then set aside. Its claim is that "citizen participation is a categorical term for citizen power", that "It is the redistribution of power that enables the have-not citizens", and that "participation without redistribution of power is an empty and frustrating process for the powerless". Its rungs run from manipulation and therapy, through informing, consultation and placation — the three she groups as tokenism — to partnership, delegated power and citizen control (rung names from the reproduction; the figure's labels are an image and were not string-verified). The paragraph a dashboard builder should read is the one on consultation, because it names the method: "The most frequent methods used for consulting people are attitude surveys, neighborhood meetings, and public hearings." Under consultation "People are primarily perceived as statistical abstractions", "participation is measured by how many come to meetings, take brochures home, or answer a questionnaire", and "What citizens achieve in all this activity is that they have 'participated in participation.'" "Inviting citizens' opinions, like informing them, can be a legitimate step toward their full participation. But if consulting them is not combined with other modes of participation, this rung of the ladder is still a sham", because it "offers no assurance that citizen concerns and ideas will be taken into account". Fung's assessment is that "Arnstein's classification still provides a useful corrective to naive and untempered enthusiasm for public participation" while "As an analytic tool, however, it is obsolete and defective in two main ways"; his own three dimensions are "who participates", "mode of communication and decision", and "the link between discussions and policy or public action". The third dimension is "what this changed", named as a dimension of participation and not a nicety.

### The official wording for reporting back

The UK government's own consultation principles put the duty in plain words. Under scrutiny: "Publish any response on the same page on gov.uk as the original consultation, and ensure it is clear when the government has responded to the consultation"; "Explain the responses that have been received from consultees and how these have informed the policy"; "State how many responses have been received"; and "Publish responses within 12 weeks of the consultation or provide an explanation why this is not possible." Elsewhere: "Do not consult for the sake of it"; "Do not ask questions about issues on which you already have a final view"; "Take consultation responses into account when taking policy forward." The courts' version, as a local-government guide paraphrases it with embedded quotations, is the four Gunning principles: consultation when "proposals are still at a formative stage"; "sufficient information to give 'intelligent consideration'"; "adequate time for consideration and response"; and "'conscientious consideration' must be given to the consultation responses before a decision is made" (secondary; the Supreme Court's own text was unreached). Say how many answered, what they said, how it informed the decision, within a stated time, and do not ask what you have already decided. That is "what this changed" as an official standard, and it has existed since 2012.

### Does reporting back change anything? The evidence

The research found no study measuring a "you said, we did" notice as such. It found four pieces of evidence around it. The strongest is behavioural and British: Sjoberg, Mellon and Peixoto tested a model of "objective efficacy—the extent to which an individual's participation actually has an impact" against "behavioral data from the online application Fix My Street (n = 399,364)" and found that "A successful first experience using Fix My Street" — a reported problem fixed — "is associated with a 57 percent increase in the probability of an individual submitting a second report", and that "the experience of bureaucratic responsiveness to the first report submitted has predictive power over all future report submissions" (†). "Associated with", in an observational dataset; but very large, and in the direction the house principle predicts. Two randomised panel-survey experiments add nuance: in the British Household Panel Survey, "tailored reports have a positive though small effect on the number of young people completing a face-to-face interview and increase cooperation for busy people when telephone interviews are included" (†); in a new civil-justice panel, "the tailored newsletter was associated with a significant increase in the wave-two response rate", while "the tailored newsletter, or sending no newsletter at all, were equally effective at inducing response, and significantly better than the general newsletter" (†). A generic report-back did worse than silence; a tailored one did better. And in citizen science, "identification accuracy and retention of novices were higher for those who received automated feedback than for those who received only confirmation of the correct identification without explanation" (†) — feedback on the contributor's own entry, not yet on what the data changed.

The practitioner frameworks describe the loop without measuring it. One defines it as six stages — buy-in, design, collect, analyse, dialogue, course correct — in which "the providers take what they've learned back to the client community to begin a dialogue", and, once there is agreement on what the feedback means, "adjust their program or service in response to the feedback"; it does not use the phrase "closing the loop", and its evidence page cites examples without numbers. Another: "When organizations respond effectively to what they hear, we call this Constituent Voice", with the advice to "Ask your constituents 2 to 5 questions frequently" and "Separate responses as positive, okay, and negative". Practice, not evidence; but practice that puts the response, not the collection, at the centre.

### What makes it co-production

The word is used loosely, and the definitions in the literature are strict enough to settle what "what this changed" is for. Boyle and Harris, in the think-tank paper that carried the term into UK public services, define it as "delivering public services in an equal and reciprocal relationship between professionals, people using services, their families and their neighbours", trace it to Elinor Ostrom's team's studies of Chicago policing in the 1970s — "it was that element of successful policing that only members of the public could provide" — and draw two boundaries: "Co-design and other forms of asking the advice of users may be helping to create space for co-production, but can lack any continuing involvement in delivery", and "When communities or users organise provision with no involvement from public service professionals, whilst this is a very valuable intervention, it is not co-production." (The one-line definition often attributed to Ostrom 1996 is footnoted by Boyle and Harris to Parks et al. 1981; the chapter does not attribute it to the 1996 paper.) By this definition, asking people's views — a survey, a map, a dot — is not co-production. It becomes co-production when the people who gave the data share in what is done with it, and "what this changed" is the visible record that they did.

The same duty appears, more strongly, in the data-governance principles that Chapter 10 will treat in full. The CARE principles' *Responsibility* is "Those working with Indigenous data have a responsibility to share how those data are used to support Indigenous Peoples' self-determination and collective benefit", and "Accountability requires meaningful and openly available evidence of these efforts and the benefits accruing". The principles were written for a specific people and a specific history and are not the studio's to borrow wholesale; the chapter cites them as the clearest statement anywhere that sharing how data was used is an obligation to the people in it. The house framework's finding — confidential, cited as in Chapter 1 — that relationships with institutions can feel "extractive" is the same point from the scene's side.

### What section 6 settles

"What this changed" is not a display flourish. It is the consequence that Arnstein's consultation rung lacks, Fung's third dimension, the consultation principles' duty, Chambers's second condition, Boyle and Harris's boundary and the CARE principles' responsibility, with one large behavioural study finding that being answered predicts taking part again. Version 1 specified it from the community's design principle alone; this chapter can ground it, and section 15 makes it a line in the record and a truth. What the chapter cannot do is design it — the form of the field, its words, where it sits — and section 16 leaves that where it belongs.

## 7 · The relational family: who is there with whom

The second family of method reads a place's life from the relationships in it: who comes to the same events, who returns, which places are the regulars' places. Its data is often already on hand — attendance lists, ticket records, check-ins — and so it is the family most likely to be built first and understood last. The brief's own data contract carries the caution "co-appearance is not a social tie"; this section finds the same caution in the network literature's founding and recent texts, and says what the family can claim once it is observed.

### The duality, and what it was built from

Ronald Breiger's 1974 paper gave the family its form: "A metaphor of classical social theory concerning the 'intersection' of persons within groups and of groups within the individual is translated into a set of techniques to aid in empirical analysis of the interpenetration of networks of interpersonal ties and networks of intergroup ties" (†). People are linked by the events they share; events are linked by the people they share; the two networks are one matrix read two ways. Breiger himself contrasted this "membership network analysis" with "sociometric approaches" — with asking people who their friends are — and applied it to the canonical case, "the study by Davis et al. (1941) of the social participation of eighteen women".

The canonical case is worth a paragraph because of how its original authors drew their conclusion. Davis, Gardner and Gardner had, in Freeman's account (which the verifier could not re-open; the research agent read it at source), "systematic data on the social activities of 18 women whom they observed over a nine-month period", who "had met at a series of 14 informal social events"; and the attendance was reconstructed from "interviews, the records of participant observers, guest lists, and the newspapers". Their inference rule, as Freeman quotes it: "Where it is evident that a group of people participate together in these informal activities consistently, it is obvious that a cliques had been isolated." Twenty-one later analyses of the same eighteen-by-fourteen matrix mostly recovered the same two groups. But the original inference rested on the word *consistently* and on nine months of ethnographic material, not on the matrix alone — and the matrix is what a ticketing feed would give.

### Co-presence is not a tie: the evidence

The data contract's caution has external support from three directions.

The strongest is a direct test. Génois and Barrat asked, across six datasets from five settings — a workplace in two different years, a hospital, a primary school, a conference, a high school — in which both co-presence and face-to-face contact had been recorded by sensors, whether the first could stand for the second. "It is however unclear how much co-presence data can inform us on actual face-to-face contacts"; "The co-presence and the face-to-face contact temporal networks share a number of structural and statistical features, but the former is (by definition) much denser than the latter"; surrogate networks built from co-presence "reproduce some features of the real data but are only partially able to identify the most central nodes of the face-to-face network"; "The performance of the various sampling methods strongly varies depending on context"; and their conclusion is that co-presence data, "while yielding interesting insights into some of the large scale properties of the contact network, is not easily usable to build in a reliable and systematic fashion surrogate contact data" that would reproduce the real contacts well enough to simulate contagion through them — the sentence's own scope. Being in the same room is a much denser network than talking to each other, and the people who look central in the first are not reliably the people central in the second.

The second is the counter-case, which turns out to confirm the rule. Eagle, Pentland and Lazer followed "94 subjects using mobile phones" that logged "Bluetooth devices in proximity of approximately five meters" over nine months at one institution, and found "it is possible to accurately infer 95% of friendships based on the observational data alone". The 95 per cent came from one factor — the one that "loads most heavily on off-campus proximity in the evening and on weekends", labelled "extra-role" — while proximity in the shared workplace was the uninformative one; and "two individuals can be friends without any observable interactions between them for a given period." Co-presence predicted friendship when it was co-presence by choice, outside the setting that brought people together anyway. Crandall and colleagues' finding "that even a very small number of co-occurrences can result in a high empirical likelihood of a social tie" (†) is from a self-selected photo-sharing population with the platform's own contact list as the "tie"; the probabilities themselves are in a body the research could not reach.

The third is the modern network position on what a co-occurrence count is. Neal and colleagues: "Projections of bipartite or two-mode networks capture co-occurrences", and "A key challenge in analyzing such networks is determining whether an observed number of co-occurrences between two nodes is significant, and therefore whether an edge exists between them." Two people who were at the same three events may have been there because the events were large and they both go out a lot. The count is a quantity to be tested against what chance and opportunity would produce before it is called a connection — which is Chapter 6's one-tailed search and Chapter 7's expected value, applied to people.

The sociology underneath all three is the distinction between opportunity and choice. McPherson, Smith-Lovin and Cook: "Similarity breeds connection"; "Geographic propinquity, families, organizations, and isomorphic positions in social systems all create contexts in which homophilous relations form"; and the analytic cut, between "homophily effects that are created by the demography of the potential tie pool as baseline homophily and homophily measured as explicitly over and above the opportunity set as inbreeding homophily". Scott Feld's focus theory is the same idea from the setting's side: "extra-network foci organizing social activities and interaction" (†) bring people into the same place, and the foci, not the people, explain much of who is beside whom. Kossinets and Watts, with data "in which interactions, attributes, and affiliations are all recorded over time", found "tie formation is heavily biased by triadic closure and focal closure, which effectively constrain the opportunities among which individuals may select" (†). Who is in the room together is first a fact about the pool. The data contract's sentence is the network literature's own.

### Scenes as measurable: what the amenity method is and admits

The most ambitious attempt to measure the character of places at scale is Silver and Clark's *scenes* project, and its method is instructive precisely for what it is not. A scene is "a cluster of urban amenities"; the project specifies "15 dimensions of the urban scenescape" in three groups (the pole-pairs from the authors' 2014 table) — theatricality (exhibitionistic/reserved, glamorous/ordinary, neighbourly/distant, transgressive/conformist, formal/informal), authenticity (local/global, state/anti-state, ethnic/non-ethnic, corporate/independent, rational/non-rational) and legitimacy (traditional/novel, charismatic/routine, utilitarian/unproductive, egalitarian/particular, self-expressive/scripted) — and measures them "for each of 40,000 U.S. zip codes" from business directories. The procedure: "A team of coders scored each amenity 1-5 for each of the 15 scene dimensions, with 5 positive, 3 neutral, and 1 negative"; "Whenever we found intercoder reliability fell below r=.8 we would add more detail to the criteria and decision-trees"; and, in the authors' 2014 account, "Each dimension's performance score is the average weight assigned on that dimension for all amenities in a given locality". The authors are exact about their standing: "We do not count amenities, but analyze their implicit substantive meanings"; "scenes and zip codes are not neatly aligned, and the limits of this measure are legion"; and, "Because there are no similar measures to contrast out proposal against using 'construct-validity' (convergent or divergent)", "we initially pursue validation by 'face validity'" and hypothesis validity. The meaning of each amenity is assigned by coders, not by the people in the place.

The authors asked the question themselves, in their book: "Can what are in the end organizational directories really get at something as seemingly ethereal and ephemeral as the experiential meaning of place?" (Silver and Clark, quoted by Borer). The reviewer's answer: "No and yes. But, again, they already knew that." "For those interested in counting the number of cultural amenities in and across cities, Scenescapes is a wonderful exemplary guide"; those "seeking to uncover the sensuous qualities of and interactions with scenes" will "have to look elsewhere for methodological support". For this chapter the lesson is the vocabulary of section 1 again: an amenity profile is an *indicator* of a scene's character, coded by outsiders, validated by face and hypothesis; it is not a descriptor, and it is not a vibe.

### Revealed return, and the regulars

Chapter 6 placed behaviour on the first rung of evidence of value, and return is the behaviour most often read as attachment. Two cautions and one constitutive fact come from the sources.

Stated return is not revealed return. In the one test reached, "Intention to return is often used as a surrogate measure for actual repeat propensity, especially in loyalty studies", and across "longitudinal data from 30 markets" and "cross-sectional data from 152 markets that visited 16 destinations", "no statistically significant correlation exists between intention and actual repeat visitation rates", because "intention is typically measured as a vague aspiration and not in a probabilistic manner" (†). A dashboard that asks "would you come back?" has not measured return. And revealed return alone is not attachment: the loyalty literature defines "Customer loyalty … as the strength of the relationship between an individual's relative attitude and repeat patronage", "mediated by social norms and situational factors" (†) — people return to what is near, cheap and open as well as to what they love, and the behaviour does not say which.

What return *does* constitute, in the third-place literature, is the place's mood. Oldenburg's eight characteristics, as a peer-reviewed secondary source words them, include "a cadre of regulars who attract newcomers and give the space its characteristic mood", and Oldenburg's own sentence, as a planning organisation quotes it: "The character of a third place is determined most of all by its regular clientele and is marked by a playful mood, which contrasts with people's more serious involvement in other spheres." The regulars are the one characteristic defined by return, and they are treated as making the vibe rather than reporting it. Counting third places has been tried by nomination — a national survey "that asked people to identify such places in their community" and found that perceived accessibility of third places predicts perceived quality of life, with the authors noting "we have little empirical evidence that speaks to the subject" (†) — and by establishment counts, which come with their own warning: "It is important to point out that availability does not necessarily mean access", and such data "do not capture organizations or groups that do not have a fixed location". A list of premises is not a list of third places; the regulars make the difference, and they are not in the list.

The device-level measures of return and dwell carry the limits Chapter 4 would expect. A transport operator's Wi-Fi pilot "collected 509 million probing requests from 5.6 million devices" and "constructed 42 million journeys from five million devices" — and "For customers who did not have WiFi enabled, no data was collected", while for some devices "the MAC address was randomised so we were unable to constuct journeys" (typo as printed). A national footfall dataset notes that "Sensors cannot distinguish between mobile devices and other Wi-Fi-enabled devices (e.g., printers or routers)", that "The signal strength and sensor range are variable", and that it "is heavily skewed toward Greater London". Dwell and return by device count devices, lose anyone whose radio is off, and lose the thread when addresses rotate — and address randomisation is now the default on most phones.

### What the relational family can claim

A person-by-event matrix is evidence of who was recorded at what. It supports statements about events (which draw overlapping crowds), about the pool (who goes out at all, where), and about concentration (Chapter 4's grain, with Kossinets's warning that leaving events out of a two-mode network "can dramatically alter estimates of network-level statistics" and inflates clustering). It does not support statements about ties, community or belonging until co-occurrence has been tested against opportunity, and centrality computed from co-presence is not shown as a measure of anyone's importance, because the surrogate networks misplace it. The network's boundary — which events and people are in it — is stated, because Kossinets found that omitting contexts "can dramatically alter estimates of network-level statistics" and that clustering is "overestimated via omission of interaction contexts". Return is a first-rung behaviour that constitutes the regulars and does not by itself distinguish love from proximity. None of this is a reason not to show the relational layer. It is the reason to label it as what it is — *who was there together* — and to let the people in it say what it meant, which is section 5's job and section 12's test.

## 8 · Traces of behaviour, and whom they describe

The third family reads a place's life from traces left for other purposes: phone connections, check-ins, transactions, geotagged posts, smart-card taps. It is the family most often called "big data", the one Chapter 4 warned describes the platform's users, and the one Chapter 8 priced as a dependency on terms. This section reads the studies that used traces as vitality, what they validated and did not, and the general account of what a trace is.

### The vitality proxy that was not checked against people

The best-known study is De Nadai and colleagues' test of Jacobs's conditions in six Italian cities. Its dependent variable: "we computed a district's activity density as the average number of Internet connections throughout a typical business day, divided by the district's area", because, "As it has been extensively done in previous work, we used mobile Internet activity as a proxy for urban vitality." Its data came from one operator, "Telecom Italia Mobile, which is the largest mobile operator in Italy with 34% of the entire market share", "aggregated every 60 minutes", "from February to October 2014". Its model was "an Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) model", and its result is the sentence everyone quotes: "as much as 77% of the variability of district activity is explained by simple structural and static features." The authors' own caveats are that "Our measure of activity has been assumed to be a good proxy for city's vitality", that it omits "temporal dynamics of district activity" such as "differences between day and night", and that with individual rather than aggregate records "one could well extract additional factors". What the paper does not contain, on a direct check, is any sentence on whether one operator's customers represent the population, or any validation of phone activity against anything a person on the street would call vitality. Seventy-seven per cent of the variance of an assumed proxy, blind to the night.

The one trace study here that went back to residents did so carefully and said what it found. The Livehoods project clustered "approximately 18 million check-ins" by who checked in where; in its study city, "42787 check-ins of 3840 users at 5349 venues". Validation: "We take a qualitative approach to validating these clusters, interviewing 27 residents" who "must have lived in their neighborhood for at least one year", asked "to draw the boundaries of their neighborhood" before being shown the clusters. The limitations are the authors': "by aggregating the behaviors of many people, the algorithm itself may be prone towards a 'majority' bias consequently misrepresenting or hiding behaviors in the minority"; the data "are therefore biased towards the types of places that people typically want to publicly share"; "The demographic of foursquare users, which is usually characterized as young professionals in the ages between 25 and 35, owners of smartphones and urban residents can also influence our results"; "Tuning the clusters is non-trivial and may lead to experimenter bias which joins the possible 'confirmation bias' of the interviewees"; and one large low-income area "lacks any representation in our mapping", a "possible depiction of the digital divide". A majority bias, a sharing bias, a demographic bias, an experimenter bias, a confirmation bias and a hole where one of the city's poorest districts was — named by the people who built it, in 2012.

The third, in London, validated one trace against another and reported the result as it was. Sulis and colleagues built "diversity" from "approximately one month of transaction records (collected between January and February 2014) of the Oyster card" and used "Twitter data as a proxy for human activity in urban space", obtaining adjusted R² of 0.843 and 0.831; then checked the vitality metric, in what they call a preliminary analysis, against a count of mapped points of interest and wrote: "The results obtained so far appear incongruous (r = 0.43, while p < .001)." Their limitations: "The main limitation of the results obtained in this work is related to the representativeness of the data", "particularly in relation to Twitter data and the narrow population sample represented"; and "geo-tagged tweets represent approximately 1% of the total amount of tweets sent by users." (A fourth study, of bank-card spending across "around 4.5 M" customers and "178 M transactions", normalised by the bank's market share and, on a direct check, said nothing about spending that never reaches a card or about who holds the bank's cards.)

### Who is in the trace

The representativeness that these studies name in passing has been measured. In the United States, core urban counties have "3.5 times more Twitter users per capita than entirely rural counties", "24.4 times the number of Foursquare users per capita" and "23.1 times the number of check-ins per capita", and Foursquare "has fundamentally failed to appeal to rural populations"; "Foursquare affords the study of urban mobility patterns, not mobility patterns in general". Among 144 million geotagged posts, the question of whether geotag users track population was answered "strongly in the negative": "controlling for other factors, population has no effect on the number of geotag users", which were predicted instead by "higher median income, being in an urban area, being further east or on a coast, having more young people, and having high Asian, Black or Hispanic/Latino populations". The trace is a map of who posts with location on, and that is a map of income, age and urbanity before it is a map of anything else.

### What a trace is

Matthew Salganik's open textbook gives the general account, in ten characteristics, each verified on its own page. Traces are *big* — "Large datasets are a means to an end; they are not an end in themselves", and "While bigness does reduce the need to worry about random error, it actually increases the need to worry about systematic errors"; *always-on* — "Always-on big data enables the study of unexpected events and real-time measurement"; *nonreactive* — "Measurement in big data sources is much less likely to change behavior"; *incomplete* — "No matter how big your big data, it probably doesn't have the information you want", and in particular "the problems of construct validity are even greater when working with data that were not created for the purposes of research"; *inaccessible*; *nonrepresentative* — "Nonrepresentative data are bad for out-of-sample generalizations, but can be quite useful for within-sample comparisons"; *drifting* — "Population drift, usage drift, and system drift make it hard to use big data sources to study long-term trends"; *algorithmically confounded* — "Behavior in big data systems is not natural; it is driven by the engineering goals of the systems"; *dirty*; and *sensitive*. The two that bear hardest on reading a felt quality from behaviour are *incomplete* and *algorithmically confounded*: the trace does not contain the construct, and the behaviour in it was shaped by the platform that recorded it.

Xiao-Li Meng's result, which Chapter 4 cited, could not be re-verified at its source this sprint (every route was closed); the nearest verified text is a later paper he co-authored, which states it as "This is the Big Data Paradox: 'the bigger the data, the surer we fool ourselves' when we fail to account for bias in data collection" (secondary) and shows "how a survey of 250,000 respondents can produce an estimate of the population mean that is no more accurate than an estimate from a simple random sample of size 10". The Google Flu lesson is the same in a sentence: "Large errors in flu prediction were largely avoidable, which offers lessons for the use of big data" (†), and in a textbook's gloss, the model "with all its data, machine learning, and powerful computing did not dramatically outperform a simple and easier-to-understand heuristic".

### What the trace family can claim

A trace is evidence of what the platform recorded, for the people the platform has. Within that population it supports comparison over time and across places, with Chapter 7's cautions; it is a candidate *indicator* of a descriptor until someone has shown that it predicts one, and in this literature almost no one has, so it is labelled unvalidated; it costs what Chapter 8 said a live or platform source costs; and its demographic skew is measured and large — towards the urban, the better-off and the young, with other differences besides. The right label is Salganik's: within-sample, useful; out-of-sample, not. Section 5's systematic review already gave the last word on the gap it would have to cross — perceived and measured place "are related but distinct constructs" — and a dashboard that shows a trace as a vibe has shown the platform's users as the place.

## 9 · Signals that are not feelings

The fourth family infers feeling from a signal: the words people post, the images of a street, the sound of a space, the songs people play. It is the family with the most impressive demonstrations and the clearest ceiling, and the ceiling has been stated by the field's own founders, confirmed by its largest tests, and, in one domain, written into law.

### Text: what the founders said it measures

The hedonometer is the founding instrument of place-scale sentiment, built from "over 46 billion words contained in nearly 4.6 billion" expressions by "over 63 million unique users", with a word list "chosen solely by frequency of usage" — "over 10,000 individual words", rated "on a nine point integer scale, obtaining 50 independent evaluations per word" by paid online raters. Its authors wrote its limits into the paper. "First, as with any sentiment analysis technique, our instrument is fallible for smaller texts" — at "the scale of a typical sentence, where ambiguity may render even human readers unable to judge meaning or tone". "Second, we are also effectively extracting a happiness level as perceived by a generic reader who sees only word frequency." "Third, we quantify only how people appear to others; as should be obvious, our method cannot divine the internal emotional states of specific individuals or populations." And on who posts: "While the demographic profile of individual Twitter users does not match that of, say, the United States".

Applied to places, the same team's geography of happiness used "over 10 million geotagged tweets gathered from 373 urban areas", with the caveats that "Roughly 15% of online adults regularly use Twitter", that "we collected only around 10% of all tweets", that the data is "a non-uniform subsample of statements made by a non-representative portion" of the population, and that "with this method we make no attempt to take the context of words or the meaning of a text into" account. Its convergent test against a survey: "The overall Spearman correlation between the rankings using Gallup's well-being index and our measure is" 0.328 (preprint figures; the published page renders them as images) — a weak-to-moderate rank correlation, at city level.

The strongest test since is Jaidka and colleagues' 2020 comparison of "1.53 billion geotagged English tweets" with "1.73 million phone surveys" across "1,208 US counties" (reconstructed abstract; the full text was closed by every route). "We find that word-level methods (e.g., Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count [LIWC] 2015 and Language Assessment by Mechanical Turk [LabMT]) yielded inconsistent county-level well-being measurements due to regional, cultural, and socioeconomic differences in language use." "Data-driven methods provided robust estimates, approximating the Gallup data at up to r = 0.64." "Regional well-being estimation from social media data seems to be robust when supervised data-driven methods are used." The reading — the chapter's, from an abstract — is that the dictionary methods, the ones that could be applied anywhere without a survey, were inconsistent, and the methods that worked were supervised: fitted, that is, to survey data of the kind they would stand in for. A text measure of place feeling works best once it has been trained against the thing it would replace.

And a place-level language signal can flip sign when the outcome changes. A 2015 study found that "A cross-sectional regression model based only on Twitter language predicted AHD mortality significantly better than did a model that combined 10 common demographic, socioeconomic, and health risk factors" (†); a 2018 re-analysis of the same files found the associations "are reversed when suicide is used as the outcome variable" and concluded "there is no good evidence that analyzing Twitter data in bulk in this way can add anything useful to our ability to understand geographical variation in AHD mortality rates". Predictive fit to one criterion is not validity for another — which is the Standards' point in section 12, demonstrated.

### Who posts, and whom the tools understand

The skew is measured twice over: in who is in the corpus, and in how well the tools read them. On the corpus: "The 10% of users who are most active in terms of tweeting are responsible for 80% of all tweets created by U.S. users"; "The median user tweets just twice each month"; users "are younger, more likely to identify as Democrats, more highly educated and have higher incomes than U.S. adults overall"; the platform population is "a highly non-uniform sample of the population" (†). In the UK, as method context only, the platform "remains the largest microblogging service, visited by 39% of UK adults in May 2025" with monthly visitors down "13% year-on-year". On the tools: Blodgett and colleagues found that of tweets aligned with African-American English, "13.2% were classified by langid.py as non-English" against "7.6% of white-aligned tweets", and that a standard parser's labelled recall was "80.4%" on white-aligned text and "64.0%" on the other. The measuring device is less accurate for some speakers, so a text-based place measure inherits unequal error by who lives there. Olteanu and colleagues give the vocabulary: *population biases*, "Systematic distortions in demographics or other user characteristics between a population of users represented in a dataset or on a platform and some target population"; *behavioral biases*, "Systematic distortions in user behavior across platforms or contexts"; *content production biases*, "lexical, syntactic, semantic, and structural differences in the content generated by users"; *functional biases*, "a result of platform-specific mechanisms or affordances"; *normative biases*, "a result of written or unwritten norms and expectations of acceptable patterns of behavior".

### Images: whose perception

Crowdsourced perception of street images is the method behind "lively" and "safe" scores for cities, and its first paper recorded who the crowd was. Place Pulse 1.0 showed "4,136 geo-tagged images from four cities" in pairs — "Which place looks safer?", "Which place looks more upper-class?", "Which place looks more unique?" — to "7,872 unique participants from 91 countries", who "contributed a total of 208,738 votes"; "76.0% identified themselves as male and 21.1% as female"; "The median self-reported age was 28 years"; and the images lack "other sensory channels that can affect perception, such as sound and smell". Its successors trained models to predict the crowd: Streetscore "predicts the perceived safety of a streetscape" from "more than 7000 participants", scoring "~1 million images"; Place Pulse 2.0 collected "1,170,000 pairwise comparisons provided by 81,630 online volunteers along six perceptual attributes" — "safe, lively, boring, wealthy, depressing, and beautiful" — "to predict human judgments of pairwise image comparisons". The claim is exact and should be kept exact: what is predicted is the judgement of online raters — in the first dataset, three-quarters men, from 91 countries — looking at a photograph without sound. A 2025 study with "1,000 participants with balanced demographics from five countries and 45 nationalities" found that existing approaches "combine responses from multi-cultural populations, obscuring demographic differences and risking amplifying biases", and that "Machine learning models trained on existing global datasets tend to overestimate positive indicators and underestimate negative ones compared to human responses" (†). "Lively", read off a street photograph by strangers, is an indicator of what strangers think a street looks like.

### Faces: the review that became a law

The general lesson of the family is stated most completely for the face, and it transfers. Barrett and four colleagues reviewed the production and perception studies and found "three key shortcomings": limited reliability — "instances of the same emotion category are neither reliably expressed through nor perceived from a common set of facial movements"; lack of specificity — "there is no unique mapping between a configuration of facial movements and instances of an emotion category"; and limited generalizability — "the effects of context and culture have not been sufficiently documented and accounted for". People "do sometimes smile when happy, frown when sad, scowl when angry" more than chance, but how they do so "varies substantially across cultures, situations, and even across people within a single situation", and "a given configuration of facial movements, such as a scowl, often communicates something other than an emotional state". The field's own ethics sheet says the same of every modality: "AER systems should not claim to determine one's emotional state from their utterance, facial expression, gait, etc."; "At best, AER systems capture what one is trying to convey or what is perceived by the listener/viewer"; "Different people express the same emotion differently; the same text may convey different emotions to different people"; "Do not use AER for fully automated decision making".

The European Union's reasons for a prohibition name the same three shortcomings, in the same order — the recital cites no source, and the match is the chapter's reading. Recital 44 of the AI Act: "There are serious concerns about the scientific basis of AI systems aiming to identify or infer emotions, particularly as expression of emotions vary considerably across cultures and situations, and even within a single individual"; "Among the key shortcomings of such systems are the limited reliability, the lack of specificity and the limited generalisability". Article 5(1)(f) prohibits "the use of AI systems to infer emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions, except where the use of the AI system is intended to be put in place or into the market for medical or safety reasons". The definition is tied to "biometric data", so the prohibition does not on its face reach text sentiment, and the Act has been amended since 2024 with further Article 5 points applying from 2 December 2026; the boundary is stated here as written. A psychometric finding became a legal reason. A studio in the UK is not bound by it, and should know that the best-evidenced position on inferring feeling from signal is now the law of its nearest market.

### Sound and songs

Two signal sources sit closer to the perceived register than text or faces, and both have a measured ceiling. The soundscape review reports Axelsson and colleagues' suggestion that "perceived sound sources are more important as indicators of perceived affective quality than sound levels", finds that "Eventfulness is positively related to sounds of people", and, in a cited study, that models on perceptual variables "explained up to 52% of the variance" where the model on acoustic measurements "explained 21% of the variance". A decibel meter predicts the felt quality poorly; what people hear, and who is making the sound, predicts it better. The lockdown study — "a database of soundscape questionnaires (N = 1,136) and binaural recordings (N = 687)" at "13 locations across London and Venice" in 2019, with "new recordings (N = 571)" in 2020 — is the clearest case of predicting a felt measure from recordings when nobody could be asked, reaching R² of 0.85 and 0.715; and its abstract adds that "contextual information is important for predicting pleasantness but not for eventfulness". The recording can stand in for the answer only where the location's context is already known from having asked.

Song features are the signal a nightlife dashboard would most want and can least rely on. The platform's reference page defines *valence* as "A measure from 0.0 to 1.0 describing the musical positiveness conveyed by a track" and *energy* as "a perceptual measure of intensity and activity", and, on a direct check, does not say how either is computed or validated: the definition is published and the derivation is not. Chapter 8 found that the platform withdrew these endpoints for new applications on 27 November 2024. The widely cited study of "765 million online music plays streamed by 1 million individuals in 51 countries" does not use the word "valence" on its open page, and distinguishes its own measure — what people chose to play — from earlier self-report and text methods, which "track the expression of affect, not affective preference for external stimuli such as music"; a finance paper offers "the positivity of songs that individuals choose to listen to" as "a real-time, continuous measure of national sentiment" and validates it "by correlating it with mood swings induced by seasonal factors, weather conditions, and COVID-related restrictions" — against other proxies, not against anyone's report. A proprietary feature of what was played is, at most, an indicator of a mood someone would have to be asked about, supplied by a dependency on terms that has already closed its door.

### What the signal family can claim

A signal is evidence of the signal. Text sentiment measures "how people appear to others" to a generic reader, tracks surveys weakly unless trained on them, and misreads some speakers more than others. Image perception measures the judgements of online raters who were not there. Facial inference has three documented shortcomings that are now a legal reason. Sound predicts the felt quality better through what is heard than through how loud it is, and song features are defined, not derived, and no longer supplied. Each can be an *indicator* of a descriptor once it has been shown to predict one in the place and population concerned, and the showing is the work. None is a descriptor. The founders of the strongest of them said so first.

## 10 · Asking the person at the moment

One method sits beside the four families rather than in any of them, because it asks the person how they feel, where they are, now — and differs from section 3's in-situ questionnaire in prompting people at random moments across their days and places rather than approaching those present at a chosen location. Experience sampling has a validation history, a geolocated form built in the UK, an official judgement on its standing, and costs that explain why it is rare.

The founding paper describes "The Experience-Sampling Method (ESM)" as "an attempt to provide a valid instrument to describe variations in self-reports of mental processes", presenting "evidence for its short- and long-term reliability" and "evidence for validity by showing correlation between ESM measures on the one hand and physiological measures, one-time psychological tests, and behavioral indices on the other" (reconstructed abstract). Its cheaper cousin, the Day Reconstruction Method, combines "features of time-budget measurement and experience sampling": "Participants systematically reconstruct their activities and experiences of the preceding day with procedures designed to reduce recall biases", validated by "close correspondences between the DRM reports of 909 employed women and established results from experience sampling" (†). The distinction underneath both is the US National Academies': *experienced* wellbeing "is concerned with people's emotional states and may also include effects associated with sensations (e.g., pain, arousal)", where *evaluative* wellbeing "refers to judgments of how satisfying one's life is". A vibe, felt in a room, is an experienced quantity; a rating of a place given later, elsewhere, is an evaluative one, and the two are not the same measure of the same thing.

The geolocated form exists and is British. Mappiness is "a smartphone app that signals participants at random moments, presenting a brief questionnaire while using satellite positioning (GPS)", which collected "over one million responses from more than 20,000 participants" and, modelling "using only the within-individual variation", found participants "significantly and substantially happier outdoors in all green or natural habitat types than they are in urban environments" (†). A follow-up joined it to crowdsourced ratings of "the 'scenicness' of photographs taken across England", with "138,407" measurements from "15,444" users against "over 1.5 million ratings", finding "individuals are happier in more scenic locations" by "2.770 points on the 0–100 happiness scale" between the least and most scenic — and stating its sample limit in five words: "Mappiness users are all Apple iOS users". A King's College tool, Urban Mind, "monitor[ed] 108 individuals who completed 3013 assessments over a 1-week period" and said of them that the sample "comprised smartphone users with higher-than-average levels of education and an average age of just 31.1 years" and "therefore cannot be considered representative".

The method's standing is stated by the National Academies panel: "Momentary assessment methods are often regarded as the gold standard for capturing experiential states", and "these methods have not typically been practical for general population surveys because they involve highly intensive methods that are difficult to scale up to the level of nationally representative surveys"; its recommendation is that experienced-wellbeing measurement "should, at this point, still be pursued in experimental survey modules" and "only be considered for inclusion in flagship surveys on a piloted basis". The reference method is not yet official-statistics material, by the panel's account, because it does not scale to representative surveys.

For a dashboard the lesson is double. Experience sampling by place is the method that most nearly measures a vibe as a descriptor — the person, the moment, the location, the spread across people and times — and the people who have done it describe samples that are young, educated, iPhone-owning and self-selected, with within-person designs to make the comparisons fair. It is also the method whose every reading is a live source: a dependency with terms, a chain, a cost in attention for the respondent (Chapter 8's objective applied to a person rather than a server). What it gives, when it is done, is the standard that every indicator in sections 7 to 9 would have to be validated against.

## 11 · The official precedent: how a felt measure was built

The UK has built one felt measure to the standard this chapter is looking for, and it took a national statistics office, an expert advisory structure, a public consultation and an extensive testing programme to do it. The personal wellbeing measures are the yardstick for what a vibe measure would owe.

### The four questions and how they were made

The preamble: "Next, I would like to ask you four questions about your feelings on aspects of your life." "There are no right or wrong answers", on a scale "where 0 is 'not at all' and 10 is 'completely'". Then, each stem completed by its own anchors before the question mark ("where 0 is 'not at all satisfied' and 10 is 'completely satisfied'", and likewise for the others): "Overall, how satisfied are you with your life nowadays"; "Overall, to what extent do you feel that the things you do in your life are worthwhile"; "Overall, how happy did you feel yesterday"; and "On a scale where 0 is 'not at all anxious' and 10 is 'completely anxious', overall, how anxious did you feel yesterday?" One evaluative question, one eudemonic and two about yesterday's experience; the structure came from a commissioned recommendation that distinguished "three broad types of SWB measure: 1) evaluation (global assessments); 2) experience (feelings over short periods of time); and 3) 'eudemonic' (reports of purpose and meaning, and worthwhile things in life)", measured separately, for "three uses": "1) monitoring progress; 2) informing policy design; and 3) policy appraisal".

How they were tested: the office "sought advice from experts working in the field of subjective well-being and consulted with specialists" on its advisory groups, and, "Based on this, as well as an extensive programme of question testing, four questions were designed", "drawing on three main theoretical approaches". "Before April 2011, we carried out small-scale cognitive testing of our four personal well-being questions", and "Cognitive testing was undertaken to understand how respondents chose their score on the 11-point scale". The office states what moves the answers: "Responses to well-being questions can be determined in part by the context of the interview"; the questions are placed "directly after the core questions on household and individual demographics"; and "people respond more positively to the personal well-being questions when interviewed by telephone rather than face-to-face". The OECD's guidelines, against which this was built, say why such care is needed: "subjective well-being measures are strongly affected by question structure and context, and the results from differently worded questions (or even a different ordering of similar questions) are likely to affect comparability"; "On balance, the evidence broadly seems to favour an 11-point numerical scale"; "Place important subjective well-being questions near the start of the survey"; "Avoid placing the subjective well-being questions immediately after questions likely to elicit a strong emotional response"; prior questions on difficult events "could set the affective 'tone' for the questions that follow"; and on mode, "CAPI with show cards should be considered best practice" while "CATI is viewed as the least reliable way to collect consistent subjective well-being data". (The 2013 guidelines have a 2025 update with "a shortened core module of three priority subjective well-being measures" (†); anything citing "the OECD core module" should say which edition.)

### How they are reported, and why

The office reports the measures "as both average means and thresholds", where "Thresholds present the proportion responding in defined response categories": for the first three questions, low 0–4, medium 5–6, high 7–8, very high 9–10; for anxiety, very low 0–1, low 2–3, medium 4–5, high 6–10. The reason is given in its frequently asked questions: "the average does not show the proportion of people who gave each response on the 0 to 10 scale"; "The average cannot shed light on whether responses are evenly distributed along the 0 to 10 scale, or the proportions of people rating their well-being at the highest and lowest levels"; "For this reason, relying only on average ratings might mask important differences in how people rate their well-being." The cut-points are grounded two ways: "qualitative testing has shown the mid-point of the scale (5) acts as an important cut-off for respondents for assessing their own well-being, with responses below five seen largely as a negative response", and "policy-makers are particularly interested in people reporting the highest or lowest levels". The purpose, in the quality report, is "a summary measure of well-being through the mean and an indication of inequality through the dispersion of the results". This is section 3's finding — a felt measure is a distribution — adopted as official practice, with the bands chosen by asking respondents where the scale turns negative for them.

### The rules for small places

The official rules for publishing a felt measure for a small area are exact, and they are Chapter 6's small-number discipline applied to feeling. "Estimates of personal well-being are not published if the sample size they are based on is fewer than 50, or their numerator is less than 5." Precision is graded by the coefficient of variation: "If the CV is less than or equal to 5%, the estimate is considered to be precise", reasonably precise to 10, acceptable to 20, and "If the CV is greater than 20% or unavailable, the estimate is considered to be unreliable". "Personal well-being questions cannot be answered by proxy." And on comparison: "The most appropriate comparisons are progress over time within the same local authority, or across local authorities that share a similar demographic composition" — Chapter 7's own-history and published-peers rules, in the office's words, for a felt measure. The annual estimates rest on "approximately 90,000 individuals". A felt measure for a district, a street or a scene has to say how many answered, and if it is under fifty it is not published.

### Built with the public, and revised by asking again

The programme began by asking. The national debate on what matters "generated over 34,000 responses, which were used to develop the national well-being framework that the ONS has been reporting on ever since." A 2022–23 review drew "A total of 118 responses" online "and an additional two" by email, with "focus groups to collect feedback from socio-demographic groups more likely to report low well-being", after which "22 new measures were added, one was replaced, and six existing measures were removed"; the office's user guide adds that "We also commissioned targeted qualitative research with under-represented groups" and that the framework grew "from 44 to 60 measures". The precedent is complete: a felt measure built by asking the public what matters; tested cognitively for how people choose a score; placed and administered with known context and mode effects; reported as bands as well as means for a stated reason; suppressed below fifty; compared within its own history or against declared peers; and revised by asking again, with the groups the first round under-heard deliberately sought. The local translation exists too: a six-month scoping project with "26 different organisations, including nine city councils, seven county or district councils, the three devolved governments" produced "26 indicators of individual wellbeing and its determinants" as the ideal set and "23 indicators" currently available. Nothing in the precedent is beyond a studio in principle. All of it is work, and the chapter's next section says which parts of the work are the validation.

## 12 · Validating a felt measure

The brief's own answer to validation is recognition: whether the people who make a scene recognise themselves in the measure. Chapter 5 found that this splits into legitimacy and validity. This section says, from the measurement standards, what validity is, what recognition supplies of it, and what else is owed.

### What validity is

The governing standard — now open to read — puts it in one sentence: "Validity refers to the degree to which evidence and theory support the interpretations of test scores for proposed uses of tests." Three consequences follow in the next lines. "It is the interpretations of test scores for proposed uses that are evaluated, not the test itself" — a vibe question is not valid or invalid; a reading of its answers for a purpose is. "Validity is a unitary concept": "It is the degree to which all the accumulated evidence supports the intended interpretation of test scores for the proposed use", and the five named kinds of evidence — "Evidence Based on Test Content"; "Evidence Based on Response Processes"; "Evidence Based on Internal Structure"; "Evidence Based on Relations to Other Variables"; "Evidence for Validity and Consequences of Testing" — "may illuminate different aspects of validity, but they do not represent distinct types of validity". And the root text underneath, Cronbach and Meehl in 1955: "Construct validation is involved whenever a test is to be interpreted as a measure of some attribute or quality which is not 'operationally defined'", and "Construct validity must be investigated whenever no criterion or universe of content is accepted as entirely adequate to define the quality to be measured". A vibe has no criterion and no accepted universe of content. Its measure is validated the hard way or not at all.

### What building one involves

The scale-development primers agree on the steps and the numbers. The current edition of the standard textbook lists nine: "Determine Clearly What It Is You Want to Measure"; "Generate an Item Pool"; "Determine the Format for Measurement"; "Have Initial Item Pool Reviewed by Experts"; "Cognitive Interviewing"; "Consider Inclusion of Validation Items"; "Administer Items to a Development Sample"; "Evaluate the Items"; "Optimize Scale Length" — cognitive interviewing promoted, in the latest edition, to a step of its own. The open primer gives "three phases that span nine steps" — domain and items; content validity; pre-testing; administration and sample size; item reduction; factor extraction; dimensionality; reliability; validity — with its conventions: "an ideal ratio of respondents to items is 10:1"; samples of "100 = poor, 200 = fair, 300 = good, 500 = very good"; "A reliability of 0.90 is the minimum recommended threshold" though "a reliability coefficient of 0.70 has often been accepted as satisfactory"; pre-testing by "A range of 5–15 interviews in two to three rounds, or until saturation". Cognitive interviewing is, in Beatty and Willis's definition (†), "the administration of draft survey questions while collecting additional verbal information about the survey responses, which is used to evaluate the quality of the response or to help determine whether the question is generating the information that its author intends" — the method that produces response-process evidence, by think-aloud and verbal probing. Test–retest reliability two weeks apart has been measured in one small sample at "r=0.59" for single-item life satisfaction and "0.64" for a diary-based net-affect measure, "probably sufficiently high to support much of the research" where "group means are compared" (†); the open textbook's rule of thumb is that "a test-retest correlation of +.80 or greater is considered to indicate good reliability".

One design decision the atmosphere literature forces is whether the measure is *reflective* or *formative*: whether the items are symptoms of a single underlying quality (and so should correlate, and be averaged) or ingredients that together build it (and need not correlate, and are summed into an index). The methods literature complains that "few studies use formative indicator measurement models, even though they should" (†), that measurement guidance "is replete with advice regarding the development and validation of multi-item scales based on reflective measures" while "the issue of index construction using formative measures has received little attention" (†), and that misspecification biases the results. A made-register measure, if one is attempted — lighting, sound, welcome, programme — is formative: the parts build the atmosphere and need not agree. A perceived-register measure whose items are meant to reflect one quality — pleasant and annoying reversed, say — is reflective: the ratings are supposed to hang together. The two are validated differently, and Chapter 5's recipe card should say which the measure is.

### What recognition is, in these terms

"The scene recognises itself" is, in the testing Standards' vocabulary, two kinds of evidence and one kind of legitimacy. It is *content* evidence — the people described agree that the items cover what matters — and *response-process* evidence — they understood the question as its author intended, which cognitive interviewing tests directly. And it is legitimacy, in Chapter 5's sense: they, not the studio, chose what counts. It is not, on its own, *internal-structure* evidence, *relations-to-other-variables* evidence or *consequences* evidence, and the textbook position on what it is worth alone is blunt: "Face validity is at best a very weak kind of evidence that a measurement method is measuring what it is supposed to", because "it is based on people's intuitions about human behaviour, which are frequently wrong"; the Standards' five sources do not include it by name. The chapter's own extension, marked as such, is that when the people described judge the items and explain their readings, the result is content and response-process evidence and not face validity alone. The one paper that defends face validity defends it as a measurable property — "face validity is a characteristic of tests that can be validly and reliably measured" (reconstructed) — which is the right reading for this chapter: recognition can be measured properly, by asking the described whether the items fit and how they read them, and the result is evidence of two kinds and a claim to legitimacy. It is necessary; it is not sufficient; and it should be gathered as evidence rather than announced as approval.

The qualitative tradition's version is *member checking*, and its own literature is careful about it. "Member checking, also known as participant or respondent validation, is a technique for exploring the credibility of results"; "Data or results are returned to participants to check for accuracy and resonance with their experiences"; it "is often mentioned as one in a list of validation techniques", and "This simplistic reporting might not acknowledge the value of using the method, nor its juxtaposition with the interpretative stance of qualitative research" (†). The foundational text, as a methods guide reports it, held it "the most crucial technique for establishing credibility" (secondary; check against Lincoln and Guba before quoting as theirs); the same guide's criticisms are that it "relies on the assumption that there is a fixed truth of reality that can be accounted for by a researcher and confirmed by a respondent", and that "Members may tell stories during an interview that they later regret or see differently". "little has been published about the effects the member checking process may have on participants" (†) — the check acts on the checked. Tracy's eight criteria for qualitative quality name *resonance* and *credibility* among "(a) worthy topic, (b) rich rigor, (c) sincerity, (d) credibility, (e) resonance, (f) significant contribution, (g) ethics, and (h) meaningful coherence" (†). Recognition, done as member checking, is a procedure with a literature, not a vote.

### What else is owed

The other three sources are the ones a studio is least likely to gather and most likely to be asked for by Chapter 2's sceptical reader. *Internal structure*: do the items hang together as the construct says they should (reflective), or build what they are said to build (formative)? *Relations to other variables*: does the felt measure move with what it should (a known-groups test: places the scene itself calls different should score differently; a convergent test: another method's reading of the same places) and not with what it should not? Section 5's finding that perceived and measured place are "related but distinct constructs" is a reason to expect modest convergence and to say so, not a reason to skip the test. *Consequences*: what the measure does once published — Chapter 5's Goodhart section and section 14's extraction finding — gathered as evidence after launch. The measure record in section 15 carries the five across its lines — content in lines 3 and 10, response process in line 7, internal structure in line 8, relations in line 9, consequences in line 12 — and the truthful early state of most of them is "not yet".

## 13 · When the felt and the counted disagree

A dashboard that carries a felt layer beside counted ones will find them disagreeing, and the doctrine for what to do then exists. Mixed-methods research names the assessment "fit": "The fit of integration describes the extent the qualitative and quantitative findings cohere", and "The assessment of fit of integration leads to three possible outcomes." "Confirmation occurs when the findings from both types of data confirm the results of the other", and "As the two data sources provide similar conclusions, the results have greater credibility". "Expansion occurs when the findings from the two sources of data diverge and expand insights" by addressing different aspects of one phenomenon. "Discordance occurs if the qualitative and quantitative findings are inconsistent, incongruous, contradict, conflict, or disagree with each other." And what to do about the third: "Options for reporting the findings include looking for potential sources of bias, and examining methodological assumptions and procedures"; "Investigators may handle discordant results in different ways such as gathering additional data, re-analyzing existing databases to resolve differences, seeking explanations from theory, or challenging the validity of the constructs." The last option is the one a dashboard is most tempted to skip: the disagreement may mean the counted measure is not measuring what the felt one is.

A second framework uses four cells rather than three. The triangulation protocol produces "a 'convergence coding matrix' to display findings emerging from each component of a study on the same page", with "consideration of where there is agreement, partial agreement, silence, or dissonance between findings from different components"; "This technique for triangulation is the only one to include silence—where a theme or finding arises from one data set and not another", and "Silence might be expected because of the strengths of different methods to examine different aspects of a phenomenon." Silence is the cell Chapter 4's six kinds of nothing did not have: not a missing value but a finding one method can see and another cannot. A room that the counted layer records as nothing and the felt layer shows as the centre of a scene is not an anomaly in the data. It is a silence in one method, and the matrix has a cell for it.

Version 1 built a rule on this — when the felt and the counted disagree, that is a finding, shown on one screen — and illustrated it with a case from the studio's own research. The chapter keeps the rule and drops the case, because the case is a finding about a place and this book does not make those. What it keeps as method is the shape: the economically marginal and culturally load-bearing room is the discordant cell, and a dashboard pre-commits, before it is built, to reporting that cell rather than resolving it by preferring the number. Chapter 7's verbs apply to the disagreement too: the layers *disagree*; the dashboard does not say which is wrong until someone has looked for the bias, re-examined the assumptions, or challenged the construct. How large a disagreement counts is a question of the felt measure's interval at its sample size — Chapter 6's small-number rules — which is why the matrix has a cell for partial agreement and why a felt result at the floor is shown with its interval.

## 14 · The studio's own positions, tested

The symmetry test, as Chapter 0 asks: the standards this chapter has applied to others' methods, turned on the studio's own positions in version 1, the Decision Book, the brief and the house framework (confidential; cited as in Chapter 1, never reproduced).

**Three registers, never collapsed.** Every literature read supports it. The perceived register has a standard; the made register has a qualitative protocol; the excess register has testimony and a warning. Nobody in any of them proposes a single number for the three together. *Strengthened.* The chapter adds the vocabulary: descriptor, description with makers named, testimony.

**The human dot plot as a collection instrument.** The teaching literature's people-as-data display shows a shape that is not in the population; the facilitation literature describes the spectrum line as a conversation in which positions are public, explained aloud and expected to move; the social-influence experiments show that visible estimates narrow without improving; a single mark on a line is a single item of a non-concrete construct. As a workshop form it is well evidenced as a conversation-opener. As a measure it is a visible, single-item, self-selected self-placement, and each adjective is a known weakness. *Weakened as a measure; kept as a form.* If the prototype uses it, the collection is private before it is public, the result is a spread with its sample stated, and it is one item among several `[Prototype flag]`.

**"What this changed" as a mandatory field.** Version 1 specified it from the community's design principle with no external precedent. Section 6 found it in Arnstein's missing rung, Fung's third dimension, the UK consultation principles, Chambers's second condition, the co-production boundary and the CARE responsibility, with one large behavioural study in its favour and two randomised experiments showing that a *tailored* report-back raises later response where a generic one does not — in one of them, worse than none. *Strengthened; now grounded.* The form remains the designers'.

**The co-production rule — authorship, consent and control, return — or it does not publish.** The definitional literature agrees that asking is not co-producing; the participation literature says consultation without consequence is "a sham"; the governance principles make sharing how data was used a responsibility. *Strengthened.* The chapter adds the selection half the rule lacked: authorship names who spoke and who did not; the spread is shown; the one per cent who contribute most are not presented as the scene.

**Discordance is a finding.** Mixed-methods doctrine names it and lists what to do; the triangulation protocol adds silence. *Strengthened*, with the case removed and the method kept.

**"Validation by recognition."** Chapter 5 already split it into legitimacy and validity. This chapter places it: content and response-process evidence from the described, plus legitimacy; "at best a very weak kind of evidence" if it is the only kind; measurable if gathered as member checking rather than announced. *Qualified, and made operable.* The brief's phrase "whether the people who make a scene recognise themselves in it" is the first validation and not the last.

**"Measure the vibe instead of feeling of safety."** The contrast case turned out to be a warning: the official single felt item was shown by split-wording tests inside the official survey to manufacture part of what it reported. A vibe item asked without cognitive testing, context control and a known mode would be no better placed. *Qualified*: the replacement must be built to the standard the original failed.

**The extraction finding.** The house framework's workshops named relationships with institutions that can feel extractive (cited as in Chapter 1; not reproduced). Chambers names "the mix and balance of extraction and empowerment" as the open question of participatory numbers; the CARE principles make the return a responsibility; the consequences source of validity evidence is where a measure's effect on the measured is recorded. *Strengthened*, and handed to Chapter 10 with a line in the record.

**"It fails if its an aggregation of 'the biggest'."** The scenes method, the vitality proxies and the trace studies all weight by what is listed, counted or connected most, and the trace literature's own authors name the "majority bias" and the hole where one of the poorest districts was. The failure register's sentence is the trace family's documented limitation. *Strengthened* — with one tension the chapter cannot resolve: the official floor of fifty answers, adopted in truth 3, would remove from a felt panel exactly the small rooms the brief says must not disappear. The two positions are both the studio's, and the chapter records the conflict rather than choosing: a room under the floor is shown as present and unmeasured, never as absent, and the floor itself is a question for the end of the book `[Revisit at end]`.

**The design system's chart for a felt measure.** The design system has provenance on every chart and no component for a distribution of felt responses, for bands with their cut-points, for the sample and its selection, or for a discordance matrix. *A gap*, logged in the conflict log.

## 15 · What holds true regardless

Seven truths, each with its grounds; then the record beneath them. They are stated for any felt measure of a place; the nightlife case in section 17 is one application.

**1 · A vibe is named as a construct and a register before it is measured, and the three registers are never collapsed into one number.** Made, perceived and excess have different instruments, units and validation histories; a "vibe score" that blends them is an index with Chapter 5's price list attached and no literature behind it. The measure says which register it is in and whether it is reflective or formative. *(Sections 1, 3, 4, 12; ISO 12913; Michels; Russell; Jarvis et al.; Chapter 5.)*

**2 · A felt measure is a distribution of people's answers, reported with its spread and its sample, never a point.** The standard's analysts compute coordinates per respondent and speak of the percentage likely to perceive; the national measure reports bands because "relying only on average ratings might mask important differences"; the mid-point cut comes from asking respondents where the scale turns and the top band from stated policy interest. A place does not have a vibe score; it has a spread of perceptions, and the spread is the finding. *(Sections 3, 11; Mitchell, Aletta & Kang; ONS.)*

**3 · Who was asked, who answered and who was missing are part of the measure.** Effort is highest among those with most at stake; people mark near home; one per cent contribute most; those who choose to participate are "frequently quite unrepresentative"; a strong togetherness score is compatible with exclusion. The measure states its sample, its recruitment and its known gaps on the face, and is not published below the official floor. *(Sections 4, 5, 11; Brown 2017; Haklay; Fung; Bethlehem; Garcia-Mispireta; ONS QMI.)*

**4 · Participation is evidence of what participants placed, not of the place, until selection is accounted for; and it becomes co-production only when what it changed is shown.** A heat map is a picture of who marked; a visible self-placement measures the room's convergence; consultation without consequence is "a sham"; "what this changed" is a duty in the official consultation principles and a predictor of taking part again. *(Sections 5, 6; Arnstein; Fung; Cabinet Office 2018; Sjoberg et al.; Boyle & Harris; Lorenz et al.)*

**5 · Co-presence is not a tie and a trace is not a feeling; each is read for what it records, and a derived signal is validated against the people before it stands for them.** Co-presence networks are denser than contact and misplace the central; a co-occurrence count is tested against opportunity before it is an edge; a trace describes the platform's users and is "incomplete" of the construct; text sentiment measures "how people appear to others" and tracks surveys only when trained on them; faces have "no unique mapping"; perceived and measured place are "related but distinct constructs". *(Sections 7, 8, 9; Génois & Barrat; Neal et al.; McPherson et al.; Salganik; Dodds et al.; Jaidka et al.; Barrett et al.; Orstad et al.)*

**6 · A new felt measure is validated from five sources of evidence for a stated use, and recognition by the people described supplies two of them and not all.** Validity belongs to an interpretation for a use; it is argued from content, response process, internal structure, relations to other variables and consequences; recognition supplies the first two and legitimacy, is "at best a very weak kind of evidence" alone, and is gathered as member checking with its own cautions. The measure is built by the steps the precedents used — items, expert review, cognitive interviewing, a development sample, reliability, and the known effects of context, order and mode. *(Sections 11, 12; AERA/APA/NCME 2014; Cronbach & Meehl; DeVellis & Thorpe; Boateng et al.; Birt et al.; ONS; OECD.)*

**7 · When the felt and the counted disagree, the disagreement is reported, not resolved by preferring the number.** Discordance is one of three outcomes of integration and has a procedure — look for bias, re-examine assumptions, challenge the constructs; the triangulation protocol's four cells — agreement, partial agreement, silence, dissonance — add silence, where one method sees what another cannot. The dashboard pre-commits to the cell before it is built. *(Section 13; Fetters, Curry & Creswell; O'Cathain, Murphy & Nicholl.)*

### Beneath the measure: the measure record

Chapters 4 to 8 each left a record beneath a dashboard's numbers — the provenance set, the recipe card, the claim record, the comparison record, the service record — and whether they are one document is for the end of the book `[Revisit at end]`. A felt measure needs its own, because none of the five says who was asked how they felt and how the asking was tested. The *measure record* has twelve lines; it is opened before the first person is asked and filled as the work proceeds.

| Line | What it states |
|---|---|
| 1 · Construct and register | What the measure is of, in words; which register (made / perceived / excess); reflective or formative; what it is not |
| 2 · Items and format | The questions as asked, their scale and anchors, the language(s), and where the words came from (standard, adapted, the scene's own) |
| 3 · Who chose what counts | How the items were arrived at and with whom; what was proposed and dropped |
| 4 · Who was asked, how, when, where | Recruitment route; in situ or later; mode; moment; the place as defined to the respondent |
| 5 · Who answered and who did not | n per place and period; response rate where known; the known gaps; the share contributed by the most active |
| 6 · The spread | The distribution and its bands; the cut-points and how they were chosen; the official floor applied |
| 7 · Response-process evidence | Cognitive interviews: how many, in what rounds, what changed |
| 8 · Internal structure and reliability | Do the items hang together (reflective) or build as intended (formative); reliability and test–retest where known |
| 9 · Relations to other variables | Known-groups and convergent tests run; what the measure moved with and did not; the trace or signal indicators checked against it |
| 10 · Recognition | Member checking: who was shown what, when, what they said, what changed; the legitimacy claim stated |
| 11 · Discordance | Where this measure disagrees with the counted layers; the matrix cell; what was done about it |
| 12 · What this changed | What the measure has been used for; what the people in it were told; the consequences evidence gathered since launch |

**A filled example, invented for the method.** *1.* Perceived register; welcome in a room, as felt by people present; reflective (four items expected to agree); not a measure of safety, quality or value. *2.* Three items adapted from a standard's attribute pairs plus one in the scene's own words from workshop 2; 0–10 with labelled ends; English and one other language, back-translated and tested. *3.* Items proposed by the studio, reduced and reworded in two workshops; two proposed items dropped as unanswerable in situ. *4.* Approached in the room, late in the evening, by a recruiter from the scene; phone-based; the place defined as the room and the night. *5.* 112 answers across three nights; approach-to-answer ratio recorded; under-25s and people who left before midnight under-represented; each respondent answered once, so no contributor dominates. *6.* Bands set at the mid-point and the top two points after cognitive interviews; distribution shown; floor of 50 met. *7.* Nine cognitive interviews in two rounds; one item reworded; one dropped. *8.* The four items correlate as expected; test–retest not yet done — *not yet*. *9.* Known-groups: two rooms the scene itself calls different scored differently; convergent test against a counted layer — *not yet*; no trace indicator validated. *10.* Results shown back at a third workshop; two readings challenged and recorded; items retained by participants' choice. *11.* One room discordant with the counted layer — reported in its cell, not resolved. *12.* Cited once in a submission; the people who answered told, at the room, in a notice — *what else it changed: not yet*.

The example is a shape and not a plan; its words, its scale and its rooms are placeholders. Several of its lines say "not yet", and a record whose lines say so is a record a sceptical reader can trust more than one that does not.

## 16 · Where the choice is yours

*Which register to measure first, and whether to measure the third at all.* The perceived register has a standard measure; the made register has the protocol and the makers; the excess register has testimony and a warning about who is missing. A studio may begin with any, and may decide that the third is shown as testimony and never scored. The chapter requires only that the choice be named in line 1.

*Standard words or the scene's own.* The soundscape attributes are tested, translated and comparable, and their weakest axis is the one the brief's word sits on; the scene's own words are legitimate, recognisable and untested. The precedent for a hybrid is destination-image research: structured items beside open questions. Which words, in what mix, is a decision about the scene's voice and the studio's, and this book does not make it.

*Private or public self-placement.* A private mark is a measure; a public one is a conversation. The prototype may want both, in that order, and the design of the room is the designers'.

*How much of the co-produced layer is community-held and not published.* The house framework asked the question; Chapter 10 treats consent at scene level. The measure record's line 5 and line 12 are where the decision is recorded, whichever way it goes.

*Whether to show a trace or signal indicator at all before it has been validated against a descriptor.* Sections 8 and 9 say what each can claim; the studio may show one as *an indicator, unvalidated here* with Chapter 4's provenance, or wait. The chapter requires the label, not the choice.

*The bands.* The national measure's cut-points were set by asking respondents where the scale turns. The studio's bands may be the same, or the scene's own, and either is defensible once line 6 says how they were chosen.

*Who does the member checking, and how often.* A procedure with a literature; its frequency, its form and its facilitation are the studio's to decide with the scene.

## 17 · A nightlife illustration — method only

What follows shows how the method would be applied to a felt layer in a dashboard about where people go at night; it draws no conclusion about any place, names no venue, chooses no words and proposes no design. It is the measure record's twelve questions asked of a hypothetical layer.

A studio wants a panel that shows how rooms feel to the people in them. Before any question is written, the record is opened.

*Line 1.* The studio names the register, and decides — this is one of section 16's choices, made here only to illustrate — that the panel is about the perceived register, that the made register is shown separately with its makers named, and that testimony is shown as testimony. It writes what the panel is not.

*Lines 2 and 3.* The studio has a tested attribute set to hand and the scene's own vocabulary. It decides, with the people who will be asked, which words to use and in what mix, and records what was proposed and dropped. It notes that the standard attribute closest to its word is a derived diagonal, and that the one study of how people use the scales found that axis the least evenly read.

*Line 4.* The studio decides whether to ask in the room, now, or later, elsewhere, and knows these are two different measures (experienced; evaluative). It defines the place to the respondent — the room and the night, in whatever words the designers choose — as the official belonging question defines a neighbourhood in walking minutes. It chooses a mode and knows the mode moves the answers.

*Line 5.* The studio records who approached whom, how many answered, and who it did not reach, and expects the ceiling that Williams and Vaske found among people already at the place. It expects participation to be uneven. It applies the floor: a room with fewer than fifty answers is not shown.

*Line 6.* The studio reports the spread, not the mean; it sets its bands by asking respondents where the scale turns, or adopts the national cut-points and says so.

*Line 7.* Before launch, the studio runs cognitive interviews — a handful, in two rounds — and rewords or drops what people misread.

*Lines 8 and 9.* The studio checks that the items agree with each other as a reflective measure should, and runs the one test it can afford before launch: two rooms the scene itself calls different should score differently. It writes "not yet" against test–retest and against convergence with any counted layer, and it does not show a trace or song-feature indicator as the room's feeling until one has been checked against this record.

*Line 10.* The studio shows the results back to the people who gave them, before publication, as a procedure and not a vote; records what they challenged; and treats their recognition as content and response-process evidence and a claim to legitimacy — necessary, and not the whole of validity.

*Line 11.* Where a room's felt result disagrees with the counted layers, the panel shows the disagreement in its cell and the studio looks for bias, re-examines assumptions and asks whether the counted measure is measuring what the felt one is.

*Line 12.* The studio writes, before the first answer, how the people who answer will be told what their answers changed, and keeps that line current.

The record is published beside the panel. Nothing in it says how any room feels, and nothing in it is a design. It is the method by which a felt layer earns the word *evidence* — and the first thing it says to the people it describes is what it asked, whom, and what it did with the answer.

## What proves a break works

The test for a departure from one of this chapter's rules is a conversation: **show the felt result, with its record, to someone who gave an answer and to someone who was not asked, and listen for three things.** Does the person who answered recognise the question they were asked and see what their answer changed? Does the person who was not asked see, from the face, that they were not — who was, how many, and what that means for the number? And can either of them tell, from the face alone, that the number is a spread and not a verdict? If all three, the break has cost nothing — this is a test of whether the face is legible, not a test of the measure's validity, which the record carries. If the person who answered does not recognise the question, the content and response-process evidence has failed; if the person not asked cannot see the gap, the selection has been hidden; if either reads the number as a verdict on the place, the register or the spread has been collapsed.

For a trace or signal shown as an indicator, the test is the convergent one: the number beside the descriptor it is supposed to predict, in the same place, with the correlation stated. For a public self-placement, it is the private version run first and compared. For a departure from the floor, it is the interval shown and the reason given.

## Design system (Style Guide v1.5) and Decision Book — conflict log

**Agrees.** Provenance on every chart applies to a felt panel as to a counted one, and the measure record is its felt-data extension. DSH-15 and DSH-16 — gaps render as gaps; carry uncertainty through filtering or suppress below a base threshold — are the official floor and the silence cell. DSH-12's "Observed, task-based testing with real users" and the Style Guide's seven tests ("Dancefloor"; "Partner"; "Screen-reader") are the symmetry test of this chapter applied to a surface. BRF-04's three axes — "affected, influential, exposed" — are the selection question in the Decision Book's words.

**Conflicts.** None direct. Three gaps that will become conflicts if unaddressed. The design system's chart components are built for counted series; a felt measure is a distribution with bands whose cut-points were chosen by a stated method, and the system needs a component that shows the spread, the bands, the n and the selection together, or a felt result will be drawn as a bar and read as a verdict. The Decision Book's data chapter treats provenance as source, period, geography and method; a felt measure's provenance is also *who was asked, who answered and who was not*, and the provenance set needs those lines or the measure record must sit beside it. And the Decision Book's "every chart carries a provenance code" has no status for *indicator, unvalidated here* — the right label for every trace or signal shown before it has been checked against a descriptor — and without it the system will either hide the trace or show it as the thing. **Logged for the design system:** a felt-distribution component (spread, bands, n, selection); a discordance/convergence matrix; a "what this changed" element; an attribution form for testimony in the respondent's own voice; an "unvalidated indicator" status. **Logged for the Decision Book:** a chapter on felt data — register, construct before item, distribution not point, sample on the face, the five sources, member checking as procedure, the integration protocol and discordance cell, "what this changed", the co-presence and trace cautions, the emotion-inference boundary — or an extension of the Decision Book's Chapter 8 that carries them.
