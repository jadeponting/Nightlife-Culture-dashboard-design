# Chapter 12 — Recognising your place

*Dashboard Design · Part 3: The reading experience*

---

*Reading this chapter.* This is one chapter of *Dashboard Design*, Nightlife Culture's research book on how evidence becomes a display a reader can trust, read and act on. The studio ("the house" in some chapters) is Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio; the brief is its founder Carly Heath's commission for the book, and her eight ways a dashboard fails, the failure register, run through it. The Vibe Map, the studio's prototype for showing a place's nightlife culture, appears only as an illustration, never as a specification. "Version 1" is the book's archived first draft, whose positions this version re-tests against the sources. Every rule carries a kind (where it comes from: Standard, Evidence, Reading, Practice or Nightlife Culture) and a tier (how firm it is: Fixed, Adaptable or Decorative); Chapter 0 explains both, and the three senses of Fixed: a requirement that can be checked, a sequence whose order is the hard line, and a statement of the record. A dagger (†) marks a source read at the authors' abstract or summary only; "secondary" a quotation reached through a named reproduction; "(read once)" a quotation returned on a single reading and not confirmed by a second. Chapter 0 holds the glossary of the words the chapters share; each chapter lists its own coinages before its sources.

## The question

What makes a person, or a scene, recognise its place in a public data display — and what must the display do so that what is counted and what is missed sit together without the counted winning; so that what a place *is* is never ranked while what it *has* is compared, with the gate on the face; so that the unit shown is the one the scene lives at, and the display says which unit that is and what it hides; so that finding a place that suits you is matching and not ranking, with the why shown; and so that whatever is cut out of a view and carried away still says what it is?

---

## The 60-second version

The brief's fourth failure — "for nightlife, it fails if they do not recognise their place within the front-end interface" — is several failures. Section 1 splits the word into four senses and section 10 into five tests; the headline findings fall under five heads, and each has its own evidence and its own remedy.

**The first is the unit.** People do not live in output areas. The largest measurement of residents' own neighbourhoods that this chapter could reach — 7,498 households in ten American cities, asked to draw "the area around where you live" on a street map — found a median drawn area of 0.35 square miles against the programme's own target areas of 2.23. The United Kingdom's smallest census units, the output areas, "were designed to have similar population sizes and be as socially homogenous as possible", and the official releases that call them "neighbourhoods" do so by definition, not by anyone's recognition. Change the unit and the finding changes: in a test on the whole of the 2001 English census, moving between output area, ward and district changed the correlation between a pair of variables in "about 60 per cent of cases", and the author's verdict — "Most of the time, not very – but there are occasions when it can make a great deal of difference. Unfortunately it remains difficult to predict these occasions" — is the fairest sentence in the literature. So the unit is a declared choice with a known cost, stated on the face with what it hides, and a page about a place offers more than one.

**The second is whether the reader is in it.** The one interview study reached of lay readers choosing between charts, some of them maps, found that "People often determined the quality of a graph containing geographical information based on how easy or difficult it was to find their home state", and that "The most recurring theme in our analysis were decisions framed or driven by personal experience" — 42 interviews, one topic, a self-reported ranking, and the authors' own request that it be replicated. The official body that wrote 331 local stories found that readers "really valued being able to read all about the place they call home", and that within days "every individual area's page had been read". No controlled experiment was found. The display acts on the interviews and flags the gap — and notes that the places those readers found were states, counties and local authorities, units with names; section 3 shows that the small-area units have neither names nor edges people hold, so the rule is that the reader's place is found by a name.

**The third is the two columns, and here version 1 loses the reason for its best-known rule.** Version 1 said the counted column must never be shown first because "repeating the opposing frame strengthens it". The corrections literature now says, in the words of its own 2020 handbook, that "exhaustive experimental attempts to induce a backfire effect through familiarity alone have come up empty"; a 2020 review concludes that "backfire effects are not a robust empirical phenomenon"; and the competitive-framing experiments that bear on it found that "framing effects depend more heavily on the qualities of frames than on their frequency of dissemination". Value-first survives — as a house stance, with the order itself resting on one ground, that the first thing shown is read as an endorsement (Chapter 11), and the missed column's presence and equal standing resting on two more: a bar chart of a gap "may encourage deficit thinking" and hiding variability "tends to increase stereotyping while emphasizing variability reduces it"; and leading with a prevalence figure "had no significant effect on issue salience" in a series of experiments with 12,448 respondents — opening on a count buys nothing, which is not the same as saying it harms. What else survives: numeric uncertainty costs little or no trust (a review of 48 experiments found "only positive or null effects" †), so the missed column's intervals are not a weakness to hide; dropping a gap is the most biased way to show it (Chapter 11), and the Analysis Function's table shorthand already has a symbol for every kind of nothing; two unlike measures never share an axis ("In general, we do not recommend using dual axis charts because they can be easily misinterpreted"), with the measured cost that side-by-side charts have "almost never outperformed" other layouts for precise comparison. And "equal visual dignity" — version 1's rule that the missed column is never the sketchy one — remains untested: the one experiment on sketchy rendering found it cost accuracy and raised engagement, and said nothing about belief.

**The fourth is the ranking, and it has three parts.** *Identity is never ranked, and that is a stance, not a measurement fact*: Velleman and Wilkinson showed in 1993 that "Scale type, as defined by Stevens, is not an attribute of the data, but rather depends upon the questions we intend to ask of the data", so a profile is nominal because the house has decided what question its profile answers. The display forms follow: the radar chart was "the least effective and least liked" in the one head-to-head on composite indicators that was reached (†), a filled radar's ranking by area reverses when its axes are reordered (†), and the United Kingdom's own area classifications were named by a published rule — "words and phrases that might be construed as pejorative were not used", and descriptors present each cluster "as consequences of factors that have happened to areas rather than the consequences of human agency" — a rule for naming without blame, whose output still includes "Hard-Pressed Living" — because a label for a condition cannot be neutral, which is why the house's labels name identity and conditions are shown as numbers. *Conditions are compared with a gate*, and the gate is a standard with official cousins: the European code's Principle 14, the Code of Practice 3.0's practice 6.6 ("comparable across and within geographies at regional, national and local levels, where possible"), and the ONS's own local statistics service, which keeps non-coherent data "as separate indicators to try to avoid inappropriate comparisons" and turns a comparison into three words by a published rule — "higher", "lower" or "similar" at one median absolute deviation. *Discovery is matching*, and the recommender literature draws the line exactly: explanations raise acceptance (the founding study's "86%" is 86 per cent of 97 people asked about the interface they had just used, and the same study found "no statistically significant difference" in decisions); neighbour-style "people like you" explanations caused users "to overestimate the actual-rating by 0.74 on average" while content cues "were found to be significantly more effective at enabling accurate assessments"; personalising the explanation was "detrimental to effectiveness, though it may improve user satisfaction" (†); and sorting by popularity is itself the treatment — in a 14,341-person experiment, "Increasing the strength of social influence increased both inequality and unpredictability of success". "An aggregation of the biggest" has a name in that literature: exposure, "largely determined by position in the ranking", where a relevance gap of 0.03 can become 30 per cent less exposure.

**And the fifth is the crop.** Of 9,958 coded posts carrying visualisations about the pandemic, of every stance, "only 12%" broke a design guideline and "the majority (55.3%)" of the charts were screenshots of existing charts; among the sceptics' charts alone, more than two-fifths were screenshots of data-exploration dashboards. The United Kingdom's official guidance puts a chart's title and source in the page text and not in the image, for accessibility, and says in terms that "This does mean the image of the chart can be taken out of context if it is copied and pasted elsewhere". So the crop test is a provenance rule before it is a layout rule: what a cut-out must carry, inside its frame, is title, unit, source, base, address and snapshot date, and for a comparison its gate and its wording — the missing-context half of what goes wrong, not the misreasoning half, which no caption cures. The one study that designed against cherry-picking found that "Juxtaposed approaches are croppable and thus not tamper-evident", that a superimposed guardrail worked best, and that 38 per cent of readers did not notice it; the lesson the chapter takes from it is tamper-evidence, and it applies it to the two columns too: each column's frame carries the bridging sentence, so a cropped counted panel still says what sat beside it.

Beneath each place's page the chapter leaves a ninth record, the ten-line *recognition record*: unit; residents' unit; scale; identity fields; labels; gates; peers; sentences; review; errors. The pass tests are version 1's, sharpened by Chapters 9 and 11: a reader from the scene says what is there and what is missing from their own knowledge of the place; a reader from a place whose counted column is thin opens its page and does not read "you lost".

## 1 · What "recognising your place" means

The brief's sentence is short and the thing it names is not one thing. When a person opens a page about a place and says "that's not us", they may mean four different things, and a dashboard that fixes one will still fail the other three.

**Sense one: the geography.** The reader looks for where they live, work or play and cannot find it, because the display's unit is not the one they hold. The evidence on how people hold a place is a method literature — sketch maps, drawn boundaries, vague regions — and section 3 reads it. Chapter 3 settled the perceptual half: a reader recognises what they already have a schema for. This chapter settles the spatial half: the schema is a place with a name and fuzzy edges, and the statistical unit is neither.

**Sense two: the self.** The reader finds the place and does not find themselves or their people in what is said about it. The evidence here is thin and interview-based, and section 4 gives it at its true strength.

**Sense three: the account.** The people the page describes do not agree that it describes them. Chapter 9 placed this: recognition by the described is member checking, which supplies two of the five sources of validity evidence and legitimacy, and never the whole. This chapter asks for the procedure's form when the members are a scene and not a sample, and finds that the literature on returning findings to a community is a literature of procedures and principles with almost no evaluation behind them — and one researcher's own account of harm done.

**Sense four: the standing.** The reader finds the place, finds themselves, agrees with the account, and still reads the page as a verdict: ranked, labelled, compared to their cost. This is the sense the deck's two columns are about, and the sense that recognition as a political category names — Taylor's "Nonrecognition or misrecognition can inflict harm" (as the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy quotes him; secondary), and Fraser's two hazards, that a politics of recognition may "displace" a politics of redistribution and may "reify" the identities it recognises. The chapter takes those two hazards as a frame for its own structure and draws no authority from the philosophy: a display of identity that stands in for a comparison of conditions is displacement on a screen; a profile that freezes a place into a type is reification on a screen.

The house already has a test that spans the four. Its own slide reads: "Does it paint a recognisable picture? Ask hyperlocal scenes whether they recognise their activity, relationships and identity. Record who is missing and what contributors change." *(Nightlife Culture; Style Guide v1.5, slides.)* That is sense three made a procedure, with sense two's "who is missing" and Chapter 9's "what this changed" inside it. The chapter's job is to give each sense its evidence, and to say what the display owes to each.

## 2 · What people believe about recognition, and what the evidence says

Eight beliefs, each common in the trade, each tested below.

**"People don't engage with statistics about their area because statistics are abstract."** The one interview study reached of lay readers choosing among charts found something else: readers judged a chart with a map in it by whether they could find home. The engagement problem, on that evidence, is the unit and the self, not the statistics. *(Section 4.)*

**"Open on the problem, then answer it."** The counted column first, the value column as the reply. Version 1 rejected this for a reason that does not survive; this chapter rejects it for three that do. *(Section 5.)*

**"Never repeat the opposing frame."** Version 1's rule, the Decision Book's ARG-12, and Lakoff's. The corrections literature has since looked hard for the mechanism and not found it. *(Section 5.)*

**"Hard data looks truer, so the softer column should be dressed up."** The first half has a measured cousin — precision is read as confidence — and the second half was barred by version 1 on the Style Guide's evidence standard and is now barred by the evidence: quantified uncertainty costs little or no trust, so the missed column's intervals are not a liability. *(Section 5.)*

**"You can't rank identity because it's nominal."** True as a stance; false as a fact about data. Stevens's typology does not fix the scale type of anything; the question asked does. The house decides the question. *(Section 6.)*

**"The ward is the neighbourhood."** No UK statistical unit was designed around what residents recognise. Output areas were built for size and homogeneity; wards vary "from fewer than 100 residents to more than 30,000"; and the deprivation indices call super output areas "neighbourhoods" by stipulation. *(Section 3.)*

**"A recommendation is just a ranking with a nicer face."** The recommender literature separates them by a measurable line: an ordering made for one reader from their own weights, with the why shown as content, is a match; an ordering published as the maker's, or driven by popularity, is a rank and behaves like one — it concentrates exposure and it changes what it ranks. *(Section 7.)*

**"A chart carries its caption."** The measured population of charts in circulation is mostly screenshots, and official guidance keeps the title and source outside the image on purpose. The caption does not travel; the image does. *(Section 8.)*

## 3 · Finding your geography: the unit

### How people hold a place

The founding method is Kevin Lynch's, from *The Image of the City* (1960): residents asked to sketch their city from memory and to describe routes, from which Lynch drew his five elements — paths, edges, districts, nodes and landmarks. The book could not be reached at source; the instruction his interviewers gave, as a secondary page quotes it, was to draw "just as if you were making a rapid description of the city to a stranger, covering all the main features" and "We don't expect an accurate drawing- just a rough sketch" (secondary; Wikipedia quoting Lynch). The method's later literature is more useful to this chapter than the founding text would have been, because it says what a sketch map can be trusted for. Wang and Schwering (2015), with 21 participants drawing 45 maps of areas they knew, found that "sketch maps are incomplete, distorted, and schematized" and yet carry "a set of seven sketch aspects that constitute invariant spatial information", among them the topology of streets, their orientation, the cyclic order of things around a point, the topology of blocks and the orientation of landmarks — each reproduced in well over nine maps in ten — while distances "were found either lengthened or shortened without discernable patterns". *(Evidence; small sample, one city.)* A review of the method's analysis found "its analytics ad hoc and piecemeal" (Gieseking 2013 †). The reading for a dashboard: what a person holds about a place is which things are there, what is next to what, and in what order along a way — not how far, and not where the line is. A display that asks a reader to recognise a place by its boundary is asking for the thing their map holds least; the two methods below ask people to draw one anyway, and measure how far they agree.

### Residents' neighbourhoods against the units

The direct measurement is drawn boundaries. Coulton and colleagues' 2001 pilot found that when residents' maps were used to define neighbourhoods, "the resulting units covered different space and produced different social indicator values than did census-defined units", and that "Residents' agreement about their neighborhoods' boundaries differed among the neighborhoods studied" (†; the body, with its sizes and overlaps, was not reachable). The same team's large survey is reachable in a research report: 7,498 households across ten American cities were read the prompt "By neighborhood, I mean the area around where you live and around your house. It may include places you shop, religious or public institutions, or a local business district", and asked to draw it in pencil on a street map with their home marked. Eighty-three per cent drew one. "The median area of the RD maps was 0.35 square miles", ranging from 1.2 square miles in one city to 0.09 in another, against "the typical initiative target area (2.23 square miles)" — roughly a sixth. Where several residents gave the same neighbourhood name, the team built a consensus area from blocks "included by at least 33 percent of the respondents" (Coulton, Chan & Mikelbank 2010; research report, not journal-reviewed). *(Evidence; the largest reachable measurement; American.)* Two limits for this book. The comparator is a programme's target area, not a census or administrative unit; and every drawn-boundary study reached asks where people *live*, and a scene's people often do not live where the scene is. What a scene's own unit looks like, drawn by the people who make it rather than the people who sleep there, is unmeasured.

The United Kingdom has no measurement of that size that this chapter could reach. What it has is definitions that leave the unit to the respondent. Understanding Society asks "I feel like I belong to this neighbourhood" with no spatial definition at all; the Community Life Survey (Chapter 9) defines a neighbourhood as "a few minutes walking distance from your home" for belonging and a "wider local area" as "within 15 to 20 minutes' walking distance from your home" for satisfaction — two frames in one questionnaire, each named. The policy literature's standard account is Kearns and Parkinson's three scales, as Lupton summarises them: "the home area, has a psycho-social purpose, fostering attachment and belonging"; "the locality, is the locale for residential activities, and denotes social status and provision"; "the urban district or region provides a wider landscape of social and economic opportunities" (Lupton 2003, secondary). And the plainest sentence in Lupton's paper is borrowed too: "neighbourhood delineation has been defined by the limitations of an available data set" (Lupton quoting Dietz).

### A vague place has a method

Where a place has a name and no edge — a city centre, a quarter, a scene's patch — the behavioural method is Montello and colleagues' (2003): 36 pedestrians stopped on the street and asked to "outline the area of the city that you consider to be downtown", then the area they were "100% confident was downtown", then "50% confident", then a core point. Of the 36, 33 drew 50 per cent regions larger than their 100 per cent regions; "The core was located an average of nearly 300 meters from the centroid of the 100% region"; and, the finding that matters for a display, "respondents will be willing to make discrete judgments about area membership" of what the authors call "a vague region". *(Evidence; one city; street-intercept sample.)* The display form that follows is bands of agreement rather than a line. A crowd-sourced neighbourhood map of Boston did it on an "arbitrary hexagonal grid", tallying for each cell how many respondents' polygons covered it — 300 submissions at the time — "divided that count by the total number of respondents for the neighborhood", and drew the 25, 50 and 75 per cent contours as "a notable minority, a majority, and a strong majority" — among, as the author says, "the self-selected people who responded to the survey" (Bostonography 2012; practice). Passive crowd data can do the same at scale: from 8 million geotagged photographs, Hollenstein and Purves (2010) outlined city cores from the "0.5–2% of tags" that name one, with a measured contributor problem — "42% of the outliers stem from only three contributors" — and the caution that the contributors "should not be considered to be representative of society as a whole". *(Evidence; method with stated bias.)* A fuzzy-set method publishes the shape as a core and a boundary zone rather than a line (Aadland, Farah & Magee 2016 †). The rule these point at is simple: a place with no administrative edge is shown as a core and bands — one person's confidence in Montello's method, the share of people agreeing in Boston's — with the number of people whose agreement it is.

### What the official units were built for

None of the United Kingdom's small-area units was designed around what residents recognise, and the ONS says so in its own description. Output areas, the smallest census unit, "were designed to have similar population sizes and be as socially homogenous as possible based on tenure of household and dwelling type (homogeneity was not used as a factor in Scotland)"; "The minimum OA size was 40 resident households and 100 resident people, but the recommended size was rather larger at 125 households"; "Urban/rural mixes were avoided where possible". Lower-layer super output areas "comprise between 400 and 1,200 households and have a usually resident population between 1,000 and 3,000 persons"; middle-layer ones "between 2,000 and 6,000 households" and "between 5,000 and 15,000 persons". They exist because wards "vary greatly in size, from fewer than 100 residents to more than 30,000. This was not ideal for nationwide comparisons, and also meant that some data could not be released for smaller wards due to disclosure issues". Wards themselves: "8,483 electoral wards and electoral divisions, with 6,904 in England", average "about 7,900" people, boundaries changed "on the first Thursday in May each year". Built-up areas are the one unit shaped like a settlement — "a geography based on the physical built environment, using Ordnance Survey topographic data to recognise developed land, such as cities, towns, and villages" — and they pay for it in time: "statistics from 2021 BUAs cannot be directly compared with those from 2011". Postcodes "mostly do not align with other geographic boundaries" and are "subject to continuous change". *(Standard (ONS geography); all at source.)*

Two official habits follow from this and both matter to a page about a place. The first is stipulation: the English Indices of Deprivation say "LSOAs are also referred to as 'neighbourhoods' throughout this release", and the 2025 edition repeats it — a unit "designed to be of a similar population size" is called a neighbourhood because a release needs a word. *(Standard (official statistics).)* The second is best-fit: postcodes and small areas are assigned to larger geographies by a method the ONS describes — "postcodes with addresses that straddle a boundary will be assigned to the area where the mean grid reference of all the addresses within that postcode falls" — and the Decision Book already requires that a comparison across boundary vintages carries "an explicit, stated lookup" because "Best-fit lookups carry error that must be declared" (Decision Book, data rules on geography; read directly). Middle-layer super output areas "were not given recognisable names when designed" — the default looks like "Ashfield 004" — and the House of Commons Library has named all 7,264 of them "based on the towns, villages and neighbourhoods that they cover", publishing drafts on a map in 2019 for public correction, with the caveat that "The names aren't intended to supplant any names that might already be used locally and they aren't 'official' names" (practice; version 2.3, 13 February 2026). That project is the nearest thing the country has to a statistical unit made recognisable by the people who live in it, and its method — publish, invite correction, keep a standing errors channel — is one this chapter will borrow in section 9. The ONS has also, since January 2023, let a reader draw their own unit: "simply select an area on a map and get the data directly", built from output areas by "population weighted centroid" — a tool its authors later described as "designed to help people get census data that didn't fit standard geographies" (practice; two posts, 2023 and 2025). The official body no longer insists the reader take its unit.

### Change the unit and the finding changes

The modifiable areal unit problem is Chapter 1's (Openshaw's "There are no rules for areal aggregation, no standards, and no international conventions") and Chapter 4 carries the ecological fallacy. This chapter needs the demonstration, because "a borough with a scene beats a borough with a superclub" — version 1's illustrative claim, not a finding — is a MAUP consequence if it is anything, and it should be stated as one. The problem has two parts, in the words Jelinski and Wu quote from Openshaw and Taylor: the scale problem is "the variation in results that may be obtained when the same areal data are combined into sets of increasingly larger areal units of analysis", and the zoning problem is "any variations in results due to alternative units of analysis where n, the number of units, is constant". The famous demonstration is theirs: for the 99 counties of Iowa, "correlations ranging from + 0.979 to - 0.811 could be produced by varying the scale and zoning strategies" (secondary, via Jelinski & Wu 1996; the "−0.99 to +0.99" that circulates could not be verified and is not printed). Fotheringham and Wong's multivariate test concluded that the problem "is shown to be essentially unpredictable in its intensity and effects in multivariate statistical analysis" (†). The United Kingdom's own test is Flowerdew's (2011), on the 2001 census of England across its output areas, wards and districts (165,665, 7,969 and 354 — read once): eighteen variables, "153 correlations at each of the three scales"; "68 of these were negative at all three scales, while 60 were positive at all three", and the rest changed sign somewhere between output area and district (the paper's own breakdown of the remainder does not reconcile with its total, and is not printed); by Fisher's test between scales, "the MAUP does make a difference in about 60 per cent of cases"; and his summary — "How serious is the MAUP? Most of the time, not very – but there are occasions when it can make a great deal of difference." "Unfortunately it remains difficult to predict these occasions" (read once) — with the general warning that "correlations over virtually the whole range from -1 to +1 can be reached by judicious placement of zone boundaries". *(Evidence; scale effect only; the national census.)*

The reading is not that small units are true and large ones false. It is that any figure about a place is a figure about a unit, that the choice of unit can change the sign of a finding, and that nobody can tell in advance which findings. So the unit is declared, with what it hides, on every place-page; and where the house chooses the scene scale as the landing unit (a choice Chapter 11 left to the studio), the page says so and offers the administrative unit beside it, because the reader's councillor, funder and licensing officer live in the administrative one.

### The scene as a unit

Version 1 used "scene" as if it were a place. The sociology of culture uses it as a unit of analysis with a definition, and the definition is the reason the scene scale is hard to draw. Straw (1991): a community "presumes a population group whose composition is relatively stable" rooted "within a geographically specific historical heritage", whereas "A musical scene, in contrast, is that cultural space in which a range of musical practices coexist, interacting with each other within a variety of processes of differentiation, and according to widely varying trajectories of change and cross-fertilization". *(Reading of a conceptual text; read for the unit, never for any scene.)* A scene, on that definition, is a space of practices, not a space on the ground, and its later theorists split it into local, translocal and virtual (Bennett & Peterson 2004; not reached; named only). The one tradition that turns scenes into a measurable unit of place is the Chicago "scenes" method: a scene is "a cluster of urban amenities", scored by coders on fifteen sub-dimensions from 1 to 5, "where 5 indicates that participation in the activities promoted by an amenity is fundamentally defined by the given sub-dimension", with the score attached to a postal area by multiplying "the number of amenities of a given type in a zip code by that amenity-type's score", summing, and dividing "by the total number of amenities in the zip code" (Silver, Clark & Rothfield, working paper; Silver, Clark & Navarro 2010 †). Read only for method, it teaches two things. The unit is built from what is listed — the census of business, the yellow pages — so, as Straw's later essay puts it, scenes "make cultural activity invisible and indecipherable by 'hiding' cultural productivity behind seemingly meaningless (or indistinguishable) forms of social life", and a method built on listings cannot see what is not listed; that is Chapter 4's aggregation finding at the level of the unit. And the method's own authors rank on it: "We can also use these profiles to create rankings and maps of the U.S. in terms of zip codes' scores on individual sub-dimensions". A profile built as identity was ranked by the people who built it. That is the pattern this chapter's section 6 exists to prevent, and it is worth knowing that the method nearest to the house's own ambition did not prevent it.

## 4 · Finding yourself: personal relevance

The evidence that a reader attends to data about their own place is real, interview-based and small, and the chapter states it at that size.

Peck, Ayuso and El-Etr (2019) ran "42 semi-structured interviews in rural Pennsylvania", asking people to rank ten charts on one topic "Based on how useful they are to you". Their headline: "The most recurring theme in our analysis were decisions framed or driven by personal experience." On geography: "People often determined the quality of a graph containing geographical information based on how easy or difficult it was to find their home state." They counted "more than 20 instances in which participants referenced a relatable component", and one participant said of a map, "It has my own county in it. If this would have been like all the countries of the world or something, I really wouldn't have been interested in it because it just affects me personally." The design implication is the authors': "it may be necessary to not only provide mechanisms that allow people to explore personal dimensions of data, but to make sure those personal dimensions are front-and-center in first encounters with a visualization." And the limits are theirs too: "It is unclear how representative our sample is"; "The findings in this paper should be replicated with controlled studies, different populations, and larger sample sizes." *(Evidence; interviews; usefulness self-reported; one topic.)* Chapter 11 took the same study's finding on source lines; this is its other half.

The Seeing Data study — "nine focus groups with a total of 46 participants", ages 11 to 70 — found six factors in engagement, of which subject matter is one: "When subject matter spoke to participants' interest, they were engaged", and "Strong emotions may arise when considering a visualization, and these also affect engagement" (Kennedy, Hill, Allen & Kirk 2016). The paper frames relevance as interest in a subject, not as seeing one's own place; the companion paper on the feeling of numbers could not be read beyond its abstract. The nearest experiment is on spatial analogies rather than places: shown a distance or area re-expressed in terms of somewhere they knew, 40 crowd-workers rated the information's helpfulness "1.9 points higher (on a 7 pt scale)" than with no analogy and "0.7 points higher than when they see generic spatial analogy", on the premise that "people struggle to understand these measurements when they cannot relate them to measurements of locations that they are personally familiar with" (Kim, Hullman & Agrawala 2016; self-rated helpfulness, not comprehension). And the one official account is the ONS's, from its 331 Census stories (Chapter 11): "We tested the concept with different users who really valued being able to read all about the place they call home in a journalistic style"; "Within a few days of publication in December 2022, every individual area's page had been read"; "The story was taken up by dozens of local news titles" — with no participant numbers, page views or trust measure given. *(Practice.)*

What is missing is the controlled study: nothing this chapter could reach has measured whether readers attend to, understand or remember a chart better when it is about their own locality, as against the same chart about somewhere else. Two things about that evidence before the display acts on it. The places those readers found were a state, a county and a local authority — units with names — and section 3 has shown that the small-area units have neither names nor edges people hold; so what the evidence supports is that a reader looks for a *named* place, and the rule is that the reader's place is found first and found by a name people use, whatever unit the figures are for. And "found home" is the beginning of attention, not the measure of understanding; none of these studies measured comprehension. The prototype can run the test the literature has not.

The political frame belongs here because it names the risk of acting on sense two alone. Fraser's warning is that "questions of recognition are serving less to supplement, complicate and enrich redistributive struggles than to marginalize, eclipse and displace them" — "the problem of displacement" — and that recognition "risks sanctioning violations of human rights and freezing the very antagonisms it purports to mediate" — "the problem of reification" (Fraser 2000; the reachable opening; reported as frame). A page that gives a scene the pleasure of seeing itself and does not set its conditions beside its peers' has displaced; a page that types a scene and keeps the type has reified. The two columns and the identity/conditions split are the display's answer to both, and the next two sections build them.

## 5 · The two columns: counted beside missed

The deck's slides 7 and 8 set the problem: one column of what systems already count about a place and one of what a scene holds and almost nobody counts. Version 1 gave the display five rules — value first; the counted column never omitted; equal visual dignity; gaps rendered full-size and labelled; no shared scale — and a bridging sentence. This section tests each against evidence version 1 did not have, and the first rule loses its reason and keeps its conclusion.

### The framing rule, re-tested

Version 1's first rule rested on a synthesis of framing research carried into the Decision Book as its Chapter 16 and its rule ARG-12: "Never repeat the opposing frame in order to deny it — Repetition reinforces it" (Fixed). The source behind that is Lakoff, in his own words: "In general, negating a frame just activates the frame and makes it stronger", and the advice "not to repeat the language of the other side or negate their framing of the issue" (Lakoff 2016, blog; no study cited on the page). The rule could not be found in the Othering & Belonging Institute's public narrative essays; if the house document attributes it there, the attribution is unconfirmed.

The empirical literature that tests the mechanism is the corrections literature, and it has moved. The *Debunking Handbook 2020*, written by twenty-two of the field's researchers: "Early evidence was supportive of this idea"; "exhaustive experimental attempts to induce a backfire effect through familiarity alone have come up empty"; "Debunking a myth makes it more familiar but the debunking usually overpowers the increase in familiarity"; and the practical rule, "Repeat the misinformation, only once, directly prior to the correction" — with the residue that remains, "Although backfire effects are uncommon, we know that repetition makes information appear true". Swire-Thompson, DeGutis and Lazer's review (2020): "We suggest that backfire effects are not a robust empirical phenomenon"; the familiarity version "rarely, if ever, occurs when the misinformation is paired with a clear and salient correction"; the origin of the idea was "an unpublished manuscript (Skurnik, Yoon, & Schwarz, 2007)" with "failures to directly replicate"; and the recommendation, "Avoiding the repetition of the original misconception within the correction appears to be unnecessary and could even hinder corrective efforts", while "needless repetitions of the misconceptions should still be avoided". The one published finding in the old direction is Skurnik and colleagues' (2005): "Repeatedly identifying a claim as false helped older adults remember it as false in the short term but paradoxically made them more likely to remember it as true after a 3 day delay" (†) — older adults, after a delay, on memory for truth status. Wood and Porter's "more than 10,100 subjects" and "52 issues" found "no corrections capable of triggering backfire" (†), but that is the worldview backfire, not the familiarity one, and the chapter does not cite it for repetition. *(Evidence; the field's own reviews.)*

Two cautions before the rule is retired. The corrections literature tests belief in a factual claim; Lakoff's claim is about evoking a frame, which is not the same thing, so the literature weakens the rule's usual justification without testing its mechanism head-on. And the framing literature proper does bear on it: Chong and Druckman's competitive experiments (869 and 1,277 participants) found that "framing effects depend more heavily on the qualities of frames than on their frequency of dissemination", that "competition alters but does not eliminate the influence of framing", and that "in no case did repetition of a weak frame allow it to prevail over a strong opposition frame". *(Evidence.)* When both frames are on the page, strength decides, not count.

So the rule "never show the counted column first because showing it strengthens it" is withdrawn as an evidence claim, and ARG-12 is logged as a conflict for the Decision Book. **Value-first survives as a house stance, and the chapter is careful about what supports what.** The *order* rests on one ground, Chapter 11's: the first thing a page shows is read as an endorsement of it, so the choice of first column is a declaration, and the house's declaration is "lead with abundance" (Nightlife Culture). No evidence reached speaks to the order of two true columns either way. The missed column's *presence and equal standing* rest on two more. The deficit evidence, which is about what a chart of a gap does to its reader rather than about repetition: Holder and Xiong's four experiments (329, 434, 281 and 266 crowd-workers after exclusions) found that "bar charts may encourage deficit thinking" — "the perception that outcome disparities are caused by groups' personal strengths or deficiencies, rather than external factors" — and that "Hiding variability tends to increase stereotyping while emphasizing variability reduces it" (first experiment d = 0.322); Quinn's three randomised experiments found that a news story about group gaps "led viewers to express more exaggerated stereotypes" (0.30 and 0.38 of a standard deviation), "did not affect viewers' explicit competence-related racial stereotyping, the explanations they gave for achievement inequalities, or their prioritization of ending achievement inequalities", and that "After 2 weeks, the effect on stereotype exaggeration faded" (†). Both concern social groups, not places; the transfer is this book's reading. And the finding that opening on a count buys nothing: in FrameWorks' United Kingdom poverty research, a series of experiments with "a nationally representative sample of 12,448 respondents", "leading with prevalence data had no significant effect on issue salience" — "leading with data is the same as saying nothing at all". *(Evidence; practitioner research with experiments.)* That is a null on salience: it shows no gain from opening on a count, not a harm.

The case version 1 took from Chapter 1 is also corrected. Chapter 1 cited a community-safety dashboard that "refocused the conversation" from an asset-based to a deficit-based model. That sentence is Sarikaya and colleagues' gloss, and the original report says something more useful: "Community engagement experts often take an asset-based approach to development"; "The asset-based approach is challenging in settings such as the Data Dashboard project"; "Yet the data sets of key relevance to public safety are largely negative"; "We invited the community to suggest place-based assets that we could include on the map"; and "We do not yet know how effective our methods have been in conveying an asset-based approach" (O'Connell et al. 2016). *(Practice; a design case with no evaluation.)* It is a supply problem — the counted data are negative — with three attempted responses, not a finding about readers. Which is exactly the two columns' problem, named by a team that met it.

### The asset tradition and its own cautions

The vocabulary of "assets" and "deficits" comes from community development, and the founding text is reachable in its 1990 form: "most Americans think about lower income urban neighborhoods as problems"; "They are noted for their deficiencies and needs"; "residents come to believe that their well-being depends upon being a client"; against which "every neighborhood has a map of riches, assets, and capacities", and the alternative is "to develop policies and activities based on the capacities, skills, and assets of low-income people and their neighborhoods" — "a map of the same territory as the neighborhood needs map" (McKnight & Kretzmann 1990; practitioner framework). The tradition's own reviews are careful. The Glasgow Centre for Population Health: "Asset based approaches are not a replacement for investing in service improvement or attempting to address the structural causes of health inequalities"; "evidence of the effectiveness of these approaches at present largely comes from case studies and small scale exploratory research"; and "it is also conceivable that the adoption of asset based approaches may increase health inequalities" (2011); "difficulties are inherent in measuring assets"; "One of the initial challenges for asset working is to find and collate data that measure positive health and wellbeing" (2012). The Lowitja Institute's review of deficit discourse — "discourse that represents people or groups in terms of deficiency – absence, lack or failure", which "narrowly situates responsibility for problems with the affected individuals or communities, overlooking the larger socio-economic structures" — insists that "These should not be mistaken for calls to deflate the realities of disadvantage", finds that gap framing "certainly appears to carry (and replicate) an implicit assumption of deficit", quotes an interviewee for whom "These statistics became like a mantra" and "I felt like statistics were defining my cultural identity", and reports "almost a complete lack of evaluation of actual impact on discourse itself" (Fogarty et al. 2018; the report quoting others where marked). *(Practitioner reviews; evidence status stated by the reviews themselves.)* The house's voice rule already holds the conclusion — "Generous: Recognise capability and knowledge before proposing something new", against "Defining people or places only through failure or need" (Style Guide v1.5) — and the chapter adds what the tradition's own reviewers add: the missed column is shown beside the counted one, never instead of it, and nothing in it deflates what the counted column records.

### Robustness reads as reality; intervals cost little trust

Version 1 argued, without evidence, that "robustness reads as reality": a column of crisp administrative numbers beside a column of ranges will be read as the real one. The measured cousin is granularity. Zhang and Schwarz (2012): "Consumers attend to the granularity chosen by a communicator and draw pragmatic inferences that influence judgment and choice"; estimates in finer granularity are read as more precise and held with more confidence, an effect that disappears when the reader doubts the communicator is being cooperative (†). *(Evidence; consumer judgement; sample sizes not visible.)* The price-precision effect — buyers "underestimate the magnitudes of precise prices" (Thomas, Simon & Kadiyali †) — is a different effect and is not merged with it.

The other half of version 1's worry — that showing the missed column's uncertainty will cost it trust — is now measured and the direction is the other way. Van der Bles and colleagues, five experiments, "total n = 5,780", one run on the BBC News site: against "a common assumption is that communicating uncertainty will reduce public trust", they "observed only a small decrease in trust in numbers and trustworthiness of the source, and mostly for verbal uncertainty communication" — a verbal hedge cost d = 0.75 on trust in numbers, a numeric range d = 0.17 and not significant. Gustafson and Rice's review of 48 experiments (†): "most findings of negative effects (such as reduced credibility and beliefs) are from experiments that operationalized uncertainty as disagreement or conflict in science"; quantified error ranges and probabilities have "had only positive or null effects, not negative effects". Dries and colleagues (2024): "communicating uncertainty buffers against a loss of trust when evidence changes" (†). And the producers' side: of 90 visualisation authors surveyed, those who omit uncertainty gave as reasons "not wanting to confuse or overwhelm viewers (53/85; 62%)" and "not wanting to make data seem questionable (15/85; 17%)" (Hullman 2019; 90 surveyed, 85 answering). *(Evidence; consistent in direction; one review read in abstract.)* So the missed column shows its intervals, its samples and its bands as Chapter 9 requires, and the chapter records that the fear of doing so is a minority view among makers and unsupported among readers. Where disagreement between sources exists — felt and counted at odds, Chapter 9's discordant cell — that is the form of uncertainty the reviews find most reliably damages credibility (a verbal hedge costs some too), and it is shown anyway, because Chapter 9's rule is that the disagreement is reported in its cell; the chapter notes the cost.

### Rendering absence

The missed column will have holes for years, and version 1's rule was that every hole is rendered full-size and labelled with why. The evidence it cited is confirmed at source and narrowed. CALVI (Ge, Cui & Kay 2023), 497 participants: "Participants' correctness (proportion of correct answers) for the 15 trick items ranged from 0 to 0.93 (M = 0.39, SD = 0.16)" against 0.80 on normal items, and the hardest misleaders were "Overplotting, Missing Data, and Missing Normalization". But CALVI's "Missing Data" is "A visual representation implies data exist but the data is actually missing" — a mark drawn where nothing was measured, not an absent category or series. Nobody this chapter could find has tested whether readers notice a missing *column*. The display findings that do bear on it are Chapter 11's (dropping a gap is the most biased way to show it; "emptiness plus explanation" raised confidence) and the Do No Harm Guide's: "It is also important to acknowledge who is and is not included in our data and charts"; "Groups whose data are and are not collected or shown often reflects who society deems as most important or valuable"; "Just by calling out the absence and by doing it explicitly, it gives a prompt to the reader" (the guide quoting Lauren Klein); and the worked case of a chart that "displayed a message over the chart explaining how the lack of sufficient data would result in estimates that were too imprecise to be reliable" (Schwabish & Feng 2021). The guide never uses the word "asset". The vocabulary for the holes already exists in the Analysis Function's table shorthand — [x] not available, [z] not applicable, [c] confidential, [u] low reliability, [w] none recorded in survey, [low] a low figure but not a real zero — with its rule that "Whenever a table contains shorthand, you should mention it and explain what the shorthand means" and that "A zero or '0' should only be used when a data point is a true zero" (*Standard (Analysis Function)*; typography to be checked by eye). Chapter 4's six kinds of nothing map onto those symbols as a reading — a true zero to 0, not collected to [w] or [x], suppressed to [c], unknown to [u], not applicable to [z] — and a hole in the missed column is one of them, named. What the evidence does not support is the stronger claim that an unlabelled gap will be read as "less value"; that is a reading, and the chapter marks it so.

### No shared scale, and what it costs

Two unlike measures never share an axis. The standard is flat: "In general, we do not recommend using dual axis charts because they can be easily misinterpreted", "the way we display lines in relation to each other can manipulate the data story, even if it is not intentional", and "It is best practice to separate the datasets and present them with their own y-axis" (Analysis Function, *Data visualisation: charts* — a "do not recommend", not a prohibition). Few's argument is the reason: on a dual-scaled chart "we are still encouraging people to compare their magnitudes", and "Magnitude comparisons between values with different units of measure and scales are not appropriate" (Few 2008; practitioner). In the corpus of charts people reported as misleading, dual axes were the fourth commonest issue (Lo et al. 2022) — prevalence in complaints, not a measured effect on readers; no experiment on a two-measure dual-axis chart was reached. *(Standard + Reading.)*

The cost of the alternative is measured and the chapter states it. L'Yi, Jo and Seo's review of 127 papers with 15 user studies found that "chart-wise juxtaposition has almost never outperformed any other layouts in terms of accuracy, completion time, and precision" for local comparison, and their guideline is "Avoid Chart-wise Juxtaposition for Local Comparison" (2020). Ondov and colleagues (four experiments, 189 retained of 200) "observe high levels of performance for overlaid versus standard small multiples" and improvements for mirror-symmetric arrangements (2019). Both test like-with-like data on one scale; neither tests unlike units. *(Evidence.)* So: for two measures in the same unit — this year against last, this place against its peers on one condition — overlay or mirror, and the comparison is precise; for the two columns, separate panels, and the comparison is slower and runs on memory. That is the price of not asserting a commensurability nobody has established, and the bridging sentence is what the eye cannot do across the gap.

### Equal visual dignity, untested

Version 1's rule that both columns are set with the same craft — the missed column is never the sketchy one — has no experiment behind it and this chapter found none. The nearest is Wood and colleagues' sketchy rendering (2012): "relative area judgment is compromised by sketchy rendering", and "where a visualization is clearly sketchy, engagement may be increased and that attitudes to participating in visualization annotation are more positive" — rougher rendering cost accuracy and invited critique, and the study did not measure belief. The Decision Book's own rule is the firmer ground: "Allocate space by rank, not by what fits — Space is read as importance whether or not you intended it" (LAY-08). *(Reading; Decision Book.)* The rule stands as a stance: the missed column gets the same type scale, the same chart quality and the same space, because space and finish are read as importance and the house does not intend to say the missed column matters less. Two mock pages, one with the columns set equally and one with the missed column rougher, read by the three user groups, would be the first test.

A worked example of the opposite is official. The Community Needs Index measures "the presence of key community, civic, educational and cultural assets in close proximity of the area" and "the levels of third sector civic and community activity", and reports them so that "In each case, a higher score indicates higher levels of community need" (OCSI 2019). Assets are counted and published as need. It is the counted column absorbing the other, and it shows how easily a measure built to balance the deficit account becomes part of it.

### The bridging sentence

Version 1 asked for a sentence that relates the two columns without a ratio. Nothing in the official writing guidance speaks to it: the Analysis Function's quality-and-uncertainty guidance has no rule on relating two unlike measures, and the ONS's writing guidance asks for "one trend or point per bullet" and text that "should not repeat the trends shown in" the charts. The only model the chapter found is the ONS's local statistics service, which turns a comparison into words by a rule: the text says "higher" if the area "is 1 or more median absolute deviations above the comparison value" and "similar" if "within 1 median absolute deviations of each other", because "This text helps distinguish between small differences that might be because of uncertainty in the statistics" (ELS QMI). That is a sentence generated from a threshold, Chapter 11's method with Chapter 7's statistics inside it. The bridging sentence for two columns has no threshold to generate from; it is written, within the poster rule, and it says one thing from each column and no relation between them that the data do not hold.

## 6 · Identity is never ranked; conditions are compared with a gate

### A stance, stated at its true strength

Version 1 said identity axes "are nominal", so ranking them is "a category error". The measurement literature does not let the claim stand as a fact about data. Stevens's 1946 typology — nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio, each with its permissible statistics — ties scale type to "the basic empirical operations performed" and offers a "pragmatic sanction" for averaging ordinal scores; Velleman and Wilkinson's critique of it is the chapter's authority. Their rendering of Stevens: "analyses on nominal data, for example, should be limited to summary statistics such as the number of cases, the mode, and contingency correlation, which require only that the identity of the values be preserved". Their objection: "Scale type, as defined by Stevens, is not an attribute of the data, but rather depends upon the questions we intend to ask of the data and upon any additional information we may have"; "It is relatively easy to construct situations in which the scale type of data depends on its interpretation or on what additional information is available"; and "the use of Stevens's categories in selecting or recommending statistical analysis methods is inappropriate and can often be wrong". Their concessions: "The concept of scale type is an important one, and Stevens's terminology is often suitable"; "Considering whether scale types are plausible subsequent to the analysis can help ferret out nonsense". And the representational counter-position they quote: the restriction is binding where the numbers "have been assigned in a representational way so as to preserve the empirical relational structure" (Hand & Keynes, via Velleman & Wilkinson). *(Evidence of method; the field's standing critique.)*

The chapter's conclusion is a correction to version 1 and a stronger rule for it. A place's genre mix, the shape of its week, who its audiences are: these are nominal because of the question the house asks of them — "what is this place like?" — and would become ordinal under another question — "which place has the most of X?" — that someone else will ask of the same data. Velleman and Wilkinson's argument is against letting scale type *prescribe* an analysis, and the chapter does not use it that way: the house's rule is a decision about which question it publishes answers to, not a claim that the data forbid the other. "Identity is never ranked" is therefore Fixed (requirement) in the register, held as the house's stance, with the measurement literature supplying the reason it can only be a stance: the house has decided what question its profile answers, and it says so, because the data will not say it for them. The practical consequence is that the profile's fields are chosen and published so that the ordinal question is hard to ask of them (a mix, not a count; a shape, not a size), and that the house's data contract records which fields are identity and which are conditions, so that the line survives the next analyst.

### What form an identity profile takes

Version 1 called the forms "profiles, fingerprints, small multiples" and left them Decorative. The evidence narrows them. On radar charts: in the one head-to-head on composite indicators, comparing radar, flower and circle charts as small multiples under a task taxonomy, "the Radar chart was the least effective and least liked", with "strong preference of participants toward the Flower chart" (Albo, Lanir, Bak & Rafaeli 2016 †; sample not visible). Feldman's objection is geometric and goes to the heart of this section: "Filled radar charts are not appropriate for such comparisons because the size of the area within the polygon is not invariant to the placement of the measures on the axes", so that "altering the placement of the measures on the axes can result in reversing the order of observations" (2013 †) — a filled radar *is* a ranking, by area, and the ranking depends on an arbitrary choice. Waldner and colleagues, 92 crowd-workers: "the most elementary 24-hours linear bar chart is most accurate and efficient and is also preferred by the users", and "visualizations for the masses should rely on linear bar charts instead of radial rose charts" (2019; one time series, not profiles). On small multiples against overlay: "techniques that create separate charts for each time series - such as small multiples and horizon graphs - are generally more efficient for comparisons across time series with a large visual span", while shared-space charts win "over smaller visual spans" (Javed, McDonnel & Elmqvist 2010 †). On glyphs: the design guidelines ask that "equal distances in data space should be perceived equally" and that "it should be possible to perceive each visual cue independently", and the survey records that star glyphs are reordered automatically "for generating more symmetric and simple star glyphs" (Borgo et al. 2013) — which is Feldman's point again: the shape a reader compares depends on an axis order nobody chose for meaning. *(Evidence; forms tested for accuracy, time, memory and preference — none for whether a reader reads one profile as better.)* The reading: a profile is shown as small multiples of linear marks, with the axis order fixed and stated, and never as a filled area. The one composite-indicator study preferred a radial form, the flower chart, on preference; the rule prefers linear marks on the accuracy evidence and holds the form open. None of the studies measured whether a form reads as "better" rather than "different", and that is the prototype's question.

### Classification as identity, in official practice

The country already names places without ranking them, and has written down how. The 2011 Output Area Classification's authors, in a peer-reviewed account: "Given the predominant public sector usage that was anticipated in creating the 2011 OAC, words and phrases that might be construed as pejorative were not used, and all descriptors had strong and literal links to the underlying distributions revealed by the data"; "Words that were overtly negative or positive were avoided where possible, and the abiding sense of the descriptors was to present the characteristics of each cluster as consequences of factors that have happened to areas rather than the consequences of human agency"; names were checked against earlier classifications and "final approval of the cluster names was sought from ONS who conducted their own internal review and consultation"; and the standing caveat, "Names and descriptions of clusters are, of course, based on the characteristics of their centroids, and … specific areas differ in the degrees to which they conform to these average characteristics" (Gale, Singleton, Bates & Longley 2016). They also record, citing Vickers and colleagues, why it matters: "names and descriptions of clusters may be contentious, especially if they reinforce negative stereotypes"; and, in their own voice, that "the procedure of conflating individual variables with one another opens up the risk of ecological fallacy". The 2011 supergroups still include "Constrained City Dwellers" and "Hard-Pressed Living"; the 2021 groups, as the public search tool lists them, include "Challenged Multicultural Communities and Students", "Challenged Communities" and "Established but Challenged" (the 2021 supergroup names could not be verified at source and are not printed). The ONS's local authority classification carries the official hedge: "The following pen portraits (descriptions) and the names given to them are based on the average characteristics of each cluster", and "Given the subjective nature of producing names based on these average characteristics and their descriptive nature, there may be instances where individual local authorities are more or less closely associated with these descriptions". The word "rank" appears on none of these pages. *(Standard (official statistics); Evidence of method.)*

Three things to take. The naming rule — literal, not pejorative, neither negative nor positive, *what happened to areas rather than what they did* — is a rule for naming without blame, and the house adopts it for any label it gives a place or a type; it is not the identity/conditions line, and the classifier did not draw that line. The hedge — a name describes a centroid and a place conforms to it more or less — is the official wording for the per-place caveat an identity label needs. And the output shows the rule's limit: "Hard-Pressed" and "Challenged" are literal and are not neutral, because they name conditions, and a label for a condition cannot be neutral however carefully it is written. Which is why the house's labels name identity, and conditions are shown as conditions, with numbers, not folded into a type's name.

Elsewhere the pattern is less clean. Understanding Scottish Places types 508 towns by k-means and offers "comparisons on each indicator against other comparable or local towns"; its type labels include "Least Privileged towns" and "Affluent towns", its inter-relationships score is built from each town's position "split into sevenths depending on their position across the 508 towns", and the "no good or bad town type" sentence that circulates about it is not on its current pages. The OECD's regional well-being tool: "There is no single ranking associated to the regional well-being web tool" and "We do not make a single statement about the overall well-being in a region" — and, within a topic, "Regions are ranked according to their values in each topic", with "similar regions" chosen by distance on the same outcome scores, so that "similar" there means similar outcomes and there is no identity layer. The German CHE university ranking refuses positions altogether: it "assigns them to one of three ranking groups: a top group, a middle group or an end group", "no overall value is derived from weighted individual indicators", so that "a situation is avoided in which minor differences in the nominal value of an indicator are misinterpreted as differences in performance and quality" (practice). *(Practice; method and screen only.)* None of the tools reached states a separation of identity from conditions as a layout principle; version 1's finding that the split has no public precedent stands, and the nearest precedents are a classifier's naming rule and a ranking body's refusal of positions.

### The gate is a standard with official cousins

Version 1 inherited the house's comparability gate from Style Guide §12 — "Any chart comparing two or more places states whether the indicator comparability gate cleared, and which of the eight criteria required harmonisation … A comparison without this note is not publishable" — and called it an axiom. It is better than that: it is the house's instance of a quality dimension every statistical code carries. The European Statistics Code of Practice, Principle 14: "European Statistics are consistent internally, over time and comparable between regions and countries"; "Statistics are compiled on the basis of common standards with respect to scope, definitions, units and classifications in the different surveys and data sources". Regulation 223/2009 defines comparability as "the measurement of the impact of differences in applied statistical concepts, measurement tools and procedures where statistics are compared between geographical areas, sectoral domains or over time", and Eurostat's metadata standard asks a producer to "Describe any problems of comparability between countries or regions" with "an assessment (preferably quantitative) of the possible effect of each reported difference". The United Kingdom's Code of Practice 3.0 writes geography into the practice: "Use data that are coherent when aggregated, consistent over time and comparable across and within geographies at regional, national and local levels, where possible" (6.6); "Use recognised standards, classifications and definitions. Explain reasons for deviations from these standards and any related implications for use" (6.7); "Explain quality issues related to the methods, systems and processes, including the extent to which the statistics are representative and comparable across the UK and internationally" (7.4); "Provide comparisons to support interpretation and signpost other relevant statistics … Explain the consistency and coherence with other related statistics and sources" (9.3). *(Standard (law); Standard (regulator).)* None sets a threshold for when a comparison may be shown; all require the producer to say how far it holds. The house's eight criteria are that saying, made a checklist, and the gate on the face is Code 3.0's practice 7.4 applied to one chart.

The ONS's own place-comparison service is the gate in operation. "The service strives to provide a UK-wide picture, allowing valid comparisons between local areas"; where releases are "not methodologically coherent, or where data are provided for different time periods", "The service presents these data as separate indicators to try to avoid inappropriate comparisons"; "We always check the coherence and comparability between indicators and agree this with data owners"; the chart is "an interactive beeswarm chart, which shows individual data points spread out to avoid overlapping" with the median marked; and the comparison is worded by the one-median-absolute-deviation rule. *(Practice; official.)* That is Chapter 7's position-in-spread, with the gate applied upstream and the words generated downstream — the fullest official precedent this book has found for comparing places without a league table, and the chapter's model for the conditions panel.

Where conditions cannot be ordered — a place ahead on space and behind on funding — version 1 asked for incomparability to be rendered as such, from the partial-order literature. That literature says what it offers and what it costs: evaluation "in terms of multidimensional comparisons among achievement profiles, rather than through attribute score aggregations", which "makes it unnecessary to scale ordinal attributes into numerical variables" (Fattore 2016 †); and the practitioners' own admission that "there are many practical cases where the interpretation of the partial order can be rather troublesome" (Annoni & Brüggemann 2009 †). No usability study of a dominance display with lay readers was found. The display form that needs no new literacy is the one the ONS already uses: each condition on its own spread, the place marked on each, and a sentence that says "ahead on this, behind on that, and the two do not add".

### The advocacy sentence

Version 1's sentence — of the form "[this place]'s scene runs at [that place]-level intensity on [a third place]-level support", with three London boroughs standing in as illustration — was offered as the ask a rank cannot deliver. The sentence survives with three corrections from this chapter. It does not join identity to conditions, as version 1 said: *intensity* is Chapter 6's family of counted activity measures, neither identity nor condition in this chapter's sense, and its first half is a comparison of a counted measure across places, so it passes through the gate like any other. Its second half names a peer by a published method or not at all (Chapter 7). And Fraser's two hazards are its two failure modes: without the second half it displaces (a portrait and no ask); without the first it has nothing a scene recognises as its own. It is written to the poster rule after the panels exist.

## 7 · Discovery as matching, not ranking

Version 1's Chapter 7 built a discovery layer — the reader who wants a place that sounds like them; the funder who wants the under-backed scene — on recommender precedents it had read through summaries. This chapter re-reads them at source, and the shape holds while several of the numbers move.

### Explanations, and what they buy

The founding study (Herlocker, Konstan & Riedl 2000) presented "21 individual movie recommendations" with different explanations to "78 users", who rated how likely they would be to go and see each film — a measure of persuasion, not of decision quality. The grouped histogram of neighbours' ratings scored highest (5.25 against base cases of 4.53 and 4.51), because "it reduced the dimensionality of the data to a point where only one binary comparison is necessary"; some explanations "had significantly lower mean response than the base cases", and "Poorly designed explanations can actually decrease the effectiveness of a recommender system". In the second experiment, "210 users participated", and on decision performance "There was no statistically significant difference between any two experimental groups". Version 1's "86% of users wanted explanations kept" is "86% of these users said that they would like to see their explanation interface added to the system", where "these users" are the "97 experimental subjects" who filled out the exit survey in the non-control groups, asked about the interface they had just used. *(Evidence; corrected.)* So: explanations raise acceptance and liking; the founding study found no gain in decisions; and bad explanations do worse than none.

Which explanations help a person judge is a separate finding. Bilgic and Mooney, with 34 subjects and 53 recommendations: the neighbour-style explanation ("people like you rated this") "causes the user to overestimate the actual-rating by 0.74 on average", while keyword-style (the item's content) and influence-style (the user's own past ratings) explanations "were found to be significantly more effective at enabling accurate assessments"; their distinction is between "the promotion approach", whose best explanation "is the one that is most successful at convincing the user to adopt an item", and "the satisfaction approach", whose best "is the one that lets the users assess the quality of the item the best". Tintarev and Masthoff's seven aims give the vocabulary — transparency, scrutability, trust, effectiveness ("Help users make good decisions"), persuasiveness ("Convince users to try or buy"), efficiency, satisfaction — with the warning that "in reality it is a trade-off" (2007); and their later finding, about tailoring the explanation's wording to the person, "Contrary to expectation, personalization was detrimental to effectiveness, though it may improve user satisfaction" (2012 †) — the match is personal; the explanation is content. The systematic review of 217 studies found that "less than a quarter (21.5%) of the studies involving techniques and tools contains any form of evaluation" and "no clear consensus on what constitutes a good explanation" (Nunes & Jannach 2017). *(Evidence.)* Version 1's rule — every match shows its why as concrete cues, built and tested for effectiveness not persuasion — is confirmed and now has its mechanism: content cues let a reader judge; social-proof cues inflate.

### Exposure, popularity and "the biggest"

The brief's eighth failure — "an aggregation of the biggest" — has a technical name in this literature. Singh and Joachims: "ranking systems have a responsibility not only to their users but also to the items being ranked"; "the scarce resource that ranking systems allocate is the exposure of items to users", "and exposure is largely determined by position in the ranking"; in their worked example, a relevance gap of 0.03 between two groups becomes "30% less exposure" for one of them under a standard position drop-off (2018). Popularity bias is its mechanism: in a standard test set, "recommendations the users get are extremely concentrated on popular items even if a user is interested in long-tail", and "the niche users are seeing the largest discrepancy" (Abdollahpouri et al. 2019); the field's monograph treats "rich-get-richer" dynamics as "a type of fairness problem" and names the principle, "The system should not inordinately favor popular, well-known, and possibly well-funded content creators" (Ekstrand et al. 2022). The cleanest experiment is older and about ranked displays as such: 14,341 participants in an artificial market of 48 songs, in an independent condition and eight "worlds" with social information; when the display moved from a grid with download counts to "one column in descending order of current popularity", "inequality increased when the salience of the social information signal was increased", and "Increasing the strength of social influence increased both inequality and unpredictability of success" — "The best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well, but any other result was possible" (Salganik, Dodds & Watts 2006; read for method about ranked displays, never for anything about music). A model of recommenders found they "can create a rich-get-richer effect for popular products", and that "it is possible for individual-level diversity to increase but aggregate diversity to decrease" (Fleder & Hosanagar 2009 †). *(Evidence; strong.)* The reading: a sorted list of places by any count is itself a treatment on the places, it concentrates attention on the already-large, and it does so more the more prominently it is sorted. Chapter 7's reactivity (Espeland & Sauder) is the institutional version; this is the display version. A discovery layer that sorts by popularity, footfall or size reproduces the aggregation the brief rejects, and the rule follows: no ordering the house makes in discovery is by size or popularity; an ordering made for a reader from their own weights is theirs, shows the weights, and exists only when they ask for it — the tools below keep their comparisons behind a reader's choice rather than on a landing page.

### Narrowing, novelty and the null

Version 1 refused to rest its anti-narrowing rule on the filter bubble, and the literature confirms the refusal. The Reuters Institute's review: algorithmic selection "generally lead to slightly more diverse news use – the opposite of what the 'filter bubble' hypothesis posits"; "We are not aware of any comparable studies that have found support for the filter bubble hypothesis"; what exists is "self-selection, primarily among a small minority of highly partisan individuals" (Arguedas et al. 2022). Zuiderveen Borgesius and colleagues: "at present, there is no empirical evidence that warrants any strong worries about filter bubbles" (2016). Bruns: the concept "remains vague and founded in anecdotes" (2019). *(Evidence; news, not place or music; the transfer is unknown.)* What the recommender experiments add is a warning in the other direction: with 582 users over 81 days, "satisfaction is negatively dependent on novelty and positively dependent on diversity", and "Too many unfamiliar recommendations may give users a poor impression of a particular recommender" (Ekstrand et al. 2014). Serendipity has no agreed definition — "serendipitous items are relevant, novel and unexpected"; "There is no consensus on definition"; "no agreement on the evaluation metric" (Kotkov 2018, dissertation) — and in one user study 302 of 2,146 rated items (14 per cent) were judged serendipitous under at least one definition — the "up to 8.5%" sometimes quoted is an extrapolation to a whole ratings database. *(Evidence.)* So version 1's adjacency row — "close to your taste, new to you" — stands as a design on first principles, with the measured caution that too much new reads as a poor match, and it is a probe, not a rule.

### The matching tools, at their screens

Version 1 took its "matching not ranking" rule from Teleport, which is defunct; what its surviving page says is "tell us what qualities of life matter to you the most" and "Results of your search are sorted by how well they match your criteria" — a per-user match ordering; the decomposed score and the absence of a global rank could not be verified. Discover Uni, the official course-comparison service, lets a reader "choose up to seven to view side by side", warns "It's important that you don't make decisions based on small differences between courses", says of its outcomes data "These students may or may not be similar to you" and "a useful part of the picture, but not the whole", and of league tables that "some may weight the things that are important to you more highly than others" — and nowhere on the pages read says it is not a league table; its no-ranking is by construction, a user-chosen set with no global ordering, not by declaration. The ONS's local statistics service builds "an ordered list of the 20 most similar areas to the area of interest, ordered by Euclidean distance", for "enquiring citizens to explore which areas are similar to their own", and the only ordered list on it is nearness to the reader's own place. Numbeo is the counter-case: "an empirical formula that assigns weights to each factor based on its importance", fixed weights, a global ordering, and "Our formulas have changed in the past". *(Practice; screens and methods only.)* The pattern the careful tools share, stated once: the only ordering shown is the reader's own — nearness to their place, or fit to their weights — and the maker publishes no ordering of its own.

### The money view, and the official method for absence

Version 1's money view — underinvestment shown as visible absence, the map of money over the map of scenes — rested on 360Giving's guidance to funders: "Find gaps, overlaps or chances to work with others"; "Spot funders working on the same issues or in the same places" (practice). What this chapter adds is the official method that already does this for culture, and what it shows about such methods. Arts Council England's *Methodology for identifying priority places* (2021) scored need from seven indicators with weights — its own investment per head and arts engagement at 1.5, deprivation, a vulnerability index, a children's composite, ethnicity and disability at 1 — standardised within each of its five areas, so that "The same number in the raw data could translate into a different need score in different areas" and "priority places have been selected by comparison with other places in their area, not other places nationally"; then scored opportunity by staff judgement, "Scores were arrived at by area teams giving each place a score between 1 and 5" on ambition, capacity and timing; then "Need and opportunity scores were not designed to be added together"; then a balancing step across each area's portfolio, where "Medium need places could only be selected as a priority place if their selection was critical to address one or more of the balancing criteria"; "up to 15 priority places" per area, 54 in all. The phrase "cold spots" does not appear in it. *(Standard (method), official; fully published.)* Two lessons. An "absence made visible" method is a composite (Chapter 5's rules apply: weights are judgements, the recipe goes on the face) with a judgement step inside it, and the published method does not make a place's selection reproducible from the data alone — which is a legitimate design, stated. And 360Giving's gap is a gap in *published* grants: no caveat on its pages says so, and the house's money view must, because a dark patch on a map of money is also a map of who has not published.

### Discoverability under Chapter 10

Version 1 made discoverability "opt-in at scene level, granular, revocable", on the precedent of opt-in directories. Chapter 10 retired "consent to be found" and "consent at scene level": a community cannot consent, it is consulted, governs and agrees; and "known but not shown" is a declared status for a category, never a marker on a unit. This chapter restates the discovery rules under that: whether a kind of place is findable at all in the discovery layer is a status of the category, set with the forum Chapter 10 describes, recorded in the safeguards record, and never a flag beside a named room. What the opt-in directories teach, read for mechanism only, is narrower than version 1 said: on each of the three reached, the listed party applies ("We do ask that the person filling out the application is the owner of the organization"; "Submit your space for verification and approval"; a signed pledge, after which the business is added to a map), and none publishes a safety rationale for the rule on the pages read. Informal and unlisted activity, under the same chapter, appears in a category's aggregate character and never with coordinates unless the category's status says otherwise — version 1's "default invisibility" restated as Chapter 10's held status. *(Practice; mechanism only.)* Self-listing is a rule those directories set for themselves; it is a precedent for a category's rule, not evidence that it is the right one. Form 696, the Metropolitan Police's former event risk-assessment form, which asked promoters to describe who would attend, enters here as the studio's frame and as version 1's test, kept: if an enforcement body opened the discovery layer tomorrow, what could it do that it cannot do today? The answer the house wants is nothing the category's status did not already allow — and the test is run with Chapter 10's walk, against public lists, before any category is made findable.

## 8 · The crop: what a cut-out carries

Version 1's crop test — no view survives cropping as a ranking; whatever is cut out carries its provenance — was argued from the Decision Book's breakout rule. This chapter measures the crop.

### What is actually in circulation

Lisnic and colleagues collected "2.2 billion tweets" and coded "9,958" posts carrying visualisations about the pandemic, of every stance (about three-fifths of the opinion posts opposed public-health measures). "only 12% of all posts contain data visualizations that violate common visualization design guidelines"; "the majority (55.3%)" of the charts were "screenshots of existing charts"; and, of the sceptics' charts alone, "more than 40% of all COVID-skeptic charts in the COVID-19 discourse on Twitter are screenshots of data exploration dashboards". The errors were mostly in reasoning and context: causal inference in 6.9 per cent of posts, cherry-picking in 5.2, an arbitrary threshold in 4.5; the commonest design fault, dual axes, at 5.4 sat beside them, and truncated axes at 1.2; "even faithfully plotted data visualizations can be used to support misinformation"; and of one chart's caveat, "This note is not a prominent feature of the chart and is not reflected in the visual design of the case curve". Their design conclusion: "the designer should also anticipate how a biased viewer may use and misuse the chart" (2023). Lee and colleagues, from "close to half a million tweets" and "over 41,000 images" with six months in five groups, found that "anti-mask groups practice a form of data literacy in spades", that "unorthodox viewpoints do not result from a deficiency of data literacy", and that "users take screenshots of the websites and debate whether or not they can be attributed to the coronavirus" (2021). Lo and colleagues' taxonomy of 1,143 reported-misleading images found "74 different types of issues" in "a taxonomy of 12 categories", with "Truncated Axis (1st), 3D (2nd)" the most discussed; it contains "Missing Title" and "Selective Data" and has no issue for cropping or a missing source (2022). *(Evidence; large corpora; one domain.)*

The reading for this chapter: the population of charts that travel is mostly screenshots of someone else's chart, often of a dashboard; what goes wrong in them is more often what is left out — the context, the baseline, the base — than what is drawn, though the drawing faults are there too; and a dashboard is a screenshot machine. Two halves follow, and provenance cures only one. The missing-context half — no base, no unit, no date, no gate — is what a frame can carry. The misreasoning half — a causal story told over a faithful chart — no caption cures, and the chapter does not claim it does. Version 1's layout rule (no ordered all-places list on any screen) stands as a stance, because a sorted list is the thing most easily cropped into a verdict; the measured problem is wider than ranking.

### What the official guidance does, and says it does

The Analysis Function: "All charts need at least one title, but it is considered best practice to give them two"; "The statistical subtitle should say what the data is, the geography the data relates to and the time period shown"; and — the sentence that decides the matter — "titles should be in the body of the page or document, as opposed to within the image", "because images of text are difficult to read for some people", with the consequence named: "This does mean the image of the chart can be taken out of context if it is copied and pasted elsewhere", and the next sentences, that putting the title in the image "does not stop it completely as titles can be cropped out. Therefore, we prioritise accessibility and advise for the title and subtitle to be in the body text." The source likewise: "this text should be in the body text of the page and not part of the chart image". GOV.UK: "Avoid using images with text"; "Do not use the image to convey information without also explaining it in the text of your content". The ONS: "Each chart must include the source of the data directly after the chart" — after, not in. *(Standard (Analysis Function); Standard (design system).)* So there is no official minimum for what a shared image carries, and the official position is that the image carries nothing, for a reason the house shares. The trade-off is the Analysis Function's own: accessibility ahead of resistance to decontextualisation.

Our World in Data is the production precedent and it cuts both ways. Its embed code "will adapt to the selections that you make on the chart", with a choice of "a live embed that always reflects our latest data updates" or "an archived embed that will never change or update"; its chart pages carry a source line and a licence; its citation blocks give a retrieval date and an archive address (Chapter 11). And its image API offers an "uncaptioned" preset — the publisher issues the stripped image itself. *(Practice.)*

### Designing for the crop

One study has designed for it. Lisnic, Cutler, Kogan and Lex's "visualization guardrails" "automatically show the contextual data that expose cherry-picking", with tamper-evidence as a design goal — "it should be difficult to get rid of the guardrail, for instance by cropping a screenshot" — and the finding that "Juxtaposed approaches are croppable and thus not tamper-evident", that the "Superimposed Primary Data guardrail was the most effective against cherry-picking" across three experiments (130, 160 and 675 participants), and the limit: "38% of participants did not notice them, and 23% noticed them but did not understand their meaning" (2025). *(Evidence; one domain.)* The guardrail is comparison data superimposed on a chart, not provenance text; the lesson the chapter takes from it is tamper-evidence, and that lesson is uncomfortable for a chapter that has just chosen juxtaposition for the two columns: whatever protects a figure must be *in* the figure's frame, not beside it, and most readers will not see it anyway. So each column's panel carries, inside its own frame, the bridging sentence that names the other column — a cropped counted panel still says what sat beside it — and the chapter records that this is a design against a measured failure, not a cure for it.

### What a cut-out must carry

Put together, the rule is a provenance rule for the missing-context half, with a layout consequence. The Decision Book already requires that "Breakout statistics carry a mandatory source line" (DAT-18), "a minimum base" (DAT-21), and that "The source travels with the figure, not with the document — Figures get forwarded and screenshotted alone" (TBL-17). Chapter 11 requires the address and the snapshot date in a generated citation. This chapter adds the comparison's furniture and the unit: every chart carries, inside its frame, title, unit, source, base, address and snapshot date, and a chart that compares places carries its gate status and its wording ("higher", "similar") too; each of the two columns' panels carries the bridging sentence. Where the house departs from the official practice — text inside the image — it does so knowingly, against the reason the Analysis Function states, and it does so *in addition to* the page text, never instead, so that the accessible route is kept; Chapter 13 tests the departure against the criteria. *(Reading; Fixed (requirement), with the accessible route Fixed (sequence).)* And the official wording standards for rank travel with any rank the house ever lets out: the Indices of Deprivation license "most deprived" and "highly deprived" but insist that "there is no definitive threshold above which an area is described as 'deprived'", that the measure "can tell you if one area is more deprived than another but not by how much", that "a small area with a rank of 1,000 is not half as deprived as a place with a rank of 500", and that "Many non-deprived people live in deprived areas, and many deprived people live in non-deprived areas"; the Scottish index publishes a "Do not use SIMD for" list — "Saying how much more deprived one area is from another", "Identifying affluent areas", comparing ranks over time; and the ONS's well-being guidance says that "simply ranking local authorities by their numerical scores can be misleading for several reasons, including sample sizes and mode effects", preferring "progress over time within the same local authority, or across local authorities that share a similar demographic composition". *(Standard (official statistics).)* Chapter 7's rules restated by the bodies that publish ranks: a rank is ordinal, relative, says nothing about distance, and is read best against self and peers.

## 9 · Harm through representation, at the scale of a place

Chapter 10 settled that harm runs through representation — label, findability, price — and is assessed for persons, groups and places. This section reads what is measured about the harm to a place, so that the rule carries its measured size.

The theory is Wacquant's: "Whether or not these areas are in fact dilapidated and dangerous, and their population composed essentially of poor people, minorities and foreigners, matters little in the end: the prejudicial belief that they are suffices to set off socially noxious consequences"; "A blemish of place is thus superimposed on the already existing stigmata traditionally associated with poverty and ethnic origin"; "People there commonly hide their address" (2007). The later statement calls it "a consequential and injurious form of action through collective representation fastened on place" (2014 †). No measurement instrument is offered. The measurement literature is thinner than the theory. Perceived reputation is defined as "how they think other city residents see their neighbourhood", and "Objective neighbourhood variables are more important in explaining perception of reputation than in explaining neighbourhood satisfaction" (Permentier, Bolt & van Ham 2011 †). Slater's "sink estate" is a category "invented by journalists, amplified by free market think tanks and converted into policy doxa (common sense) by politicians" (2018 †) — a label doing policy work. And the one causal test in the United Kingdom found less than the theory predicts: 2,001 applications to 667 real jobs in the low- and medium-skilled market from fictional candidates at addresses with poor and bland reputations; a positive response to 17 per cent of applications; among the 192 jobs with any positive response, the bland-reputation candidate was favoured 62.5 to 59.9 per cent, a net preference of 2.6 percentage points, p = "0.1170", "small and it was not statistically significant", with the authors' caveat that "in a larger correspondence test study, the net preferences found would achieve statistical significance" (Tunstall et al. 2014). *(Evidence; the one field experiment; first-stage selection only.)*

The reading, at its measured size: a place's reputation is real, is partly explained by its measured conditions, is made by labels and coverage as much as by facts, and in the one experiment that tested a concrete penalty, the penalty was small and uncertain. The rule is therefore proportion, as Chapter 10 required: a dashboard that publishes a place's conditions is one voice in a reputation, its labels are the part it controls, and the labels are where the discipline goes.

### The label review

Three official label debates give the discipline its content. The 2011 OAC's rule (section 6): literal, not pejorative, neither negative nor positive, what happened to areas rather than what they did. The "left behind" report's boxed admission: "The term 'left behind' is controversial and contentious. It has been argued that it is patronising"; "We do not use it to imply that the areas so described lack people with skills and commitment or a rich heritage"; "It is also a term that people seem to instinctively understand", kept "in the absence of better" — and its caveat on unit, "ward-level data can mask variations in need at the very local level" (Local Trust/OCSI 2019). The deprivation indices' refusal of a threshold for "deprived" beside their licence to say "most deprived". *(Standard (official statistics); Practice.)* The house's procedure, assembled from these: every label a place-page gives a place or a type is written by the 2011 OAC's rule, carries the ONS's per-place hedge, is checked against the voice rule ("Defining people or places only through failure or need"), and is shown to the people it names before it is published — which is section 1's third sense, and the next subsection's procedure.

### Member checking when the members are a scene

Chapter 9 placed recognition by the described as member checking and left the procedure's form for this chapter. The literature's definition already allows for a group: member checks are "when data, analytic categories, interpretations and conclusions are tested with members of those groups from whom the data were originally obtained" (Qualitative Research Guidelines Project, paraphrasing Lincoln & Guba; the page-314 attribution circulates without a page number and the book was not reached). What was reached is a literature of procedures with no evaluation and one researcher's account of harm done. Candela returned a written analysis to three teachers, found it "not flattering", and wrote "I may have caused harm to Mrs. O'Neill as I was sending back a negative analysis of her situation", proposing that "member checking should be used as a reflective space for participants" (2019). Carlson's choices of form: "hard copies, electronic copies, listening to the audiotapes with the researcher, or having the researcher read the transcripts aloud" (2010). The Urban Institute's Data Walks put findings on posters at "five stations at about six minutes per station" for groups of "no more than four or five people", rotate on a chime, "leave titles at the door", and end with a share-out — a guide, not an evaluated method, whose authors "feared that sharing too many bleak statistics around risky youth behaviors would only reinforce negative messages and stereotypes" (Murray, Falkenburger & Saxena 2015). Community-based participatory research's seventh principle: findings are produced, interpreted and disseminated "to community members in clear language respectful to the community", with partners "as reviewers and co-authors of publications" (Detroit URC 2011). Participatory mapping's ethics: "The maps are never final or static"; "they have to be crosschecked, improved, and updated"; "the original physical output of a participatory mapping exercise stays with those who generated it", "with a trusted entity nominated by the informants"; "Who owns the output?" (Rambaldi et al. 2006). The Urban Institute's equitable-data principles: "Seek out and incorporate communities' interpretation of the data"; "Return data and research results to community members in a form they can use"; "Account for how publication may reinforce inequities or close disparities" (Gaddy & Scott 2020). *(Practice; Standard (community); none evaluated.)*

The procedure the chapter assembles, as a procedure and not a finding: before a place-page is published, the page — its unit, its labels, its two columns, its sentence — is shown to people from the scene it describes, in the room, at Chapter 10's forum or a convened session, in a form chosen with them; what they say about the unit, the labels and what is missing is recorded by role and count, never by name (Chapter 10); what changed is recorded (Chapter 9's "what this changed"); the record goes beneath the page; and the page says it was reviewed and when. Who counts as the scene's members, when a scene is by definition not a stable population, is the forum's question and not this chapter's. The one account of harm is the one to design against: a page that returns an unflattering portrait to the people in it, without the room to answer, is Candela's letter at the scale of a scene. Where the page carries a condition a scene will not like, the review is where it is first seen, not the launch. Whether being shown one's page in the room reads as respect or as surveillance is Chapter 9's open question and remains open. The MSOA names project is the standing form — publish, invite correction, keep an errors channel — and a place-page keeps one.

## 10 · The failures, decomposed

"It fails if they do not recognise their place" is, by the sections above, five failures with five tests. The tests are read-aloud tests run in a room with the three user groups and a holder of the harm frame; they test legibility and standing, which is what this chapter is about, and not decision quality, which is Chapter 2's and the prototype's. Each is run with at least a handful of readers from each group, and a page passes a test when most of them pass it; the numbers are the studio's to set.

*Wrong unit.* The reader cannot find where they live, work or play because the page's unit is not theirs. Test: a reader from the scene names the place in their words and finds it by that name within the three-phones time (Chapter 11), and then says whether the unit named beneath the figures is the place they meant. Remedy: the unit declared, the administrative unit beside the scene scale, the vague place shown as a core and bands, the reader able to draw their own.

*Not in it.* The reader finds the place and nothing of their people. Test: Peck's question — does the reader find home, and what do they say next? — with a pass when what they say next is about the page's content rather than its omissions. Remedy: the missed column present at full size, with its holes named.

*The account is wrong.* The described do not agree. Test: the member check, recorded. Remedy: the review before publication, the errors channel after.

*The counted won.* The page reads as the harm frame with a reply. Test: version 1's, kept and made specific — a holder of the harm frame states the page's claim in the form of Chapter 11's three (sentence, figure, next step), and the claim they state is the missed column's; a scene member says what is there and what is missing from their own knowledge of the place — knowledge they bring, not a gap they are asked to detect, which readers do badly. Remedy: value first as endorsement; equal space and finish; separate scales; the sentence.

*You lost.* The page is a verdict: a rank, a type with a verdict in its name, a comparison that reads as a league table in a screenshot. Test: version 1's losers' test — a reader from a place whose counted column is thin opens its own page and is asked what it says about the place — and the crop test run on every panel. Remedy: identity never ranked; conditions on their spreads with the gate; labels by the classifier's rule; provenance inside the frame.

## 11 · What holds true regardless

Seven truths, each with its grounds, then the record beneath them. Stated for any public dashboard about places; the nightlife case in section 13 is one application.

**1 · A figure about a place is a figure about a unit, no official unit was designed around what residents recognise, and changing the unit can change the finding — so the unit is a declared choice, stated on the face with what it hides, and a page about a place offers more than one.** Output areas "were designed to have similar population sizes and be as socially homogenous as possible"; "LSOAs are also referred to as 'neighbourhoods' throughout this release"; a median drawn neighbourhood of 0.35 square miles against a target area of 2.23; "the MAUP does make a difference in about 60 per cent of cases"; "it remains difficult to predict these occasions"; the ONS lets a reader "simply select an area on a map and get the data directly". *(Section 3; ONS; MHCLG; Coulton, Chan & Mikelbank; Flowerdew.)*

**2 · Readers look for a named place first, and a place with a name and no edge is shown as a core and bands — of one person's confidence, or of agreement across people — with the number of people behind them.** "based on how easy or difficult it was to find their home state"; "decisions framed or driven by personal experience"; "every individual area's page had been read"; 33 of 36 drew a 50 per cent region larger than their 100 per cent region; 25, 50 and 75 per cent contours "among the self-selected people who responded". No controlled study was found. *(Sections 3, 4; Peck, Ayuso & El-Etr; ONS; Montello et al.; Bostonography.)*

**3 · The counted column and the missed column sit side by side, the missed column first as a declared endorsement and never alone, each on its own scale, with equal space and finish, every hole named with its kind, the missed column's uncertainty shown, and each panel's frame naming the other — because a bar chart of a gap may encourage deficit readings, quantified uncertainty costs little or no trust, and two unlike measures on one axis invite a comparison the data do not hold; the old reason for the order, that repeating the opposing frame strengthens it, is withdrawn, and the order rests on endorsement alone.** "exhaustive experimental attempts to induce a backfire effect through familiarity alone have come up empty"; "framing effects depend more heavily on the qualities of frames than on their frequency of dissemination"; "Hiding variability tends to increase stereotyping while emphasizing variability reduces it"; "only positive or null effects, not negative effects" (†); "In general, we do not recommend using dual axis charts"; "Space is read as importance whether or not you intended it". *(Section 5; Lewandowsky et al.; Swire-Thompson et al.; Chong & Druckman; Holder & Xiong; Gustafson & Rice †; Analysis Function; Decision Book LAY-08.)*

**4 · What a place *is* is never ranked, as a stance the house declares, because scale type follows the question asked and the house has decided its question; the profile is shown as small multiples of linear marks with a fixed, stated axis order and never as a filled area, the form held open to test; and any label for a place or a type is written without blame — literal, not pejorative, neither negative nor positive, naming what happened to an area rather than what it did — and carries the per-place hedge, while conditions are shown as numbers and never folded into a name.** "Scale type … depends upon the questions we intend to ask of the data"; "the Radar chart was the least effective and least liked"; "not invariant to the placement of the measures on the axes"; "words and phrases that might be construed as pejorative were not used"; "consequences of factors that have happened to areas rather than the consequences of human agency"; "more or less closely associated with these descriptions". *(Section 6; Velleman & Wilkinson; Albo et al. †; Feldman †; Gale et al.; ONS.)*

**5 · What a place has is compared, each condition on its own spread with the place marked, against itself over time and against peers chosen by a published method, with the comparability gate and its wording inside the frame — because comparability is a quality dimension every statistical code requires a producer to state, and the official place-comparison service already does it this way.** "comparable across and within geographies at regional, national and local levels, where possible"; "Explain reasons for deviations from these standards"; "The service presents these data as separate indicators to try to avoid inappropriate comparisons"; "higher" at "1 or more median absolute deviations"; "A comparison without this note is not publishable". *(Section 6; Code of Practice 3.0; ESS Code Principle 14; ELS QMI; Style Guide §12.)*

**6 · Finding a place that suits you is matching: the only ordering shown is the reader's own, made on request from their weights or their nearness and shown with the why as content cues, and the house makes no ordering by size or popularity — because a sorted list is itself a treatment on what it sorts, concentrates exposure on the already-large, and social-proof explanations inflate while content cues let a reader judge; and whether a kind of place is findable at all is a status of the category, consulted, governed and agreed.** "exposure is largely determined by position in the ranking"; "30% less exposure"; "increased both inequality and unpredictability of success"; "overestimate the actual-rating by 0.74 on average"; "personalization was detrimental to effectiveness"; "There was no statistically significant difference" in decisions; "an ordered list of the 20 most similar areas to the area of interest". *(Section 7; Singh & Joachims; Salganik, Dodds & Watts; Bilgic & Mooney; Tintarev & Masthoff †; Herlocker et al.; ONS; Chapter 10.)*

**7 · Whatever is cut out of a view carries, inside the frame, its title, unit, source, base, address and snapshot date, for a comparison its gate and its wording, and for either column the sentence that names the other — because most charts in circulation are screenshots, more of what goes wrong in them is missing context than bad drawing, official guidance keeps title and source outside the image on purpose, and juxtaposed context is croppable; the misreasoning that no caption cures is named and not claimed.** "the majority (55.3%)" screenshots; "only 12%" design violations; "This does mean the image of the chart can be taken out of context if it is copied and pasted elsewhere"; "Juxtaposed approaches are croppable and thus not tamper-evident"; "38% of participants did not notice them"; "The source travels with the figure, not with the document". *(Section 8; Lisnic et al. 2023, 2025; Analysis Function; Decision Book TBL-17.)*

## 12 · Where the choice is yours

*Which unit the page lands on.* The scene scale is the house's declared choice; the chapter asks only that the choice be declared, the administrative unit shown beside it, and the cost (MAUP; comparability over time for settlement-shaped units) recorded.

*Whether to let readers draw their own unit.* The ONS does. It costs a disclosure check at query time (Chapter 10's walk) for every drawn area.

*How the vague place is drawn.* Core and bands; the thresholds (25/50/75 are one precedent) and the number of people behind them are the studio's.

*How the two columns are composed.* Side by side is the logical relation, not necessarily the layout; any composition that keeps the scales separate, the space and finish equal, and the missed column first is open. The holes' form — hatched, ghosted, a full-size empty vessel — is Decorative, provided the kind is named.

*Which identity fields the profile has, and in what order.* The order is fixed and stated; which fields, and what question the profile answers, are the house's, and the data contract records them.

*What the house's own type labels are.* Written by the classifier's rule; whether the house classifies places at all is a choice this chapter does not make.

*Which peer method.* Chapter 7's open choice, unchanged.

*How much of the discovery layer exists.* Matching with content cues, or no discovery at all; whether the money view is built; and which categories are findable, with the forum.

*How provenance sits inside the frame.* The departure from official practice is the chapter's rule; its form — a footer strip, a margin block, a stamp — and how much of it is legible at phone size are open, within Chapter 13's criteria.

*The review's form.* The room, the printed sheet, the data walk, the online draft with an errors channel; chosen with the scene.

## 13 · A nightlife illustration — method only

*Illustrative; fixes nothing about the design; invents no findings.*

A page for one place, opened by three readers: someone from the scene, a licensing officer, a funder.

**The unit, first.** The page names the place in the forum's words and says beneath it which statistical unit the figures are for, that the ward is a different shape, and that the shape can be changed. The scene reader finds the name; the officer finds the ward; neither is told the other is wrong.

**The two columns.** Left, first: what the scene holds — a profile of identity fields as small linear multiples in a fixed order, each with its provenance tier in text; then the holes, full size, each labelled with its symbol and its kind; then the felt measures with their spread and their sample (Chapter 9). Right: what systems count — incidents, licensing actions, complaints — same type, same space, same finish, its own scales, its own caveats (an invented example: a note that incident counts follow where officers were, and officers are not everywhere). No axis is shared. Between them, the bridging sentence, within the poster rule, one thing from each column; and each panel's own frame repeats it.

**The conditions.** Below, each condition on its own spread of the peer set, this place marked, the wording generated by the one-deviation rule, the gate's status and harmonised criteria printed inside each chart's frame. Where a gate failed, the condition stands alone with no comparator and says why. Where the place is ahead on one and behind on another, the sentence says so and does not add them.

**The label.** The place carries no label of the house's making except a type name written by the classifier's rule, hedged, and reviewed at the forum; the officer reads a description of what happened to the area, not a verdict on what it did.

**Discovery.** For the reader who came from the house's own question, what does your city sound like: three places, chosen from their own weights, each with its why as content cues — nights a month in the genre, crews, free-entry share, years running — the weights shown and changeable, no ordering by size anywhere, and an adjacency row marked as such. For the funder: the map of published grants over the map of scenes, with the sentence that a dark patch is also where nothing has been published, and with the category's findability status visible as text.

**The crop.** Every chart on the page carries, inside its frame, title, unit, source, base, address and snapshot date, and for a comparison its gate status and wording; the two columns' panels carry the bridging sentence; the page text repeats all of it for the screen reader. A screenshot of any panel says what it is and what it sat beside.

**The review.** The page's record says it was shown to the forum on a date, what changed, and where to report an error. The funder sees that line too.

**The tests.** The scene reader, on a phone, says what is there and what is missing within the three-phones time. The officer states the page's claim without hostility. The funder says where the gap is and where the publishing gap is. A reader from a place whose counted column is thin opens its own page and reads what is real and what would help. And someone takes a screenshot of the conditions panel and sends it to a journalist, who can see the gate, the unit and the date without asking.

## What proves a break works

Run the five tests of section 10 on the page as built, with the three user groups and a holder of the harm frame. Then the two that this chapter adds. The *crop test, measured*: screenshot each panel and hand it to someone who has not seen the page; they must be able to say what the panel is about, its unit, its source, its date, for a comparison whether the gate passed, and for either column what the other column holds — without the page, and without being told where to look. If they cannot, the context was beside the frame. This is a legibility test; the chapter's own evidence says that a third of readers do not notice in-frame context unprompted, so the pass rate is the measure, not a single reader's success. The *review test*: the recognition record's line 9 is read back to the people who were in the room; they confirm that what they said is what is recorded and that what changed is what they asked for. It is a check on the record, not a validation of the account, which Chapter 9 says member checking cannot supply alone. A break in any Fixed rule above is logged with these two results attached.

## What this chapter could not settle

- No controlled study of whether readers attend to or remember data about their own locality; the chapter rested on 42 interviews and one official account.
- No UK measurement of residents' own neighbourhood boundaries at scale; the chapter used the American survey of 7,498, and showed that UK definitions leave the unit to the respondent.
- No experiment on whether readers notice an absent category or series; the chapter narrowed CALVI to what it tests and carried the rule as a reading.
- No test of "equal visual dignity"; the rule is a stance on LAY-08.
- No study of whether a profile form reads as "better" rather than "different"; the chapter chose the form the accuracy evidence prefers.
- No lay-reader test of dominance displays; the chapter chose the ONS's spreads-plus-sentence form.
- No evaluation of any community-review procedure, and one researcher's account of harm; the chapter assembled a procedure from guides and principles and marked it so.

## Words this chapter uses

- The *two columns* are version 1's name for the deck's slides 7 and 8: what systems already count about a place (incidents, licensing, complaints, footfall, spend) beside what a scene holds and almost nobody counts (belonging, trust, care, talent, joy) — the *counted* column and the *missed* column.
- A place's *identity* is its profile — what it is like, what it sounds like, the shape of its week, who comes — and its *conditions* are what it has or lacks: space, support, funding, affordability, security of tenure.
- A *rank* is an ordering of units published as the maker's ordering; a *match* is an ordering made for one reader from their own weights and shown to them alone.
- A *label* is the name given to a category, cluster or type of place.
- *Recognise* is the chapter's own word, which section 1 splits into four senses and numbers, because the failure the brief names — "they do not recognise their place" — turns out to be several: four senses of the word in section 1, and five failures with tests in section 10.
