# Chapter 13 — Make it look like art

*Dashboard Design · Part 3: The craft*
*Draft for steer · 17 August 2026*

---

## The decision

**How does the instrument read as nightlife's — "like art, not a government boring thing" — without a single Fixed rule bending?**

---

## The 60-second version

> The founding instruction, from the Skiddle call: *"with a really high design standard, we
> make it look like art… not look like a government fucking boring thing."* The founding fear
> is that rigour forbids it. The evidence says the opposite — **the rules don't forbid the
> art; they name where it lives.**
>
> The embellishment research, read as a line: decoration was accused of harming charts; the
> measured verdict is that **titles and text carry the message** and memorable visuals are
> often *also* effective; the latest work finds salient embellishment reads as *more
> enticing* — while over-familiar decoration has normalised into wallpaper. Aesthetic
> judgement forms in 50 milliseconds and rewards **looking like what you claim to be**. And
> the institutional proof exists: an expressive rendering of noise data hangs in MoMA's
> permanent collection.
>
> Which resolves this book's oldest open tension. Prototypicality rewards matching the genre —
> but **the claim decides the genre.** This instrument's claim is not "dashboard"; it is
> *nightlife's evidence*. So the prototype to match is the culture's own visual language — the
> club poster, five words or less; the flyer; the lineup; the dark room and the light that
> cuts it — a tradition with fifty years of communicating fast, at night, in competition, to
> exactly these audiences. The government-boring look isn't the rigorous option. It's just a
> different genre's costume — and the wrong prototype for this claim.

## How the decision is made

### Step one — know the three tiers here better than anywhere

The tier model does its hardest work in this chapter. **Fixed** holds everything Chapters 1–11
built: contrast, structure, honesty, disclosure, the recipe on the face. **Decorative** is
genuinely free — and this book has kept it stocked: grounds, texture, motion-as-feel,
covers, the sound-bounce register, the poster surfaces. The failure mode is the one the
Decision Book measured over and over: **meaning drifting into the decorative layer** — the
finding that exists only in an illustration, the distinction carried only by an accent. The
art is welcome everywhere; load-bearing meaning lives only where the rules can hold it.

### Step two — read the embellishment evidence as a licence with conditions

- The "useful junk" claim — embellishment aiding memorability without harming accuracy — has
  been contested since it was made. **[Contested; the original study stands but generalises
  poorly.]**
- The strongest measured result: across 393 visualisations, **titles and supporting text
  dominate what is attended and recalled**, and "a memorable visualization is often also an
  effective one." **[Measured.]** Decoration doesn't carry the message; words do — so
  decoration is free *because* the words are doing their job (Chapter 1's sentence rule was
  the licence all along).
- The newest line: salient, novel embellishment reads as **more enticing**, while
  ubiquitous decoration has normalised — readers now parse over-familiar ornament as generic.
  **[Measured/evaluated.]** Translation: the government template isn't neutral; it's
  *invisible*. Distinctiveness is not a risk to seriousness — sameness is a risk to being
  seen at all.
- And the 50-millisecond judgement rewards **low complexity and high prototypicality**
  **[measured]** — which is not a verdict against boldness; it is a verdict against clutter
  and against costume that doesn't match the claim.

The standing conditions from the axioms: name what the expressive form buys (register,
attention, retention); **ship the precise version alongside** — the package rule *is* the
licence; there is no beauty exemption from the accessibility audit. **[Axioms — Decision Book
Ch 7; Chartability.]**

### Step three — resolve the prototypicality tension: the claim picks the genre

Chapter 1 logged the tension: prototypicality rewards looking like what you claim to be, and
the brief demands *not* looking like the incumbent genre. The resolution, now formal: **the
instrument's claim is nightlife's evidence, so its prototype is nightlife's visual
tradition** — the poster (five words or less, read at walking pace, at night, in
competition), the flyer, the lineup hierarchy, the stamp and the lanyard, the dark ground and
the cutting light. That tradition is itself a fifty-year-old discipline of fast legibility
under worse conditions than any dashboard faces — which is why the house's poster mantra
out-toughens the ONS title rule (Chapter 1 already recorded the tightening).

Institutional cover, should anyone demand it: Fragapane's *Noise Pollution* — expressive
encoding of sound data — sits in MoMA's permanent collection **[verified in raw sweep]**; the
Rich Picture sits in the systems-thinking canon **[Decision Book Ch 10, axiom]**; the
decorative tier sits in the Decision Book itself. The art-register is not this book's
indulgence. It is precedented practice with its conditions attached.

### Step five — the two-audience test is the whole game

The seven tests already contain this chapter's proof: **committee-room and dancefloor,
simultaneously.** The art register must never cost the committee room (that is what the Fixed
floor guarantees), and the rigour must never cost the dancefloor (that is what this chapter
guarantees). The style guide's line is the operating instruction: *"Strong design must never
make weak evidence look stronger than it is"* — and its unwritten twin, now written: **strong
evidence must never be made to look weaker than it is by timid design.** A boring instrument
about collective joy is a contradiction the culture will smell instantly — the register *is*
part of the claim's credibility with half its audience.

## Worked example

*Illustrative — the Croydon page, dressed.*

The headline is set like a poster: five words — **"CROYDON RUNS ON ITS OWN SOUND"** — in the
house display face, on the night ground. Beneath it, unchanged from Chapter 5, the precise
sentence with its gate and provenance: the poster and the evidence, layered, each doing its
job. The portrait renders in the sound-bounce register — marks that pulse *once, subtly* on
load (motion as feel; instant under reduced-motion). The gaps in the value column are drawn
as ghosted vessels with the reason labels set like set-times on a flyer. The place plays.
The committee-room export of the same page — one click — carries identical content with the
poster layer quieted: not a different truth, a different volume.

**The test, run both ways:** the promoter says *"this looks like ours."* The analyst finds
every number recomputable. Neither audience paid for the other's experience.

## Pressure points

**Where to push hard.** The poster surfaces, the audio identity, the drawn absence, the
physical layer at workshops — all decorative-tier, all distinctively hers, all free. This is
where the studio's "make it look like art" is not a tension with the book but its reward.

**Where it looks like art and is actually damage.** The finding that exists only in the
mural. The judgement smuggled in an accent colour. The gorgeous canvas no key can enter. The
vibe-o-meter with beautiful typography. Each is a Fixed breach wearing the register as
cover — and the tier test strips the cover in one question.

**What proves a break works.** The two-audience test above, plus the Decision Book's removal
discipline: print the page mono, silence the audio, disable the motion, strip the poster
layer — the evidence must stand complete in what remains. If it does, the art was free. If it
doesn't, meaning leaked into the decorative layer, and Chapter 14's log wants the entry.
