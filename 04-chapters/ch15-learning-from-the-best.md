# Chapter 15 — Learning from the best

*Dashboard Design · Part 4: The boundary and the practice*

---

*Reading this chapter.* This is one chapter of *Dashboard Design*, Nightlife Culture's research book on how evidence becomes a display a reader can trust, read and act on. The studio ("the house" in some chapters) is Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio; the brief is its founder Carly Heath's commission for the book, and her eight ways a dashboard fails, the failure register, run through it. The Vibe Map, the studio's prototype for showing a place's nightlife culture, appears only as an illustration, never as a specification. "Version 1" is the book's archived first draft, whose positions this version re-tests against the sources. Every rule carries a kind (where it comes from: Standard, Evidence, Reading, Practice or Nightlife Culture) and a tier (how firm it is: Fixed, Adaptable or Decorative); Chapter 0 explains both, and the three senses of Fixed: a requirement that can be checked, a sequence whose order is the hard line, and a statement of the record. A dagger (†) marks a source read at the authors' abstract or summary only; "secondary" a quotation reached through a named reproduction; "(read once)" a quotation returned on a single reading and not confirmed by a second. Chapter 0 holds the glossary of the words the chapters share; each chapter lists its own coinages before its sources.

---

## The question

Sector by sector — public health dashboards, finance and risk, economic development and place, the culture and charity sectors' impact practice, and the great public data projects — what did the best public data displays get right about trust, provenance, change over time and reach; how is each of those four properties measured or evidenced where it is measured at all; what did each sector get wrong or leave unsolved; which of their mechanisms are documented well enough, with a printed rule and a stated reason and either evidence or a named cost, to be borrowed by a public dashboard about a place's culture; and does the chapter find enough that Chapters 2 and 5 did not to earn its place as a chapter rather than a reading list?

## The 60-second version

**Trust is measured for producers, and for how government presents their figures, and almost never for displays.** The Public Confidence in Official Statistics survey asks the same questions in waves (2,364 adults in 2023; 3,398 in 2021): "87% of respondents who gave an opinion indicating that they tend to trust it (71%) or trust it a great deal (16%)" — the ONS — while agreement that "the government presents official statistics honestly" "remained relatively low (32%, compared to 35% in 2021)"; the two items have sat between 54 and 60 points apart across four waves. Charities are trusted at 6.5 out of 10 (4,681 respondents, 2026), and the drivers people name are where the money goes (55 per cent) and "a real difference" made (51 per cent). The most-used public dashboard in the UK, by its own team's account, evidences its trust with one blog sentence — "an outstandingly high level of user trust in the dashboard data at 90.3%, up from an already high level in November when 73.4% of users responded that they trusted the data" — from a self-selected on-site survey with no n or wording; the one randomised test found of whether a dashboard changes trust — trust in government, read through the authors' own summary (secondary) — reported that "information disclosure has no direct effects on willingness to trust". A central bank's layered report raised comprehension in two experiments (285 and 2,275 readers) and moved trust only slightly, for one "relatable" wording, in one; "the simplification of language increases public comprehension more than the inclusion of visuals" (†). Showing uncertainty as a range beside a point lowered trust little or not at all — "only a small decrease … mostly for verbal uncertainty communication" across five experiments (n = 5,780), none in the field, and none in the office across two waves of over 3,000 and over 4,000 — while a verbal hedge alone "tends to have little effect" on understanding (read once).

**Provenance is where the best projects are most alike, and the pattern is a record per metric plus a dated log.** UKHSA gives every metric a page with the same five headings — Summary, Rationale, Definition, Methodology, Caveats (read once) — and a change log, separate from the weekly refresh, whose entries are labelled and paired: "Issue: COVID-19 deaths data", "Resolved: duplicated rows in bulk download files". Fingertips gives every indicator a thirty-two-field record with the numerator and the denominator defined and sourced separately. Johns Hopkins kept two ledgers: its own errors in a machine-readable errata file and its sources' revisions in a dated prose list with the reason. Our World in Data's testing dataset carried, for every observation, "a URL at which the specific observation of the corresponding raw data can be found". The ONS separates a revision ("a scheduled change") from a correction ("an unplanned revision … because of the identification of an error", read once) and labels the second as such. The statistics regulator's 2025 guidance makes a minimum list for any official dashboard — sources, status, strengths and limitations, revisions, update schedule, archive, contact — and a clause the sectors' own documents had not: "come with a plan for maintaining, updating and retiring the dashboard".

**Change over time is documented well where the rule for the arrow is printed, and badly where it is not.** Fingertips will not draw a trend arrow unless "There must be at least five points in the series", the periods are independent, the trend passes "χ² > 9.5495" (p < 0.2 per cent) and survives dropping any one point — and still says the arrows "do not constitute a rigorous analysis of the time series". Its colour rule: "indicator values should only be given red or green RAG ratings if they are statistically significantly higher or lower than the comparator", with the warning that "one in twenty red or green ratings could be expected even where all the underlying distributions are essentially amber". The OECD's regional tool prints its arrow rule in one sentence (±5 per cent against a fixed start year); Wales prints a significance test where intervals exist and a declared expert judgement where they do not; Scotland's per-indicator rules lived on pages that now redirect. Finance keeps vintages ("Economic data time travel since 2006"), and the reason is on record — re-running a classic test on later data, "the coefficients change dramatically", though for many studies results were "generally robust" (read once); the ONS prints the size of its own revisions ("The mean absolute revision between the first and final estimates is 0.27 percentage points"); the Bank of England's fan chart has a printed band rule that changed from 10 per cent steps to 30 per cent steps without the picture changing, survived a review that said "They should be eliminated", and was then found, in the Bank's own staff test of 300 members of the public and 75 experts against five other formats, to be "well understood". A cumulative axis "can show only an upward or flat trend", and in two experiments with 2,549 participants it "consistently led to participants believing that they and others were at more risk" (both read once). The deprivation indices say in print what a rank cannot: "a small area with a rank of 1,000 is not half as deprived as a place with a rank of 500", and "Indices releases should not be viewed or construed as a time-series".

**Reach is counted in server events and self-reports, and almost never as decisions.** "over 300,000 unique users every day, and five million hits a week"; "3·6 billion page views"; "nearly 100 million page views" in a year; Nomis, forty-five years old, publishes no usage figure on any page reached; a case study of the ONS's local statistics service reports "93% of users found it easy to use" with no n; Our World in Data's own audience survey found 17.5 per cent of respondents said they download data when analytics showed "1.6% of sessions have a CSV download" (read once). The one service assessment of a dashboard team told it to "create a measurement framework that demonstrates how metrics map to key performance indicators (KPIs) and goals". The question that measures use is the one a Scottish government evaluation asked of 41 users of an internal dashboard: whether the data informed "their decisions or conclusions as part of their role" (49 per cent, read once).

**Public data projects end or change hands — four of the five read here have since 2021 — and the culture sector's own yardstick had no published plan.** Johns Hopkins gave four weeks' notice, a reason located upstream ("U.S. states and counties began to consistently reduce the amount of publicly reported data", read once), and a frozen open repository; the COVID Tracking Project announced its end date five weeks ahead and promised "gap analyses noting where replacement data still doesn't exist"; Our World in Data swapped a dying source for a living one with "The URL of all our charts and the variable names in our dataset will remain the same" and later listed, dataset by dataset, what continues and what stops; UKHSA ran old and new in parallel and published a 1.4 GB archive with a page of its known gaps. The Audience Agency entered liquidation on 10 September 2026; its notice names no successor for Audience Spectrum or Audience Finder, and the former funder's statement rests on "our understanding is that organisations are currently still able to access and download their historic data". The quality-metrics line the culture sector built — whose 2016 evaluation found 63 per cent of organisations agreeing the framework was useful and the resulting data "regularly referenced as 'interesting' rather than 'useful'" — became a funding condition; separately, the funder's mandatory audience-data platform had its survey requirement suspended from 2023 to 2025; and a review of the funder, read through the trade press (secondary), says "The present reporting requirements are not fit for purpose."

**What the chapter adds to Chapters 2 and 5.** A set of mechanisms with their printed rules, reasons, evidence and costs — the five-heading metric page and the thirty-two-field record; the labelled change log and the two ledgers; the revision-versus-correction distinction and the printed revision size; the vintage archive; the no-arrow-without-a-rule standard and the one-in-twenty warning; the incomplete-tail band with the direction of its bias; the not-a-time-series sentence and the not-half-as-deprived sentence; the refusal to aggregate; the fixed trust question in waves, of those with a view; the question of whether the data informed a decision; the retirement clause and the sunset note. Seven of these the chapter holds true regardless (section 11); the rest are an offer with terms. Beneath the dashboard the chapter leaves a twelfth record, the *precedent record*: one entry of nine lines per mechanism considered — the mechanism and the record it feeds; the source and its date; the rule as printed; the reason given; the evidence or its absence; the condition or cost; what the house changes; the status; the review date. The pass test is that every borrowed rule the build uses can be traced to an entry, and that no entry rests on "this is what everyone does".

## 1 · What "learning from the best" means here

This chapter exists because "take inspiration from the best in the world" was said at the start. Chapter 2 settled what turns a reader's decision and what is measured about trust in a display; Chapter 5 settled how a measure is built and audited. So this chapter has a test to pass before it has anything to teach: it must find, sector by sector, things those chapters did not, and it must deliver them as *mechanisms* with their terms rather than as examples to admire. It found about forty, listed in section 9; about six were already in the book in another form.

**What "the best" means here.** The chapter did not pick the best by taste. It read the displays the brief named, the ones the sectors' own regulators, reviews and evaluations cite, and the ones whose documentation other displays copy — and it reports each for what its documentation makes transferable, not as a verdict on the project.

**The four properties, made measurable.** The brief's four words are not the same kind of thing, and the chapter keeps them apart. *Trust* is reported here only where someone asked people a question about it, with the question's wording, the sample and the base; the chapter distinguishes trust in a producer (the ONS; "charities"; a central bank) from trust in a display, because the first is measured in long series and the second almost never is. *Provenance* is read as documentation: what a metric's page carries, what a change log records, whether a dataset's past versions can be retrieved. *Change over time* has two senses that the sectors handle differently — how a display shows that a quantity moved (arrows, colours, trends, fans) and how a display's own figures move after they are first published (revisions, vintages, corrections) — and the chapter reads both. *Reach* is reported in whatever unit the source counted in, with the unit named, because the units do not convert: a "feature layer request" is a map tile, a "hit" is a server event, a "unique user" is a cookie, a survey percentage is a self-report. Where a source counts decisions informed, the chapter says so, because that is the only unit the failure register cares about.

**The borrowing test.** A mechanism is borrowable when three things are true of it. Its rule is printed — thresholds, intervals, colours, cadence, wording — so that someone else can apply it and a reader can check that it was applied. Its reason is stated, so that the house can tell whether the reason holds for a place's culture as it held for a pandemic or a bank. And either its evidence is known (someone measured what it did, with a sample and a design) or its cost is named (what it needs — a forecast-error history, five annual points, confidence intervals, a stable indicator set — and what it does when those are absent). A mechanism with no printed rule is a *look*; one with a rule but no stated reason is a *habit*; one with a rule and a reason but neither evidence nor a named cost is a *bet*. The chapter uses the three words in those senses only. The precedent record in section 11 is the form in which the test is run and kept.

**What the chapter is not.** It is not research into any sector's subject matter: the public health displays are read for their banding rules and not for what they say about health; the deprivation indices for what they say a rank can mean and not for which areas rank where; the charity codes for what they require and not for any charity. It is not research into nightlife, in any form. It is not a verdict on any of these projects as projects; several of them did their work under conditions no studio will face, and the chapter reads them for what their documentation makes transferable. And beyond the seven truths and their procedures, it does not decide what the Vibe Map borrows; section 9 lists what each sector offers with its terms, section 12 says which decisions are the studio's, and the precedent record is where each decision would be written.

What the failure register asks of this chapter is mostly the two failures that sit at either end of a display's life: "It fails if it looks the same every month" and "It fails if it breaks or isn't reliable". The sectors that have run public displays for years — a labour-market statistics service since 1981, a mortality database with a versioned method, a dashboard whose team reported "over 300,000 unique users every day" — have written down how they handle the first (revisions, vintages, arrows with rules) and the second (release calendars, change logs, retirement plans), and that writing is what this chapter went to find. Section 10 reads the whole register.

## 2 · What people believe about the best, and what the evidence says

**Belief: the most-used dashboards have the best evidence that they are trusted.** The UK's coronavirus dashboard evidences its trust in one blog sentence, from an on-site survey with no sample size or question wording; the regulator's praise ("one of strongest examples of open data and statistics being used by a huge number of individuals") is praise; the one randomised test found of a dashboard's effect on trust reported no average effect and a split by prior trust. Where trust is measured in series it is measured for producers — the ONS, charities — by a fixed question repeated in waves. Section 3 and section 7. *(Evidence; Practice.)*

**Belief: showing uncertainty makes people trust the number less.** Five experiments with 5,780 participants found "only a small decrease in trust in numbers and trustworthiness of the source, and mostly for verbal uncertainty communication"; in a field test in a BBC article, a range beside the point estimate "did not affect participants' trust in the number"; two waves of a UK GDP experiment found that communicating uncertainty "improves the public's understanding of data uncertainty, but does not reduce its trust in the statistical office", and that "The textual uncertainty qualifier given to Group 2 tends to have little effect" (read once). A verbal qualifier did little for understanding; the range did the work, and the measured cost in trust was small and mostly for the verbal form. Section 4. *(Evidence.)*

**Belief: pictures make the public understand.** In the Bank of England's four-arm experiment with 2,275 respondents, the published visual summary improved comprehension by "25% (0.5 points)", half the words did as well as the pictures, and plain "relatable" wording did best at "42% (0.8 points)" — which still left readers at "2.85 out of 5 questions correctly" (read once). The journal version's abstract: "the simplification of language increases public comprehension more than the inclusion of visuals" (†). Section 4. *(Evidence.)*

**Belief: a rank is harmless if the method is published.** The place instruments' own method texts, and two analysts of ranking, say otherwise: "they show that one area is more deprived than another but not by how much"; "The web tool does not include a regional composite well-being index"; "rankings can be too unreliable to be helpful"; "Typical risk matrices can correctly and unambiguously compare only a small fraction (e.g., less than 10%) of randomly selected pairs of hazards" (†). An audited index that publishes rank intervals says that "These confidence intervals also have to be taken into account when comparing economy rank changes from one year to the next". Section 5. *(Standard (official statistics); Evidence of method.)*

**Belief: an index built to track change over time tracks change over time.** The deprivation indices, updated "every 3 to 5 years", "cannot be used to identify real change in deprivation over time", and their users asked for exactly that; the Health Index, built so that "comparisons can be made between scores over time", last released in June 2023 and showing "Next release: To be announced" on 6 October 2026, with no status notice found; the levelling-up metrics were announced before some of them existed and are still reported annually by a government that "has moved away from the levelling up programme" because a statute requires it. Section 5. *(Standard (official statistics); Practice.)*

**Belief: the yardstick's owner will always be there.** The Audience Agency, custodian of the culture sector's audience segmentation and its audience-data platform, entered liquidation on 10 September 2026 with no successor named in anything that could be read. Section 6. *(Practice.)*

**Belief: usage is reach.** The units do not convert, and no source read counts decisions except by asking. Section 1's rule, and section 8. *(Reading.)*

**Belief: a public data project is forever.** Four of the projects read have ended or changed source since 2021, and the statistics regulator now asks every official dashboard to "come with a plan for maintaining, updating and retiring the dashboard". Section 7. *(Standard (regulator); Practice.)*

## 3 · Public health

Public health built, under pressure and in public, the most complete provenance furniture of any sector read, and left the most exact printed rules for colour and for arrows. It also left the thinnest evidence of its own reach and trust, and three documented retirements.

### A dashboard that says what it is not

The UKHSA data dashboard — successor to the coronavirus dashboard, which was retired on 21 December 2023 — carries a page headed "Compliance" that states, pillar by pillar against the Code of Practice for Statistics, what the dashboard meets and what it cannot. "it is not possible for the dashboard to be classed as official statistics", because "different classifications of data are displayed" (read once); among the things it cannot do is "Pre-announce the release time of data (usually 4pm every Thursday) via the release calendar" (read once) — the official release calendar is for official statistics, which this is not — so it makes the commitment on its own page instead: "so we commit to update the data at 4pm every Thursday"; "On the rare occasions when delays are unavoidable, we announce this on our homepage"; "We announce any unexpected changes to the data we release on the homepage and add an explanation to our What's new or About pages"; "We have carried out over 100 individual user research sessions"; "allowing users to download data from every graph displayed on the dashboard". The Code is open to voluntary application by producers who are not official statisticians, and this is what a voluntary statement looks like: a short public page saying which requirements a mixed-status display meets, a fixed weekly release time, and a named place where surprises are announced. *(Practice; Standard (official statistics).)*

### A page per metric, and a log apart from the refresh

Each metric on the dashboard has a documentation page — the two read carry the same five headings, Summary, Rationale, Definition, Methodology, Caveats (confirmed at source for the cases page) — and a machine name that matches the API. The cases page, for example: "New cases are reported by specimen date - the date the first sample that identified the infection was taken"; "Data for the last 5 days by specimen date are considered incomplete" "as it takes time to get test results and record them on relevant lab systems"; "This figure will underestimate the actual number of COVID-19 infections". The log entry that announced the documentation (21 December 2023) names the template's purpose in four clauses: "definition (what the metric is), rationale (why we're showing the metric), methodology (how the metric is collected and calculated), caveats (additional information that will help users to understand what the metric is showing)" (read once). What the template lacks is as instructive: no separate "source" or "update frequency" field was visible on the pages read; cadence lives on the compliance page and in the log. *(Practice.)*

The log — "What's new" — is the chapter's primary change-log specimen. "Here we'll show a timeline of changes that have happened outside of the weekly data refresh"; its entries are labelled (New metric, New Feature, New content, Update, Change to metric, Data issue, Other — read once as a set) and the data issues are opened and closed in public: "Issue: COVID-19 deaths data" — "Due to a data issue, COVID-19 deaths data are only available to 29th December 2023"; "Resolved: duplicated rows in bulk download files", ending "We appreciate the user feedback we received flagging this issue"; "On 11 November 2024, a data processing error led to a pipeline failure and prevented the COVID-19 positivity dataset from being produced." (read once). Cadence changes are logged as changes to the metric: "Respiratory viruses data to be updated every 2 weeks"; "The severity of the measles outbreak has been downgraded. As a result, we will report measles data monthly rather than every 2 weeks." (read once). A geography change is logged with its consequence: "This will also affect the previous time series for this data." (read once). *(Practice.)*

### The two dates, and the tail that always looks like a fall

A series of events that arrive late has two dates — the date the thing happened and the date it was reported — and public health chose the first. PHE explained in April 2020 that its surveillance reports counted "by the date the specimen was taken from the person being tested, rather than the date the lab carried out testing and sent data to PHE", and that "While this gives the most accurate analysis of how the cases progress over time it does mean that the latest days' figures are usually incomplete – so it shouldn't be seen as a sudden large drop in cases." The archive page says what the other date is for: "Data presented by 'publish date' is not a reliable indicator of trends over time since this represents the date on which data was first published and may include the publication of a backlog of data or subsequent revisions to the data." (fragment verified; remainder read once). On the successor dashboard the incomplete tail is drawn as a band and the change was logged — for the measles chart, whose window is four weeks where the cases metric's is five days: "A blue shaded section now shows the reporting delay, affecting the last 4 weeks of data collected"; "This replaces the dashed lines that we previously used to represent these data"; "The figures shown in this period are likely to underestimate the number of cases". And when a source stopped, the date basis changed and the change was logged: the source "stopped publishing data on deaths with COVID-19 on the death certificate by date of death occurrence", so "we have changed the dashboard to show deaths with COVID-19 on the death certificate by date of registration". *(Practice.)*

The mechanism, in four parts, is borrowable whole for any counted series with late returns: attribute each event to the date it happened; state a fixed incompleteness window in the metric's own documentation; mark the window on the chart with a sentence giving the direction of the bias; keep the by-publication-date series and say it is not a trend series. *(Reading.)*

### Reach and trust, as the team reported them

The team's own posts are the only source. September 2020: "over 300,000 unique users every day, and five million hits a week"; an on-site survey that drew "within 48 hours we had 17,000 responses"; "around 10,000 people online refreshing the page" at the four o'clock update; "Users tell us they are even using the dashboard to make decisions about whether they should go on holiday to a certain area, visit a friend or get their hair cut." (fragment verified; sentence read once). March 2021: "nearly 200 metrics at 4pm each day"; "about 40 million individual figures"; "a team of 18"; "76.5 million hits in just 24 hours"; and the trust sentence — "Results from our most recent survey showed an outstandingly high level of user trust in the dashboard data at 90.3%, up from an already high level in November when 73.4% of users responded that they trusted the data." — with no sample size, wording or sampling given; users were said to dislike "delays, changing definitions and big corrections". The regulator called it "one of strongest examples of open data and statistics being used by a huge number of individuals" and its API "an exemplar for the provision of open data across government"; it won the Royal Statistical Society and regulator's award for voluntary adoption of the Code (secondary; trade press). *(Practice; Evidence, self-reported.)*

Two things follow. The usage figures are the team's own and the trust figure is unusable as evidence, which is the pattern the whole chapter finds: the practice of publishing the numbers is borrowable, the numbers are not. And the one external assessment of the team — a service assessment against the government's standard, published February 2025 for a winter-viruses successor — told it to explore "whether members of public need to access population health data outside of the COVID-pandemic", to research "potential service users who are not already using the coronavirus data dashboard" (read once), and to "create a measurement framework that demonstrates how metrics map to key performance indicators (KPIs) and goals", because "the team has identified sensible metrics to track, although it's unclear which goals these relate to" (read once). Even this team, at the alpha stage of its successor, had not yet tied its usage metrics to goals. *(Practice; Reading.)*

### Retirement, done in public

The coronavirus dashboard was "for use in the height of a global pandemic"; "the new dashboard is designed and built for longevity"; "some of the metrics are less useful than they were during the pandemic and could be misleading"; the successor's research drew "over 5,000 respondents", its sessions included "those who aren't users of the dashboard", and it ran alongside the old one from September 2023 (read once). The full archive followed about twelve weeks after the switch-off, "1.4 GB" with subsets, and with its gaps stated: "Some data is missing for the period October 2020 from files presented by Lower Tier Local Authority (LTLA)." (read once); "October 2020 data has been imputed using available data currently held in internal UKHSA systems" (read once). *(Practice.)*

### Fingertips: the rules for colour and for arrows, printed

The public health profiles tool (Fingertips, now the Office for Health Improvement and Disparities') publishes the fullest rules in any sector read for colouring a value and for drawing an arrow, and the chapter quotes them because they are the standard against which every other sector's arrows and colours should be read. For a designer the two rules come to this: a colour is a verdict, so it is given only when the data can support one; an arrow is a claim about a trend, so it is drawn only when the series can support one; and both come with a printed sentence saying how often they will be wrong.

*Colour means significance.* The RAG guide (version 20 June 2017): "where green denotes a 'favourable' value, red an 'unfavourable' value and amber a 'neutral' value"; and the rule — "indicator values should only be given red or green RAG ratings if they are statistically significantly higher or lower than the comparator". Three kinds of comparator: a fixed reference value, a reference population value, a reference sample (read once). For a fixed comparator: "RAG ratings are defined as red or green if a fixed reference value falls above the UCL or below the LCL respectively, and amber otherwise." (read once). On overlap: "If two confidence intervals do not overlap then it is accurate to state that the indicators differ at the appropriate significance level." (read once), but "where confidence intervals do overlap it is does not follow that there is no evidence that the indicator values differ significantly" (as printed). On independence: overlapping three- or five-year periods "can never be compared using methods that assume independence" (read once). On multiple comparisons, the sentence the chapter would print on any coloured table: "With 95% confidence there is a 5% risk that each such indicator is misclassified"; "one in twenty red or green ratings could be expected even where all the underlying distributions are essentially amber". Where an indicator has no good direction, "the terms 'higher' and 'lower' should be used with neutral colouring such as shades of blue, from light to dark". Any extra threshold "is essentially subjective, unless there is scientific justification". The guide ends with a six-question checklist — polarity; comparator type; indicator type; assumptions stated; definitions documented; method appropriate (read once) — and, as read, has no sentence about ranks or league tables; quintiles appear in the tool's own method list below, as a separate way of colouring that the guide does not govern. *(Standard (official statistics).)*

*The interval method is named.* "The preferred OHID method is the Wilson Score method" "(Wilson, 1927) which has been evaluated and recommended by Newcombe and Altman", because "it does not fail to give an interval when the numerator count and, therefore, the proportion is zero" and the normal approximation "does not perform well when the numerator or denominator is small"; a benchmark is treated as exact and the test is whether it lies outside the area's interval ("If the target or benchmark value is outside the confidence interval, it can be inferred that the difference" is significant); and the interval "does not include imprecision resulting from systematic error (bias)". The comparison method is a per-indicator setting exposed in the tool's public API — "Confidence intervals overlapping reference value (95.0)", "Overlapping confidence intervals (95.0)" (read once), "Confidence intervals overlapping reference value (99.8)", "Quintiles", and "No comparison", described "Undefined. Indicator will not be coloured." — so a reader with the API can find out which rule coloured a cell. *(Standard (official statistics); Practice.)*

*An arrow has a rule.* The trend-marker guidance: "There must be at least five points in the series"; "Time periods must be independent of one another: not overlapping, or rolling time periods"; annual data only (read once); no breaks; the test is run on the most recent five values ("In the Fingertips application, N is 5"); "If χ² > 9.5495 the trend is significant (p < 0.2%)"; and then the robustness step — the slope "calculation is repeated five times, each time excluding one of the five points", and if removing any one point produces a trend in the opposite direction, an amber marker is shown instead of an arrow (read once). Colours: "an upward arrow will be green and a downward arrow red" where high is good; "a blue marker is used to identify significant trends in either direction" where there is no good direction. And the caveat on the whole device: the markers "do not constitute a rigorous analysis of the time series". *(Standard (official statistics).)*

*The record per indicator.* The tool's metadata export has thirty-two fields; the header row, read once as a whole and verified field by field for twelve of them: "Indicator ID, Indicator, Indicator number, Rationale, Specific rationale, Definition, Data source, Indicator source, Definition of numerator, Source of numerator, Definition of denominator, Source of denominator, Methodology, Standard population/values, Frequency, Confidence interval details, Disclosure control, Rounding, Caveats, Notes, Impact of COVID-19, Copyright, Data re-use, Links, Indicator Content, Simple Name, Simple Definition, Unit, Value type, Year type, Polarity, Date updated". The numerator and the denominator are defined and sourced separately; a plain-language "Simple Name" and "Simple Definition" sit beside the technical ones; the caveats field carries revision notes. The RAG guide's requirement: "Metadata should define clearly the polarity of the indicator, as well as detailing the data sources and methodology." (read once). *(Practice.)*

*Two more devices, and a retirement in progress.* Two of the tool's other displays carry their own warnings, which Chapter 5's funnel discipline rests on. The spine chart (a bar showing where an area sits in the range of all areas): "The spine is the comparator and is usually the national or regional average to which local area values are compared."; "Spine charts only display relative differences." (read once). The funnel plot's own warning (a chart in which small areas are expected to scatter more widely than large ones): "even within expected random variation, 2 out of every 1,000 observations are likely to lie outside the 3 standard deviation funnel and 5 out of every 100 observations are likely to lie outside the 2 standard deviation funnel" (read once) — Chapter 5's funnel discipline, in its home sector's words. Fingertips keeps a dated geography log ("EPCN version from June 2025 applied", read once) and is itself being replaced by a GOV.UK service named "Find public health data", with a monthly public progress page and the assurance that "until the new service launches, health statistics will still be published on Fingertips" (read once). *(Practice.)*

### Johns Hopkins: two ledgers, and an ending with a date

The dashboard that "launched the first global real-time coronavirus surveillance system" could be read only in its team's own words and its repository. The founding account: "to provide researchers, public health authorities and the general public with a user-friendly tool"; "From January 22-31 the entire data collection and processing was managed manually"; "on February 1, we adopted a semi-automated living data stream strategy"; data kept "initially as google sheets, now in a GitHub repository". Its reach, as its makers reported it in the 2022 paper: "As of June 1, 2022, the dashboard has served the global audience for more than 30 consecutive months, totalling over 226 billion feature layer requests and 3·6 billion page views"; the peak day, "more than 4·6 billion requests and over 69 million views". The retirement notice (10 February 2023) counted differently — "more than 2.5 billion" views and "200 billion" data requests — and the project page differently again ("between 3 – 4.5 billion usage requests per day" at certain periods, read once). Reach here is counted in server events, in at least three incompatible units. *(Practice; Evidence.)*

The repository's README is the chapter's second provenance specimen, and the clearest separation found of a producer's own error from a source's revision. "All cases, deaths, and recoveries reported are based on the date of initial report." (read once); "If the error results from an issue on our collection of the data, the error will be listed in the errata.csv in the csse_covid19_time_series folder." (read once); "If the error results due to a change from the source, the change and reasoning will be listed below." (read once) — a dated prose list of entries such as "April 24 2020: New York City, NY | Back distribution of probable deaths" (read once). Two ledgers: a machine-readable file for *our* mistakes, a dated list with reasons for *their* revisions. *(Practice.)*

The ending: four weeks' notice; a reason located upstream — "Starting in 2021, however, U.S. states and counties began to consistently reduce the amount of publicly reported data" (read once); "The final update of the CRC's data will take place on March 10, 2023." (read once); "After that date, all of the resource center's raw data will remain publicly accessible through the site's Github repositories." (read once); the repository banner, "On March 10, 2023, the Johns Hopkins Coronavirus Resource Center ceased its collecting and reporting of global COVID-19 data", under a CC BY 4.0 licence, pointing users to the WHO and the CDC. *(Practice.)*

### Our World in Data's pandemic datasets: provenance per observation, and a sunset note

The testing dataset's data descriptor (*Scientific Data*, 2020) documents a practice no other source read matches — provenance recorded per observation rather than per dataset: every observation carries a "Source URL", "a URL at which the specific observation of the corresponding raw data can be found", and a "Notes" field for "any notes to aid the interpretation of this specific observation"; every series carries a unit label — "people tested", "tests performed", "samples tested" (read once), or "units unclear". Upstream corrections pass through unaltered and are flagged: abrupt daily changes are sometimes official corrections, "in which case our database includes them without alteration"; "revisions to the data are included on the day the revision is made, not when the revised tests occurred". Its reach: the data "is viewed by tens of thousands of people every day". The vaccinations resource (*Nature Human Behaviour*, 2021) is "collected through a combination of manual and automated means", "regularly audited for accuracy against the original official sources", with the limit "We do not audit official reports beyond technical validation" (read once), and was the WHO's source ("The World Health Organization (WHO) relies on this dataset for its official COVID-19 dashboard"); that paper, as read, does not describe a per-observation source URL, so the per-observation practice is documented for the testing data only. *(Evidence of method; Practice.)*

When the Johns Hopkins source died, the project swapped the series rather than splicing it: "The URL of all our charts and the variable names in our dataset will remain the same"; "An archive of the last version (8 March 2023) of our dataset based on data from Johns Hopkins University is available for download here" (read once). In November 2024 it wrote the sunset note: "countries have reduced their reporting frequency, some metrics have become more complex to interpret, and several datasets are no longer being updated"; "Most of these datasets are no longer being updated by the data provider, so we will no longer be updating them" — listing, dataset by dataset, what continues at what cadence and what stops. *(Practice.)*

### The reporters' disclaimer

The WHO's dashboard carries the aggregator's standing disclaimer in its plainest form: counts "reported by countries, territories and areas"; "All counts are subject to variations in case detection, definitions, laboratory testing, vaccination strategy, and reporting strategies"; "Caution must be taken when interpreting all data presented"; "While steps are taken to ensure accuracy and reliability, all data are subject to continuous verification and change". It is generic where Our World in Data's source URL is specific; both are provenance, at opposite ends of the cost. *(Practice.)*

### What was measured about the dashboards

*The pages.* Ivanković and colleagues (*JMIR*, 2021) assessed "158 dashboards from 53 countries" in July 2020 against a published tool, with an expert panel scoring actionability on a five-point scale (read once). "Only 20 of the 158 dashboards (12.7%) stated both their primary purpose and intended audience"; "Approximately a quarter of the dashboards (39/158, 24.7%) did not report their data sources"; "Less than half of the dashboards (63/158, 39.9%) specified metadata"; "only one-fifth of the dashboards (31/158, 19.6%) included text explaining the quality and meaning of the data"; most "reported indicators over time (138/158, 87.4%)" (read once); 20 of 158 were appraised as highly actionable (read once). Their seven features of an actionable dashboard (abstract): "(1) know their audience and information needs; (2) manage the type, volume, and flow of displayed information; (3) report data sources and methods clearly; (4) link time trends to policy decisions; (5) provide data that are "close to home"; (6) break down the population into relevant subgroups; and (7) use storytelling and visual cues." On trust: "A clear source of data and explanation of an indicator's construction, including potential limitations, was found to be an important component of trust" in the dashboard — the panel's judgement rather than a reader's. Their own limit: "the appraised actionability of the dashboards ultimately does not confirm their use in practice, and evaluating this was beyond the scope of this study". (The abstract's "color-coding was common (93/158, 89.4%)" is a slip; the body's consistent figure is 93 of the 104 dashboards that used visual cues.) *(Evidence of method.)*

*The teams.* Barbazza and colleagues interviewed "Eighty informants, representing 33 national COVID-19 dashboard teams across the WHO European Region" between April and June 2021 (read as the preprint). "COVID-19 dashboards were developed in a learning-by-doing approach"; "More than half of the dashboards reported open data (21/33, 64%)" (read once); an informant: "The prime minister [country] sees the data at the same time as the guy down the road"; teams described errors as inevitable and stressed "the importance of disclosing errors as they happen" (read once), under a lessons theme the authors name as trust (read once); and use was not studied — "monitoring user analytics was not pursued" (read once); "there wasn't time for deep analysis of user behaviour". Producers' testimony, under the theme the authors called trust: disclose errors as they happen. *(Evidence of method.)*

*The readers, once.* Ismail and colleagues (*BMC Public Health*, 2022) evaluated one regional dashboard with a year of analytics and an on-site survey at the anniversary (n = 794): "a total of 2,248,456 views during its first year"; "Overall, 76.4% of respondents reported that the dashboard influenced their personal planning and behaviour." — with the authors' own limits, that online recruitment "could not avoid the potential bias of subjective selection or nonresponse bias" (read once) and that surveying at one year samples "only … those that are still making use of the dashboard one year later" (read once). It is the most complete published "who used it and did it matter" design found, and a statement of what that design cannot show. *(Evidence.)*

*Two experiments.* Padilla and colleagues (*Scientific Reports*, 2022) showed "34 visualization techniques" of the same mortality data to N = 2,549 across two online experiments and measured change in perceived risk: "visualizing data using a cumulative scale consistently led to participants believing that they and others were at more risk than before viewing the visualizations" (read once), because "cumulative y-axis can show only an upward or flat trend" (read once); "Significant increases and decreases in perceived risk can be achieved simply by changing the y-axis without any modifications to the underlying data or forecast predictions." (read once). And Crepaz and Arikan's survey experiment (May 2020; read through the authors' blog; n not stated there) randomised respondents to a high-information dashboard, a low-information one, or nothing: "information disclosure has no direct effects on willingness to trust"; neither condition "produced a statistically significant effect on the respondents' levels of political trust compared to respondents in the control group" (read once); among those with low prior trust, disclosure produced "a 6% drop in political trust" (read once). The outcome measured is trust in government rather than in the display, and the average effect is nil. *(Evidence.)*

### The regulator's minimum list

The Office for Statistics Regulation's *Regulatory guidance: Dashboards* (21 August 2025) is the nearest thing to a UK standard for a public dashboard, and it was written after all of the above. "Producers should weigh up the advantages, disadvantages and risks of using a dashboard before developing one." (read once). "At a minimum, a dashboard should" (read once): adhere to accessibility legislation; "contain clear, visible links to the data source and the producer of the data"; label the statistics' status; carry "clear, concise and prominent information about the strengths and limitations of the statistics"; "include information about revisions and corrections"; be coherent with the rest of the release; "contain accurate details of the update schedule, the date of last update and links to other sources where the data may be available in different formats, including archived data"; "come with a plan for maintaining, updating and retiring the dashboard, so that it continues to meet user needs"; and carry the producer team's contact details (the list read once; the retirement clause verified). Further: "Critical caveats and quality warnings should be prominent and upfront"; "Where any errors are identified, a prominent note should be added to the dashboard, ideally on the initial interface" (read once); attention to revisions "particularly where estimates presented change status over time"; "Visuals and graphics should be used, where possible, to help communicate any uncertainty"; "Visualisations that work for the whole population may fail when the user filters down to a small subgroup"; and on ending — "There should be a plan in place to archive the dashboard when it is decided that there is no longer a user need for it"; "Before a dashboard is archived, a prominent notice should be added that informs users when the dashboard will be archived" "and from which point the data on it will no longer be updated, for full transparency"; "the underlying data should be made available by other means to comply with the Code". On the page read for value, the guidance does not ask for usage analytics (read once); its landing page adds that "Producers should also have processes in place from the start for archiving content as these will be difficult to implement otherwise." *(Standard (regulator).)*

What public health got right: a record per metric; a log apart from the refresh with issues opened and closed; the two dates handled and the tail marked with its bias; colour that means significance and an arrow that has a rule and a warning; two ledgers for error and revision; the ending announced, archived and reasoned. What it got wrong or left open: trust evidenced by one self-selected percentage; reach counted in incompatible server units; usage metrics not yet tied to goals; a cumulative axis that moves perceived risk; and, across 158 dashboards in the first summer, purpose and audience both stated by one in eight and quality text by one in five. *(Reading.)*

## 4 · Finance and risk

Finance is the sector that has thought longest about how a published figure changes after it is published and how an uncertain future is drawn, and it has run experiments on its own readers. Its displays are not the kind the house will build; its mechanisms for change over time are the most developed in any sector read.

### Talking to the public, measured

Haldane and McMahon (*AEA Papers and Proceedings*, 2018) tested the Bank of England's layered communication on "a survey of 285 members of the UK general public, recruited by a third-party company" and on first-year graduate economists (68, read once), randomly assigned to the traditional summary or the new layered content, with three outcomes: comprehension, movement of expectations toward the forecast, and perception of the institution (read once). "First, the results confirm that the new layered content is easier to read and understand, even for technically-advanced MPhil students." "Second, those that read the new layered content tended to develop an improved perception of the institution. While this is not statistically significant in the public survey, it is highly significant in the MPhil sample." (two passes). The layered content had "a Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level of 7.8 (eighth grade level), which compares with the Monetary Policy Summary which had a Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level of 13.4" (two passes). On reach: website activity in the day after the first layered report "almost doubling compared with earlier IRs" (two passes), and, of attention on one social platform, "One clear finding is that monetary policy news itself, rather than the means by which it is communicated, is the largest single factor determining the reach of Twitter activity." The abstract's own summary: "it is unclear the general public have benefitted from these enhanced central bank communications", and "there are compelling reasons for central banks to continue trying to target communication at the public". *(Evidence.)*

Bholat, Broughton, Parker, Ter Meer and Walczak (Bank of England Staff Working Paper 750, August 2018 — the brief's 2019 citation is the *Journal of Monetary Economics* version, 108, 1–15, with four authors) ran four arms with "A total of 2,275 respondents" from an online panel matched to UK gender, age, region and income (read once): the Monetary Policy Summary; the published Visual Summary; a Reduced Text version with half the words; a Relatable Summary. Five comprehension questions; trust on a scale where "0 means 'distrust completely' and 10 means 'trust completely.'"; perception of the Bank on a five-point scale (read once). Results: "compared to the Monetary Policy Summary, the Visual Summary improved the average comprehension score by 25% (0.5 points)"; Reduced Text "improved the average comprehension score by 30% (0.6 points)"; "the Relatable Summary improved direct comprehension scores by 42% (0.8 points) relative to the Monetary Policy Summary" — "equivalent to answering, on average, 2.85 out of 5 questions correctly in the Relatable Summary condition, compared to 2.02 on average in the Monetary Policy Summary condition" (read once); "The Reduced Text Summary increased comprehension by a small amount, but not in a statistically significantly way compared to the current Visual Summary." (read once). Trust: "We found a small but statistically significant difference in trust for participants that read the Relatable Summary"; "There were no other statistically significant differences across treatments." The stated limit: participants were paid, "whereas visitors to the Bank's website in reality do not receive remuneration" (read once). The journal abstract: "the simplification of language increases public comprehension more than the inclusion of visuals" (†). *(Evidence.)*

Around the experiments sit the Bank's own statements of why it layers — Haldane's "stylised fact" that "around 95% of all the words central banks utter are inaccessible to around 95% of the population", which he calls a stylised fact and is not a measurement; "a one-sentence, one-graphic version for the time-poor; a 60-page chart-tastic technical text for the masochist"; "The number of website hits has increased markedly since publication of our first layered Inflation Report in November last year", with no figure — and a later paper's caution that "recent central bank efforts to simplify language reduced traditional syntactic measures, but conceptual complexity has actually increased" (McMahon & Naylor, 2023, †): shorter sentences were achieved while the ideas got harder. No Bank evaluation of the visual summary later than 2018 was found, and in October 2025 the Bank announced that "A new Monetary Policy Overview will set out how the analytical inputs have informed the MPC's collective policy decision." (read once). *(Practice; Evidence, †.)*

What this gives the house is an evaluation design — a randomised reading test with comprehension, expectation and perception measured separately — and a finding to carry: plainer wording moved comprehension at least as much as the pictures did (the journal abstract puts it more strongly, †), and comprehension moved more than trust. *(Reading.)*

### The fan chart: a printed band rule, a review that said eliminate it, and a test that said keep it

The Bank's inflation fan chart is the sector's best-documented uncertainty display, and the chapter reads it as a case in how a rule is printed and changed. The 1998 description: "The fan chart was chosen to meet the criterion that it should give information on the whole of the forecast distribution, without claiming a spuriously high degree of precision." (two passes); the band rule — the central band holds "10% of the distribution", "That band is coloured the deepest shade of red", "Each pair of bands covers 10% of the distribution", "Pairs of bands continue to be added until 90% of the distribution is covered", "There is an equal number of red bands on either side of the central band (eight)"; and the purpose, "It was hoped that this would promote discussion of the risks to the economic outlook, rather than on small changes to the central projection." (read once). By November 2025 the rule had changed and the footnote said so: "The fan charts are constructed so that outturns are expected to lie within each pair of lighter areas on 30 occasions, with outturns expected to lie within the fan on 90 out of 100 occasions." (read once; second clause verified); calibrated to match historical forecast errors since 2004 "but exclude errors during the pandemic given its exceptional nature"; "The Committee no longer uses the calibration of the fan chart skew to reflect judgements on the balance of risks to the central projection."; and for output, "the distribution reflects uncertainty around past data revisions and future evolution" — the fan extends back over recent quarters, because the published figures for those quarters will themselves be revised. The band convention changed from 10 per cent steps to 30 per cent steps under the same name, and only the footnote records it. *(Practice; Standard (method).)*

The Bernanke review (April 2024), recommendation 11: "Despite their distinguished history, the fan charts as published in the MPR have weak conceptual foundations, convey little useful information over and above what could be communicated in other, more direct ways, and receive little attention from the public." "They should be eliminated." — while "it remains important to communicate the degree of forecast uncertainty and the balance of risks" (read once). The review's "little attention from the public" cites no survey; a pass listing every fan-chart sentence in it found none. What the Bank did (October 2025): "For now, we will continue to provide fan charts, based on historical forecast errors"; "MPC judgements about the balance of risks will be communicated through qualitative statements, rather than judgemental adjustments to the fan charts". And what was then measured (McMahon, Naylor, Rholes & Rickards, Staff Working Paper 1,196, July 2026; not yet peer-reviewed): "Our first sample consists of 300 individuals representative of the UK general public, recruited via the Prolific online survey platform" and "Our second sample comprises 75 experts", shown a point forecast, a fan chart, a range, a box-and-whisker, a dot plot and a speedometer (read once): "We find that fan charts are well understood and perform best at jointly conveying both expectations and uncertainty"; a second study of "1,600 UK participants across four stages" (†) found fan charts act "as an 'insurance policy' that helps protect central bank reputation" and that "the public consistently underestimates the degree of uncertainty". It is the Bank's own staff testing the Bank's own chart, and the paper names its motive: the review "recommended discontinuing fan charts while emphasising the continued importance of communicating forecast uncertainty". An earlier perception study (Tak, Toet & van Erp, 2014, †) with "Participants (n = 140)" concluded that "perceived certainty is affected by its visual representation" and that reading depends on numeracy. *(Standard (method); Evidence.)*

The Office for Budget Responsibility's version prints the same kind of rule and the same kind of refusal: "The chances of any economic or fiscal forecast being accurate in every dimension are infinitesimally small"; bands of "10 per cent probability bands"; "The final fan charts do not represent our assessment of specific risks to the central forecast"; "Neither can they fully capture the extreme range of potential outcomes"; later, simulations "generating a very large number (thousands) of scenarios" with four bands a side "implying an 80 per cent chance that debt will be within the fan in each year"; and the plainest statement of what a fan is — the bands "show the outcomes that someone might anticipate if they believed that forecast errors in the past 20 years offered a reasonable guide to future forecast errors" (read once). Across the sector, "Fan charts are the most common tool, published by almost 75% of the 25 central banks in our sample in 2025" (BIS, March 2026), with the caution that "If too wide, fan charts risk diluting the central bank's messages about the outlook and raising questions about its forecasting ability". *(Standard (method); Practice.)*

The mechanism the house can borrow is not the chart, which needs a forecast-error history most projects lack. It is the footnote convention: a sentence on the chart stating what share of outcomes each shade should contain, what history calibrates it, and what was excluded and why — and the lesson that a display's rule can change under the same picture, so the rule must be printed with the date. *(Reading.)*

### Risk dashboards: mostly without ranks, and a board that prints what its display is not

The European Systemic Risk Board's risk dashboard (Issue 56, July 2026) "is a set of quantitative and qualitative indicators of systemic risk in the EU financial system", "published quarterly, one week after its adoption by the General Board", with two annexes on methodology and indicators; "The risk dashboard should not be considered to be a policy statement on systemic risks"; "The risk dashboard is a set of quantitative indicators and not an early-warning system"; "Users may not rely on the indicators as a basis for any mechanical form of inference"; and on its face a cut-off — "Data available to the ECB by Friday, 12 June 2026 were taken into account". It uses no colour coding and no rank; its only thresholds are reference lines such as a treaty's debt ratio (read once). The one regulator display read with a printed traffic light is the European Banking Authority's: "Traffic lights provide the trend of the RI risk indicator given the historical time series"; a colour scale "green for the "best bucket", yellow for the intermediate and red for the "worst bucket""; fixed absolute thresholds per indicator (two rows confirmed twice: non-performing loans under 3 per cent, 3 to 8, over 8; return on equity over 10, 6 to 10, under 6 — the rest must be read from the PDF by eye); and colour applied to the share of banks' assets in each bucket rather than to named institutions (read once). The Federal Reserve's staff heat map standardises each series against its own history — "We first standardize the raw data by subtracting from each series its average value or its trend (10-year moving average) then dividing by its time-series standard deviation."; "44 indicators", "14 component sources of vulnerability" — and states the cost: "the arrival of additional data can change the standardized and rescaled values for the entire time series", so "the current measures of the vulnerabilities over history can differ from their "real time" counterparts" (read once). The Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee: decisions "are not mechanically tied to any level or rate of change for an individual or specific combination of indicators; rather, the FPC makes a comprehensive, qualitative assessment informed by the indicators" — a table of numbers, no colour. *(Standard (regulator); Practice.)*

The borrowable things are the systemic-risk board's two sentences and a date — what the display is not; that no mechanical inference may be drawn from it; the cut-off date on the face — and, where colour is used, the banking authority's fixed printed thresholds applied to shares rather than to named units. The other two displays read print their own version of the first sentence. The Fed's heat map adds the cost of colouring against history: old cells recolour when new data arrive. *(Reading.)*

### Vintages and revisions: keeping what was known when

Finance keeps old versions of its figures because results change when the figures do. ALFRED, the St Louis Fed's archive, "is a collection of vintage versions of U.S. economic data"; "ALFRED makes it possible to gather data as reported by a source on past dates in history", to "reproduce past research, build more accurate forecasting models, and analyze economic policy decisions of the past with data that were available at the time"; its strapline, "Economic data time travel since 2006." The reason, from Croushore and Stark (*Journal of Econometrics*, 2001; read in its working-paper text of 1999): "In many cases, however, the data that should be used in these studies are not the (final, revised) data available from government statistical agencies today, but rather the original, unrevised data available to economic agents who were around at the time." — and the demonstration, that re-running a classic test on the same period with later data, "the coefficients change dramatically" (the remainder of the sentence read once); with the balance that "for many of the studies we examine, the results are generally robust, at least qualitatively, for different vintages of the data" (read once). *(Practice; Evidence.)*

The ONS writes the distinction the chapter adopts: "A revision is a scheduled change to any published Office for National Statistics (ONS) output" and "A correction is an unplanned revision to any published ONS output because of the identification of an error." (read once); "Changes as a result of a correction will be clearly labelled as a correction rather than a revision"; "The scope of future planned revisions is regularly announced in advance of the annual reassessment of data"; and "Revisions triangles supporting this information are also published on the ONS website, to allow users to analyse past revisions for themselves." (read once). It prints the size of its own revisions in the headline release: "Early estimates of GDP are subject to revision (positive or negative)"; "since Quarter 1 2000 there is a mean revision of positive 0.08 percentage points between the first estimate and the final estimate, which is published three years later"; "The mean absolute revision between the first and final estimates is 0.27 percentage points"; and in the method document, "revisions are typically small (around 0.1 to 0.2 percentage points), with the frequency of upward and downward revisions broadly equal" because "Revisions are an inevitable consequence of the trade-off between timeliness and accuracy". *(Standard (official statistics).)*

Who sees a figure first is a trust rule with a statute behind it. "As of 1 July 2017, pre-release access to Office for National Statistics (ONS) statistics was removed in all but exceptional circumstances", because "Equality of access to official statistics is a fundamental principle of statistical good practice"; other producers remain under the 2008 Order, under which a producer "may not grant pre-release access for a period in excess of 24 hours prior to the time set for the publication", must to "restrict pre-release access to the minimum number of persons", allows longer only where "the public benefit likely to result from such access significantly outweighs the detriment to public trust in official statistics" (read once), and requires a published "list of the job title and organisation of persons to whom the person responsible has granted pre-release access" (read once). The IMF's data standard asks for the same at the level of a country: an advance release calendar that "provides release dates for the current month and at least the following three months", "simultaneous release to all interested parties", "the identification of internal government access to data before release to the public", and "advance notice of major changes in methodology" (read once). *(Standard (official statistics); Standard (law); Standard (method).)*

### Uncertainty and trust, measured

Van der Bles, van der Linden, Freeman and Spiegelhalter (*PNAS*, 2020) ran five experiments, "total n = 5,780", including a field experiment in a BBC News article (n = 1,531, read once): "we observed only a small decrease in trust in numbers and trustworthiness of the source, and mostly for verbal uncertainty communication"; in the field, a range beside the point estimate "did not affect participants' trust in the number"; the recommendation, "the communication of uncertainty around basic facts and numbers in the media is best conveyed through numerical ranges with a point estimate". Galvão and Mitchell (ESCoE, 2021; later in the *Journal of Money, Credit and Banking*) ran "two waves of a randomized controlled online experiment" on UK GDP — "more than 3,000 (nationally representative) adults" in 2018 (read once) and "more than 4,000 adults" in 2020 — because "Economic statistics are commonly published without any explicit indication of their uncertainty": communicating it "encourages the public not to take estimates at face-value, but does not decrease trust in the data"; "improves the public's understanding of data uncertainty, but does not reduce its trust in the statistical office."; the formats that worked were "notably intervals, density strips and bell curves"; and "The textual uncertainty qualifier given to Group 2 tends to have little effect." (read once). The trust item was the statistical office's: "Personally, how much trust do you have in economic statistics produced by the Office for National Statistics (ONS)?" (read once). *(Evidence.)*

The regulator has drawn the conclusion for the sector. On GDP revisions (November 2023): "the uncertainty surrounding these statistics is in itself a key message"; "We consider that ONS has been clear in its communications in 2023 that revisions are normal and that in less-volatile times, revisions are small." And on where uncertainty goes missing (September 2022): "We found that uncertainty is typically better depicted and described in statistical bulletins and methodological documents than it is in data tables, data dashboards and downloadable datasets." — the regulator naming dashboards, with tables and datasets, as where uncertainty goes missing. *(Standard (regulator).)*

### What finance gets wrong, documented

Cox's *What's Wrong with Risk Matrices?* (*Risk Analysis*, 2008, †): "Typical risk matrices can correctly and unambiguously compare only a small fraction (e.g., less than 10%) of randomly selected pairs of hazards."; for some risks they can be "worse than useless," leading to worse-than-random decisions (read once); "different users may obtain opposite ratings of the same quantitative risks" (read once); they "should be used with caution, and only with careful explanations of embedded judgments". A mathematical argument rather than an experiment, against the traffic-light grid as a decision device. The sector's other documented faults are the ones its own texts admit: a review that asserted public inattention without evidence; a heat map whose history recolours; an aggregate that, the ECB wrote of its own 2011 indicator, "did not reflect the crisis in certain euro area countries" (read once); shorter sentences hiding harder ideas; and the statistics regulator's warning (2023) that "seemingly small changes – 0.1%, 0.2% etc" are over-read when a figure is called "estimated" without context (read once). *(Evidence of method; Reading.)*

### The marks on a provisional number

Both the European and the UK systems print a figure's status beside it. Eurostat's flags: "b = break in time series", "e = estimated", "f = forecast", "p = provisional", "u = low reliability", with ": = not available" (read once). The UK's Analysis Function, in its symbols guidance (January 2022): "[p] = provisional", "[r] = revised", "[f] = forecast"; "Whenever you use a shorthand marker in a table it should be in square brackets, for example: '[r]'."; and "We do not recommend using symbols such as '. :', '*' or '†' anymore because they can be difficult to see and screen reader software can find them hard to recognise." (read once) — which this book's own dagger convention meets in Chapter 16. *(Standard (official statistics).)*

What finance got right: vintages kept and addressable by date; revision and correction as two labelled things, with the revision's size printed; a release calendar and a published list of early readers; uncertainty as a printed band rule, dated; a sentence on every regulator's display saying it is not a verdict; and its own readers tested, with the finding that ranges do not cost trust and hedges do not help. What it got wrong: the look of precision in grids that cannot rank what they colour; a review that recommended dropping a chart without citing evidence about its readers; and conceptual complexity rising as sentences shortened. *(Reading.)*

## 5 · Economic development and place

These are the Vibe Map's nearest kin: public displays about named areas, built from official statistics, read by councils, funders and residents. Their technical documents are where the sector warns against the uses its own displays invite.

### The ONS's local statistics service, behind the landing page

Chapters 11, 12 and 14 read the Explore Local Statistics service's pages; this section reads its method and quality documents. Its stated users include "inquiring citizens who are interested in finding out more about their local areas", alongside analysts and policy officers (read once). Its metadata record per indicator, from the quality report (read once as a list): "title, description, link to the original statistical release, last updated date, department name of the data owner, any caveats, interpretation notes, whether the indicator is standardised …, whether the indicator is marked as official statistics in development, unit of measure, appropriate decimal place for the data". Its rules: "We provide confidence intervals, which give an indication of the uncertainty, when they are available in the original statistical release"; "We aim to update within three weeks of the updated statistics becoming available", with the lag stated as a limitation (read once); an indicator page carries "About these estimates", "Get the data", four views ("Map", "Line chart", "Bar chart", "Table") and the count — each indicator "is one of 116 local indicators on the Explore local statistics service". *(Standard (official statistics); Practice.)*

Its "similar areas" method is fully specified: "These groups are created by using K-means clustering on a range of publicly available data sources"; "Standardisation involves transforming each indicator to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one"; "We optimise the number of clusters between 4 and 15 for each of our models", with principal components to reduce the indicators and silhouette scores to choose (read once); an "ordered list of the 20 most similar areas to the area of interest, ordered by Euclidean distance"; a global model beside economic and demographic ones (read once); and two caveats — the clustering "is designed as a snapshot, and should not be viewed as comparable over time with previous versions", and "the nearest neighbours for an area may not all be in the same cluster". Chapter 12's peer method now has an official specification to cite. *(Standard (official statistics).)*

Its reach evidence is thin. A case study claims "93% of users found it easy to use" and "57% rated it" extremely useful, with no sample, date, method or denominator; the quality report says "We regularly conduct user research on the service." and "We run a feedback survey in the service" (read once); no participant numbers or findings are published anywhere reached. The service sits under a subnational data strategy whose ambitions are to "Produce more timely, granular and harmonised subnational statistics", "Build capability and capacity for subnational statistics and analysis" and "Improve the dissemination of subnational statistics", with data "collected and recorded at the lowest possible geographical level" (the strategy's quotation of the GSS geography policy) and "flexible user-defined areas". *(Practice; Standard (official statistics).)*

### Nomis: forty-five years, a revisions log per dataset, no usage figure

"Nomis is run by the University of Durham on behalf of the Office for National Statistics"; "First launched in 1981"; "the most detailed and up-to-date statistics from official sources for local areas throughout the UK"; "All the facilities and data downloads on our Web site are free"; "You can access all the data on our site without registering". A dataset page carries "Revisions and corrections" and "Contact details for this dataset" as section labels, a dated one-line revisions log ("Estimates revised due to identified error"), the reference period ("This is the reference period for the most recent figures available from this dataset"), a release calendar with "Next update", "Status" ("Confirmed" or "Provisional") and a fixed release time ("Data is usually released at 7am on the release day"), and a two-tier access rule — open rounded data, licensed unrounded data (read once). In 2004 it was scheduled to be folded into a neighbourhood statistics service it has since outlived (read once). It publishes no usage figure on any page reached. *(Practice.)*

### The deprivation indices: what a rank may and may not say

The English Indices of Deprivation 2025 (published 30 October 2025) carry the genre's best usage text, and the chapter corrects its own brief: the "twice as deprived" rule it expected is not in the FAQ; what exists is two sentences in two documents, one about ranks and one about scores. On ranks, the FAQ: "For example, a small area with a rank of 1,000 is not half as deprived as a place with a rank of 500." (verified); "This means that each can tell you if one area is more deprived than another but not by how much." (read once); the research report, "they show that one area is more deprived than another but not by how much". On scores: "an area with a score of 60 on the IMD is not simply twice as deprived as an area with a score of 30"; "it is recommended that ranks and deciles, but not scores, are used". On what it measures: the Index "is designed to identify aspects of deprivation, not affluence"; a high-ranked area "is not necessarily among the most affluent"; "the Indices are specifically constructed to identify deprivation and not affluence"; "the Indices discriminate well between deprived neighbourhoods but not so well between those areas in the less deprived part of the distribution". On time: "Indices releases should not be viewed or construed as a time-series"; "But the Indices are not designed to provide 'backwards' comparability with previous iterations"; "Updates and changes to methods and datasets come at the expense of" backwards comparability; and the one "can": comparison across editions "does allow some comparisons to be made, but only in terms of comparing the rankings as a snapshot in time, accompanied by appropriate caveats" (read once); "users should be aware, and make clear in analysis, that such changes are relative to other areas"; and an unchanged rank "does not mean that there have been no changes to the level of deprivation in the area". On the decile: "dividing them into 10 equal groups" — with the admission that the bands "are not precisely equal" (read once) — and "although there is no definitive cut-off at which an area is described as 'deprived'"; "The Index is not a suitable tool for targeting individuals." (read once); "No single summary measure is the 'best' measure". The method behind the rules: small-area estimates are shrunk because "the resulting estimate may be unreliable, with an unacceptably high standard error", using "weighted combination of that small area's score and the mean value for a larger grouping of areas"; the domain weights "are unchanged since the construction of the IMD 2004"; "The exponential transformation stretches out the deprived end of the distribution". No rank intervals are published. *(Standard (official statistics).)*

Its users asked for what it cannot give. The 2022 consultation response: "In total, 117 respondents replied to the Indices Futures' consultation", most from local government; "Users requested that a release calendar be made available to aid planning"; "The Indices are typically updated every 3 to 5 years"; "They cannot be used to identify real change in deprivation over time". The FAQ's form — a question, a "can" or a "cannot", and a worked example with numbers — is the mechanism; it is borrowable as a form of words wherever a display orders places. *(Standard (official statistics); Reading.)*

### The Health Index: built for change over time, and stalled without a notice

The ONS Health Index for England "consists of 56 indicators" in fourteen subdomains and three domains (read once); "The Health Index has been scaled to a base of 100 for England, with a base year of 2015"; "a score of 110 represents a score that is one 2015 standard deviation higher than England 2015's score"; "A higher number always means better health and a lower number means worse health"; and the design goal, "meaning comparisons can be made between scores over time and between different geographies" — the standardisation parameters frozen in the base year, so that the method "only causes back-series changes when the reference year is updated" (read once). "Equal weighting is used to weight the three domains"; robustness by method comparison, with correlations "above 0.9" between methods (read once); "Just over 22% of all records were affected by imputation in some way"; and the disclosure the chapter would borrow from it — "For 21 of 56 indicators, change may have been expected in 2021 but could not be measured because of unavailable or inconsistent underlying data". Its last release was 16 June 2023; on 6 October 2026 its bulletin read "Next release: To be announced"; no discontinuation or pause notice was found on any page reached or by search. The accurate description is an index built to track change, not updated for over three years, with no published status found. *(Standard (official statistics); Practice.)*

### Two nations' arrows

Scotland's National Performance Framework labels each of "81 National Indicators" as improving, maintaining or worsening: "Performance is assessed as improving, maintaining or worsening based on the change between the last two data points of an indicator"; made "independently of Scottish Government Ministers" by senior analysts (read once); with two declared non-states, "Performance to be confirmed" and "Indicator in development" (the second read once). There is no single threshold; the rule lives indicator by indicator in the prose of the consolidated performance page — a significance rule ("This increase represents a significant change at the 95% confidence interval and the arrow is therefore performance improving"), a margin rule against a three-year average ("more than 0.1 percentage points above"), and references to unprinted methodology ("This suggests that performance is maintaining, under the methodology for this National Indicator") — and the per-indicator pages that carried each rule now redirect to a collection page announcing "a set of streamlined national indicators" "aligned with the UN Sustainable Development Goals". The thresholds could not be retrieved. Wales prints its rule compactly: "We assign each measure as either improved, deteriorated, no change or not assessed"; "Where possible confidence intervals are used to assess if the change is statistically significant"; "For some of the indicators that are not based on survey data, confidence intervals are not available", in which case "change over time has been assessed based on guidance from the data expert" (read once); "Change is assessed since 2015"; and "We have not considered whether the milestones are on course to be met, simply the direction of change." (read once). *(Standard (official statistics); Practice.)*

Read together with Fingertips: an arrow is a rule, and the rule must be printed where the arrow is, dated, and kept when the pages move. Scotland's comparison of the last two data points means one unusual year can flip the arrow; Wales's fallback to expert judgement is declared and not reproducible; both are better than an arrow with no rule, and worse than five independent points, a significance test and a leave-one-out check. *(Reading.)*

### The OECD's refusal to aggregate

The OECD Regional Well-Being tool compares a region with "467 OECD regions" across "eleven topics" (income, jobs, housing, health, access to services, environment, education, safety, civic engagement and governance, community, life satisfaction — read once), and its user guide states a choice the house has made elsewhere in this book: "The web tool does not include a regional composite well-being index"; "we do not make a single statement about the overall well-being in a region"; because "The trade-off between a composite index (which conveys a single unified view, but may dilute information) and a range of indicators (which offers detailed information, but is more difficult to communicate) is widely debated." (read once). Its scale is relative and says so — "A higher score indicates better performance in a topic relative to all the other regions", min–max normalised to 0–10 with outliers trimmed at the 5th and 95th percentiles for two indicators (read once); its peers are "based on the sum of the absolute differences in the topics scores", "the so-called Manhattan distance", and "The top four regions from different countries with the lowest distance to the selected region are displayed"; and its arrow rule is one sentence: "Well-being trends compare the score of the region from the most recent year to its score in 2000 or the closest year available"; "It shows if the region has progressed in the topic, relatively to the other regions"; "Evolution of the score above +5% or below -5% over the period is considered respectively as an improvement" "(increasing arrow) or a decline (decreasing arrow), otherwise as a stable situation (horizontal arrow)" (read once). No significance test. *(Standard (method); Practice.)*

Four mechanisms, three with a catch: a printed refusal to aggregate, with the reason; a relative score that moves when other regions move; a dead-band arrow against a fixed start year that measures change in a relative score, not in the thing measured; and peers forced to be in other countries. *(Reading.)*

### Ranked tables, and a scale for evidence

The Centre for Cities' data tool compares "the 63 largest urban areas on 17 indicators" (read once) "defined as primary urban areas (PUAs)" "rather than individual local authority areas", and ranks them: tables headed "10 cities with the highest" and "10 cities with the lowest", with a "Rank" column; no confidence intervals; no warning found about comparison with earlier editions. It is the opposite convention to the OECD's, and the chapter reads it as a contrast rather than a mechanism. The What Works Centre for Local Economic Growth's evidence standard is a mechanism: "We define SMS 3 methods to be the minimum standard for our reviews"; level 3 requires "a comparison group used to provide a counterfactual (e.g. difference in difference)"; level 5 is "Reserved for research designs that involve explicit randomisation into treatment and control groups"; because "Establishing causality requires the construction of a valid counterfactual" and "That outcome is fundamentally unobservable, so researchers spend a great deal of time trying to rebuild it"; "The ranking of individual studies is not an exact science and often involves a degree of judgement"; and in use, "We identified 53 studies that met our evidence standards", "Eleven studies were scored as SMS 4 and 42 SMS 3", excluding before-and-after comparisons "because we cannot be sure that changes can be attributed to the effect of the EZ", with the standing cautions "Be cautious of findings based on a small number of studies" and "None of the studies considers cost effectiveness". The sector's own statement that a before-and-after change is description rather than impact, which is Chapter 7's finding in an evidence centre's words. *(Practice; Standard (method).)*

### The levelling-up metrics: announced before they existed

The 2022 technical annex set twelve missions (read once) with headline and supporting metrics at mixed geographies, and named its own gaps in the same document: "but not by region, and this is an important data gap"; "most proxy measures of institutional capital are not produced in a timely and consistent manner"; "This mission is exploratory"; "There are considerable challenges to developing measures for pride of place"; "there are currently no official UK Government statistics that provide this data at a regional level" (read once). The 2024 statement promised "metrics that are publicly available and routinely updated", noted that "In some cases, metrics listed are England only or England and Wales only", and bound the government: "The Government will be required to report annually against its missions and metrics and lay the report before Parliament". The May 2025 and July 2026 annual reports: "This Government has moved away from the levelling up programme"; "Social Fabric Index: not implemented"; metrics not refreshed because producers "have not published new data since last year's report"; and an intention to repeal the Part of the Act that requires the report. No successor dashboard is referenced; the old subnational indicators explorer now serves the ONS's local statistics page. *(Standard (official statistics); Practice.)*

The lesson for change over time is in the format and the fate together: a per-mission statement of metric, geography and data gap is a borrowable form; a metric set tied to a programme has the programme's life, and its report outlives it only by law. *(Reading.)*

### Place indices evaluated, audited and abandoned

Three further pieces. The Scottish Government's published evaluation of its internal "near time data service" dashboards (August 2024) — a management tool, read here only for its evaluation design — is a borrowable template because it printed its denominators: "41 users completed the survey in Microsoft Forms. This equates to a 5% response rate."; "809 individuals were invited."; "24 participants took part: 14 dashboard users and 10 developers."; "Around 400 individual users had accessed a dashboard. The dashboards were opened around 6,500 times." (all read once) — and asked the question that measures use: whether the data informed "their decisions or conclusions as part of their role" (49 per cent of 41, so about twenty people; read once). A statistical audit of a global index (the JRC's of the Global Innovation Index 2024) shows what a rank with an interval looks like: "All published GII 2024 ranks lie within the simulated 90 percent confidence intervals"; "These confidence intervals also have to be taken into account when comparing economy rank changes from one year to the next"; from "a total of 5,000 Monte Carlo simulations" (read once) over missing-data treatment, weights and aggregation formula — Chapter 5's rank interval, done annually by an auditor. And County Health Rankings, the best-known ranked place index in the United States, stopped ranking: "Beginning in 2024, Health Groups are displayed in the county health snapshot", "ten unequally-sized groups based on the data" that "groups counties based on similarity and meaningful gaps in the data", with margins of error "Where possible"; nine years earlier an editorial in the field (Arndt, 2015) had argued from simulation that "rankings can be too unreliable to be helpful", that "Rankings are driven, in part, by random noise", and that "the degree to which the rankings are random directly drives the degree to which any decisions based on them are random". *(Evidence of method; Practice.)*

### Describing change, in the statisticians' words

The Government Statistical Service's guidance on communicating change (2018; "currently under review", read once): "Describe the direction of change, its absolute size and its relative size over time"; "Emphasise that changes within the bounds of random variation are indicative, rather than definitive"; "Phrases like 'care must be taken' and 'exercise caution' are not sufficient by themselves, because they do not help the reader to understand what they can and cannot do with the numbers." (fragment verified); a model sentence, "This decrease is not statistically significant and it is likely that the natural variation in the figures explains the change"; and "Use phrases like 'broadly stable'" where it is too early to say (read once). It says nothing specific to small areas; the nearest official sentence is a small-area GVA release's "We advise a degree of caution when making comparisons between data at the most granular (building block) level" "because data at the lowest levels are more volatile" — which, by the guidance's own standard, is the insufficient kind of phrase. *(Standard (official statistics); Reading.)*

What economic development got right: a per-indicator metadata record and a refresh target with its lag admitted; a fully specified similar-areas method with a snapshot caveat; a revisions log per dataset and a release calendar with status; a paired "can and cannot say" text for ranks and deciles, with the reason comparability is lost; a base-year index with frozen parameters and a count of what could not be measured; a printed refusal to aggregate; an evidence scale with a declared minimum; a model sentence for non-significant change. What it got wrong or left open: arrows whose rules were per-indicator and are now unfindable; an index built for change that stopped without saying so; metrics announced before they existed; ranked tables with no interval and no edition caveat; reach evidenced by percentages without an n; users asking an index for the one thing its makers say it cannot give. *(Reading.)*

## 6 · The culture and charity sectors' impact practice

This is the sector whose instruments the studio will be asked to use, and whose codes it will be measured against. Read for method, it is a cautionary tale with a few good forms of words in it. Its instruments, in order of appearance: the *quality metrics*, a set of twelve dimension statements rated on sliders by an organisation, its peers and its public, tested nationally in 2016; the *Impact & Insight Toolkit*, the platform built on them, which is live; *Illuminate*, the funder's separate mandatory audience-data platform, live since 2023; *Audience Finder* and *Audience Spectrum*, the audience-data service and the segmentation that the Audience Agency ran; and the national participation survey. (Three things the chapter's own brief expected were not found: the Toolkit did not end in 2023 and Illuminate did not replace it; no independent evaluation of the Toolkit itself exists in anything reached, only the 2016 evaluation of the national test; and the official guidance on valuing culture carries no warning about economic multipliers.)

### A profile instrument, evaluated before it became a condition

Chapter 5 read the 2016 national test of the quality metrics as a profile instrument. What it did not have was the independent evaluation of the test, which surveyed the organisations and assessors who took part. Nordicity (6 September 2016) surveyed "a total population ('N') of 316 contacts, receiving a total sample size ('n') of 209 responses and accounting for an overall 66% response rate" — organisations 97 of 144, peer assessors 112 of 172 — with "19 one-on-one interviews, four focus groups across the country with 20 individuals". Findings: "Overall, a majority (63%) of respondent organisations either strongly agreed or somewhat agreed that the QM framework was useful to their organisation"; "nearly half (47%) of respondent organisations somewhat or strongly agreed that the time and resources spent on the CC platform was appropriate"; "For those who had completed full triangulation, the resulting data was regularly referenced as 'interesting' rather than 'useful'."; "the majority of organisations do not see that it will be effective as a tool for benchmarking with other organisations"; "it was commonly reporting that Audience Finder had to be deprioritised" (as printed); on the slider, "there were concerns that public and self-assessors completed it too quickly without much thought on positioning (and with a natural movement to 'swipe' to the right)"; "enthusiasm is measured and contingent upon a number of key amendments and assurances"; "clearer articulation of the purpose of the QM programme is needed to ensure buy-in". *(Evidence of method.)*

The quality metrics then became, for the larger funded organisations, a requirement: Selwood records that the funder "requires those NPOs in receipt of at least £250,000 of its funding to use the Toolkit to report how their peers and publics rated at least four examples of their work per year", and the Toolkit's own history says version 1 was commissioned "to be used by band 2 and 3 National Portfolio Organisations (NPO) and other ACE funded organisations and projects from 2019-2022 (extended to 2023 as a result of the COVID-19 Pandemic)" (read once); today "it is not mandatory to sign up to the Impact & Insight Toolkit", except that for one programme "Toolkit registration and Culture Counts use is a funding requirement"; "Culture Counts is a data processor."; "It is not owned by Arts Council England."; and the provider's own account, "As of October 2025, there are over 800 organisations registered to the Impact & Insight Toolkit project; over 500 of which are NPOs." with the admission that "many NPOs were anxious about the Impact & Insight Toolkit when it was launched, particularly about how the resulting data might be used by Arts Council England." Selwood's analysis (*Cultural Trends*, 2019) names the structural problem: "NPOs are potentially compromised by their dual role as data providers and grant recipients, as is ACE in its capacity as an official statistics provider and development agency."; "The Toolkit's operation, which is largely determined by both vested and conflicting interests, contrasts starkly with the ways in which Taking Part, and ACE's other official statistics are expected to operate under UKSA's jurisdiction." The other academic critiques could be read only in abstract or not at all (Gilmore, Glow & Johanson 2017, †; Phiddian et al. 2017, not read). *(Practice; Evidence of method.)*

### The mandatory platform

Illuminate "is the Arts Council's Audience Data platform.", "Built by PwC.", live from "Thursday 1 June 2023"; "All funded organisations must use Illuminate to report to the Arts Council on funded activity and audience or participant numbers."; "Over 1,000 organisations are using the platform and we have seen more than 64,000 activities reported and over 220,000 survey responses collected via Illuminate in the first year."; it carries no dimension sliders and does not mention quality or Audience Spectrum. Its mandatory survey requirement was suspended twice — to April 2024 (read once), then "by an additional year" — with the funder's reason as reported in the trade press (secondary), "We made this decision to give organisations more time to adapt to the new reporting requirements and ensure they focus on contributing the essential information." and "Following user feedback we are looking to make improvements to ensure the platform and survey collection mechanisms are as suitable and straightforward for as many users as possible"; the previous target had been "a minimum of 380 survey responses over the course of the year" per organisation. In December 2025 Baroness Hodge's independent review of the funder, as quoted by the trade press (secondary), said: "The present reporting requirements are not fit for purpose."; "They are too onerous and there is widespread scepticism about whether ACE uses all the data it demands"; "A more proportionate and targeted approach to reporting must be found"; "The Arts Council is sitting on a wealth of data and analysis that people felt is not properly shared with arts organisations"; and, in its favour, that "Illuminate has provided previously unavailable data for a range of interested parties on funding distribution and audiences." *(Practice; secondary for the review.)*

### The yardstick's owner fails

The Audience Agency's notice of 11 September 2026: "we regret to announce that The Audience Agency will enter a voluntary liquidation process"; "This decision follows sustained financial pressures and extensive efforts to identify a viable way forward"; "continuing to provide the full scope of our affordable and free services has proved impossible in the current financial climate"; the company "was placed into Liquidation on 10 September 2026"; "Significant public investment has contributed to the development of The Audience Agency's research, platforms and evidence base."; "colleagues and partners are exploring appropriate ways in which this legacy might benefit the sector in future." The notice names neither Audience Spectrum nor Audience Finder, no data transfer and no successor. The former funder, through a spokesperson quoted in the trade press (secondary): "From 2015 until March 2023, we grant-funded TAA to deliver the Audience Finder programme, which gathered large-scale ticketing and audience data from the cultural sector. We are aware that on Friday, TAA announced that it will enter voluntary liquidation, and our understanding is that organisations are currently still able to access and download their historic data. We are in contact with TAA to understand next steps." On 6 October 2026 the segmentation site still describes the tool under a 2026 copyright line and the audience-data site still invites sign-in (read once); nothing further has been published. Chapter 6's audience yardstick is orphaned, and the continuity arrangement is one sentence of "our understanding". *(Practice.)*

### The national survey, replaced with a line on the chart

The DCMS Participation Survey ended in September 2025 (read once). Its last technical report: "In total 82,513 addresses were sampled, from which 36,074 respondents completed the survey – 31,596 via the online survey and 4,478 by returning a paper questionnaire."; "This constitutes a 0.42 conversion rate (responses/sampled addresses), a 29% household-level response rate, and an individual-level response rate of 24%." Its successor, the Community and Engagement Survey: "Fieldwork for the CES began in October 2025"; "the CES replaced DCMS' Participation Survey and Community Life Survey"; "is a continuous push to web survey of adults aged 16 and over in England"; "The content for the CES has been kept broadly similar to the previous surveys but combining the surveys has resulted in efficiencies and more coherent outputs."; a later quarter's response (January to March 2026) — "a 0.25 conversion rate (responses/sampled addresses), a 27.69% household-level response rate, and an individual-level response rate of 14.65%". And the break-in-series rule, which is the mechanism: "A vertical dotted line is included in charts to indicate the change in survey name and structure, although estimates can still be compared." — a marked discontinuity and an explicit statement on comparability, in one sentence. *(Standard (official statistics).)*

### Valuing culture: what the official guidance warns against

DCMS's *Embedding a Culture and Heritage Capital Approach* (December 2024): "Standard economic measures are an incomplete measure of public welfare and value added, as they do not take into account assets and services that do not have market prices."; five steps — "Consider the culture and heritage assets"; "Consider the culture and heritage services"; "Consider the welfare impacts"; "Monetise the welfare impacts"; "Project future benefits across the asset's life to estimate total asset value"; and its cautions — appraisal "should always be conducted in a proportional way", "should also sit alongside a wider set of evidence that can inform decision making", and "One limitation that is important to be aware of is double counting between methods, and as such, some non-market valuation methods cannot be used in conjunction with each other". The words "multiplier", "GVA" and "economic impact" are reported absent. Chapter 6's gross-to-net discipline stands on its own grounds. *(Standard (official statistics).)*

### The codes, in their own words

Four frameworks the sector reports against, each quoted for what it asks. Inspiring Impact's Code of Good Impact Practice (2013; a code for charities on how to measure and report their effect; the programme "ran from 2011 until early 2022" and the Code survives re-hosted): "Take responsibility for impact and encourage others to do so too"; "Focus on purpose"; "Involve others in your impact practice"; "Apply proportionate and appropriate methods and resources"; "Consider the full range of the difference you actually make"; "Be honest and open"; "Be willing to change and act on what you find"; "Actively share your impact plans, methods, findings and learning"; with the sentence on contribution — "you're willing to accept that you may not be having the impact you intended" and that changes "may have occurred without your involvement, may be short term, or may be the result of the work of others" (read once). NPC's four pillars (2014; a think-tank's method for charity measurement): "Map your theory of change"; "Prioritise what you measure"; "Choose your level of evidence"; "Select your sources and tools"; "A theory of change should be at the heart of your approach because it shows what you want to achieve and how you plan to achieve it, setting out the causal links between your activities and your end goal."; "Choose an appropriate level of rigour of evidence that suits the needs of your stakeholders." Social Value International's principles (the standard behind social-return accounting): Involve stakeholders; Understand what changes; Value the things that matter; Only include what is material; Do not overclaim; Be transparent; Verify the result; Be responsive — with "Only claim the value that activities are responsible for creating." and, where results reach external audiences or support significant decisions, "independent assurance is required" (read once). The Impact Management Project's five dimensions (a consensus vocabulary for describing an effect), two of them the ones any claim of contribution needs: "Contribution tells us whether an enterprise's efforts resulted in outcomes that were likely better than what likely would have occurred otherwise." and "Risk tells us the likelihood that impact will be different than expected." The Charities SORP's primary text could not be read; an accountancy body's summary says the 2026 edition, effective 1 January 2026, asks the smallest charities to "consider how their work made a difference" and requires larger ones to "explain the impact the charity is making" (secondary). *(Standard (method); Practice; secondary.)*

What the sector says about its own compliance: a 2012 survey of 1,000 charities found "75% of charities measure some or all of their work" and "Only 5% of charities say that wanting to improve services is a primary motivation for increasing their impact measurement efforts."; the Charity Commission's random sample of 105 trustees' reports found "52% of the trustees' annual reports demonstrated a clear understanding of the public benefit reporting requirement, similar to last year's 51%."; among the top 100 charities by income, "the vast majority (94 per cent) of charities fail to report a single efficiency measure in any channel of communication" (Hyndman & McConville, 2016). On the sector's own numbers, improving services is rarely the stated motive for measuring, and about half of sampled reports showed a clear understanding of the basic public-benefit requirement. *(Evidence; Evidence of method.)*

### Trust in a sector that reports impact, measured for twenty years

The Charity Commission's trust tracker asks one fixed question — "on a scale of 0-10 where 10 means you trust them completely and 0 means you don't trust them at all, how much trust and confidence do you have in charities?" (read once) — in waves, annual since 2020 and every second year before (read once): 2026, "4681 completes were achieved.", mean trust 6.5, "Over half (57%) say they have high trust in charities" (7 to 10); 2025, "4092 completes were achieved.", 6.5, 57 per cent; a series running back to 2005 (6.3) through a trough of 5.5 in 2018 (read once). The drivers people name, 2026: "Most of the money raised is spent directly on the causes the charity supports" 55 per cent; "The charity makes a real difference to the people and communities that it serves by achieving its stated purpose" 51 per cent; "It is easy to see how much the charity has raised, and how this money has been spent" 40 per cent. The mechanism — one fixed question, banded, repeated, with drivers as a ranked list — is borrowable whole. What donors do with impact information is a separate measured thing: "60% of donors say they care about impact, 10% look mainly for information on impact and ultimately just 2% use an informed assessment of impact to choose between charities." (survey of 3,000; NPC 2013); and a study that tested "how donors respond to new information about a charity's effectiveness" (Karlan & Wood, 2014) was read in abstract only, with no finding quotable from it (†). *(Evidence.)*

What the culture and charity sectors got right: a pilot evaluated independently, with N, n and response rate printed, before roll-out; a marked break line with a comparability sentence; codes that name contribution and risk as things to state; a trust question held fixed for two decades. What they got wrong: an instrument whose own pilot evaluation found its triangulated data "interesting" rather than "useful" and its benchmarking doubted by most organisations, made a condition of funding; a separate mandatory platform whose core requirement had to be suspended from 2023 to 2025; data demanded of which a review reports "widespread scepticism about whether ACE uses all" (secondary); a yardstick whose custodian failed with no plan; and impact reporting of which about half of sampled reports showed a clear understanding, with improving services rarely the stated motive. *(Reading.)*

### Our World in Data as an institution

"Our World in Data is a project of the Global Change Data Lab, a non-profit organization based in the United Kingdom (Registered Charity Number 1186433)." (read once); "All visualizations, data, and articles produced by Our World in Data are completely open access under the Creative Commons BY license."; funded by foundations and "More than 4,000 people have donated over the past couple of years." ### Trust in a producer, measured in waves

Public Confidence in Official Statistics (run by NatCen for the UK Statistics Authority, which oversees the office it measures) is the fullest trust measurement read, and the one the chapter reads the others against. 2023 wave (published May 2024): "a representative sample of 2,364 adults aged 18 and over in Britain" "from 1,695 households"; "an adjusted household response rate of 21.2%"; online with paper offered to non-responders. "Most respondents trusted the Office for National Statistics (ONS), with 87% of respondents who gave an opinion indicating that they tend to trust it (71%) or trust it a great deal (16%)." (verified); "In 2023, 85% of respondents said they trusted ONS statistics"; "free from political interference (72%)"; government presents official statistics honestly "remained relatively low (32%, compared to 35% in 2021)"; "Even fewer respondents (25%) tended to agree that newspapers present official statistics honestly."; "ONS had the highest level of reported trust of all (87%) among respondents, similar to levels of trust in the courts (82%) or the Bank of England (79%)"; "Trust was lowest in the British media (25%), the Government (31%) and the UK Parliament (36%)". 2021 wave: "a representative sample of 3,398 adults"; "In 2021 PCOS was run for the first time as a stand-alone push-to-web survey."; "In 2021 89% of those able to express a view said they trust ONS and 87% said they trust ONS statistics."; "Only 35% of people able to express a view agreed that the government presents official statistics honestly"; "82% of people able to express an opinion agreed that official statistics are generally accurate, up from 78% in 2018"; and the caution the house would copy when its own method changes — "There is a need for caution when making comparisons between 2021 and earlier years of the study". The series, of those with a view: trust in the ONS 88, 88, 89, 87 (2014, 2018, 2021, 2023); free from interference 66, 73, 74, 72; "presents official statistics honestly" 28, 31, 35, 32 (2014 and 2018 figures read once; the 2023 household response rate was 21.2 per cent). No 2025 wave was found. *(Evidence.)*

The mechanism is four things: a fixed question set; waves; "of those able to express a view", said so; and a printed caution when the mode changed. The finding is the gap — between 54 and 60 points across four waves — between trust in the producer and agreement that the government presents its figures honestly, which is Chapter 2's distinction between the producer and the presenter, measured. *(Evidence; Reading.)*

### Custodians of long series

The Human Mortality Database (project overview; its Methods Protocol is at version 6, August 2025): "As much as possible, we have followed four guiding principles in creating this database: comparability, flexibility, accessibility, reproducibility."; "the database is limited by design to populations where death registration and census data are virtually complete, since this type of information is required for the uniform method used to reconstruct historical data series."; "we have not attempted to correct the data for systematic age misstatement (misreporting of age) or coverage errors"; input data published beside the estimates (read once); "assistance from database users in identifying problems is always appreciated!" Comparability by exclusion, one uniform method, inputs beside outputs, a versioned and dated protocol. The UK Data Service, "the principal repository for economic, population, and social research data in the UK" (read once), cites by persistent identifier — "Using the study's DOI will always bring the user back to this edition of this dataset, even if its location on the internet should change in the future." (read once) — and asks citers for "The precise version of the dataset used e.g. edition number or release date info" (read once); its rule for when a change earns a new identifier could not be reached. *(Practice.)*

### Ending well

The chapter has four documented endings and one undocumented one. Johns Hopkins (section 3): four weeks' notice, a reason upstream, a frozen open archive, named successors. Our World in Data (section 3): a source swap with stable URLs and variable names and the last old-source vintage archived; a dated note listing what continues and what stops, dataset by dataset. UKHSA (section 3): parallel running, a dated switch, a stated reason, a full archive with its gaps listed, twelve weeks later. The COVID Tracking Project (February 2021): "But the work itself—compiling, cleaning, standardizing, and making sense of COVID-19 data from 56 individual states and territories—is properly the work of federal public health agencies."; "We will spend the next five weeks publishing detailed guidance for data users who are looking for sources to replace our work, as well as gap analyses noting where replacement data still doesn't exist."; "we're making sure that it comes as close to lasting forever as anything can on the internet"; a final update on a named date (read once). And the Audience Agency (section 6): none of these. The regulator's guidance now asks for the plan in advance and a notice before the end. Three ways a sector's data intermediary has ended are on record: no arrangement (the Audience Agency); re-hosting by a former partner (Inspiring Impact's Code); a planned wind-down with archive and gap analysis (the COVID Tracking Project). *(Practice; Standard (regulator).)*

What the great projects share is a short list of practices, each with a counterpart line in one of this book's records, some of which the book already carried in another form: a release calendar or a stated cadence; a methods document; a change log; a stated next update; open data under a named licence; a named custodian; and, in the best cases, a written ending. The reach they report is in pageviews and media citations, labelled as such; the trust they enjoy is measured for the statistics office and inferred for the rest. *(Reading.)*

## 9 · What is borrowed, as method

Each item below is a mechanism that passes the borrowing test — rule printed, reason stated, evidence or cost known — stated with its terms. Beyond the seven truths, none is a decision; section 12 says whose the decisions are, and the precedent record in section 11 is where each would be written if adopted.

**From public health.** A documentation page per metric with fixed headings (definition, rationale, methodology, caveats; add source and frequency, which the UKHSA template lacks and the Fingertips record has); cost: one page per metric, kept. A change log apart from the refresh, with a label vocabulary and issue/resolved pairs; reason, from the dashboard teams: disclose errors as they happen; cost: the discipline of keeping it. Event date rather than report date, a stated incompleteness window in the metric's page, the window marked on the chart with the direction of its bias, and the by-publication series kept and labelled as not a trend; condition: a series with late returns. A colour given as a judgement only for a significant difference from a named comparator, a neutral colour by default, a neutral ramp where there is no good direction (Fingertips' choices are amber and blue; the house's palette is its own), and the one-in-twenty sentence printed; condition: confidence intervals, or numerators and denominators from which to make them. An arrow only after five independent annual points, a stated test and a leave-one-out check, and otherwise no arrow and a neutral marker; condition: five annual points — which, for a new series, means no arrows until they exist, and a printed reason why. Two ledgers for error: a machine-readable errata file for the house's own, a dated prose list with reasons for its sources'; reason: a reader can tell whose mistake moved a figure; evidence: none on readers. A voluntary statement of which Code requirements the display meets and cannot; cost: a page. A retirement with notice, reason, archive and successor; reason: three endings documented it and one did not; cost: deciding the terms before launch.

**From finance and risk.** Vintages: every published data file kept and addressable by date; cost: storage and a naming rule. Revision and correction as two words, with corrections labelled as such; the typical revision's size printed once there are enough releases to compute it. A release calendar at least a quarter ahead, same-moment release, and a published list of anyone who sees a figure early; cost: discipline. A printed band rule on any uncertainty display, with the calibration history and exclusions, dated. The systemic-risk board's two sentences and a date — what the display is not, that no mechanical inference may be drawn, and the data cut-off on the face — which the other regulator displays read print in part. Status marks in square brackets — [p], [r], [f] — and not symbols. Uncertainty as a range beside a point, never a verbal hedge alone; evidence: n = 5,780 and two waves of over 3,000 and 4,000. A randomised reading test as the evaluation design, with comprehension, expectation and perception measured separately; evidence: two Bank studies.

**From economic development and place.** A per-indicator metadata record of the ONS's eleven items; a refresh target with the lag admitted as a limitation; intervals shown where the source publishes them and said to be absent where it does not. A fully specified similar-areas method (standardise, reduce, cluster with a chosen range, list the nearest by distance) with the snapshot caveat; condition: enough comparable areas and published code. A question-and-answer text for any ordering of places, each answer a "can" or a "cannot" with a worked example in numbers, including the not-half-as sentence and the not-a-time-series sentence with the reason. A base-year index with frozen standardisation and a printed count of indicators whose change could not be measured; condition: a stable indicator set, and a status notice when it stalls. A printed refusal to aggregate, with the reason. A direction-of-travel label with its rule printed where the label is — a significance test where intervals exist, and, where they do not, either no label or a declared judgement that is named as one — measured from a fixed baseline, with the two declared non-states, "to be confirmed" and "in development". An evidence scale with a declared floor for any "what difference did it make" claim, with counts at each level. The model sentence for non-significant change. A release calendar, because users asked for one.

**From culture and charity.** A pilot evaluated independently before roll-out, with N, n and response rate printed. A dotted line on the chart at a break, with a sentence saying whether estimates remain comparable. A data-processor statement saying who owns the data and who does not; reason: the dual role Selwood names. ## 10 · The failures, read for the sectors

**"It fails if it looks the same every month."** The sectors answer this with the arrow and the revision, and both have rules: no arrow without the points and a test, no colour as a verdict without significance, and the revision's size printed so a reader knows how much a figure usually moves. What changes on the screen each month under those rules is the data, the date, the log and the revisions to earlier figures — which is what a reader of an official series sees — and not an arrow or a colour that the data cannot yet support; a display that cannot yet draw an arrow says so and says when it can. The Health Index is the sector's warning: a display built to show change that stopped, with no notice found.

**"It fails if it's just a fancy spreadsheet with some charts that tell you lots of big numbers, but not what actually happens."** Ivanković's panel put "link time trends to policy decisions" among the seven features of an actionable dashboard and found purpose and audience both stated by one dashboard in eight; the GSS asks for "the direction of change, its absolute size and its relative size over time" in words; the OECD prints the reason it will not aggregate. The sectors' answer to "what actually happens" is a written sentence about the change, with the rule that licensed it printed beside it.

**"It fails if a viewer reads it and asks 'what am I looking at'."** The five-heading metric page and the "Simple Name" and "Simple Definition" fields beside the technical ones are the sectors' answer; so is the compliance page that says what the display is not.

**"For nightlife, it fails if they do not recognise their place within the front-end interface."** Chapter 12's, and this chapter adds only one part of the answer: once a place is found, the sectors have two documented ways of showing it beside places like it without ranking them — the ONS's specified similar-areas method and the OECD's nearest regions — and the second's rule that peers come from other countries is a catch for a local display, noted in section 5.

**"It fails if people don't use it."** Reach is counted in units that do not convert; two evaluations asked their readers directly, one of a public dashboard (76.4 per cent said it influenced their planning, among those still using it a year on) and one of an internal one (49 per cent said the data informed their decisions, of a 5 per cent response). The house can ask the same question and should expect an answer of the same shape.

**"It fails if it breaks or isn't reliable."** The service record (Chapter 8) now has the sectors' case law: a release calendar with Confirmed/Provisional; a fixed release time; a log where surprises are announced; two ledgers for error; a retirement plan, which the statistics regulator now lists among the minimum for an official dashboard and asks to be in place "from the start".

**"It fails if people don't trust it."** Trust is measured for producers and almost never for displays; where it is measured for a display the percentage is self-selected and the randomised test found no average effect. The sectors' lesson is to measure it the way the producers do — a fixed question, in waves, of those with a view — and to stop reporting on-site percentages as trust. And the gap between trust in the statistics office and agreement that government presents its figures honestly says where the public's doubt sits: with the presenter rather than the producer, which is the doubt the records beneath the dashboard exist to answer.

**"It fails if it's an aggregation of 'the biggest'."** Three place instruments have written a refusal of the single number or the single rank: "we do not make a single statement about the overall well-being in a region"; "No single summary measure is the 'best' measure"; ten unequal groups "based on similarity and meaningful gaps in the data" where ranks used to be.

## 11 · What holds true regardless

Seven things this chapter can state for any public dashboard, each with its kind and its grounds.

**1. Trust in a display is almost never measured, and where format was tested it moved comprehension more than trust.** In the literature read, trust is measured in series for producers, by a fixed question in waves, of those with a view; the central bank's layered report moved comprehension in two experiments and moved trust only a little, for one wording, in one; the one randomised test of a dashboard found no average effect on trust in government and a split by prior trust (secondary); the most-used dashboard's trust figure is a self-selected percentage with no n. So the house measures trust the way the producers measure theirs — a fixed question, repeated, of those with a view, with the base stated — or does not claim to have measured it, and never reports an on-site percentage as trust. *Evidence; Fixed (requirement).* Grounds: PCOS 2021 and 2023; the Charity Commission's tracker; Haldane & McMahon 2018; Bholat et al. 2018; Crepaz & Arikan 2021 (secondary); the UKHSA blog's 73.4 and 90.3 per cent.

**2. Provenance is a record per metric and a dated log apart from the refresh, with the house's own errors kept separately from its sources' revisions.** The record carries at least definition, rationale, method, caveats, source, frequency and date, and, where the metric has them, its numerator, its denominator and its polarity; the log labels its entries (new metric, change to metric, data issue, resolved, and so on) and pairs issue with resolved; a revision is a scheduled change and a correction is labelled as a correction. *Practice; Standard (regulator); Fixed (requirement).* Grounds: the UKHSA metric page and log; the Fingertips 32-field record; the JHU README; the ONS revisions policy; the ELS metadata list; OWID's field rules; OSR's minimum list.

**3. No arrow without a printed rule, no colour given as a judgement without significance, and the one-in-twenty sentence beside any table coloured by significance.** The rule for an arrow names its independent points, its test and its robustness check; where the data cannot meet it the display shows no arrow and says why; the rule is printed where the arrow is, dated, and kept when pages move; the sentence beside a coloured table says that about one coloured cell in twenty will be coloured by chance. Colour that encodes a value on a map or a scale is Chapter 3's matter, not this rule's. *Standard (official statistics); Fixed (requirement).* Grounds: Fingertips' RAG and trend-marker guidance; the OECD's ±5 per cent sentence; Wales's significance-or-expert rule; Scotland's lost thresholds; the GSS's model sentence; and, for the choice of axis beneath any arrow, Padilla et al. 2022 on the cumulative axis.

**4. A series whose records arrive after the event is drawn by the event's date, with the incomplete recent period marked and the direction of its bias stated; and every published version of a figure is kept, dated, so that its later movement can be seen and, once there are enough releases, its typical size printed.** *Practice; Evidence; Fixed (requirement).* Grounds: PHE 2020 and UKHSA's documentation and log; ALFRED; Croushore & Stark 2001; the ONS's 0.08 and 0.27 percentage points; the Fed heat map's cost.

**5. Uncertainty is shown as a range beside a point; a verbal hedge alone did little for understanding and was the form in which any loss of trust appeared.** *Evidence; Fixed (requirement).* Grounds: van der Bles et al. 2020 (n = 5,780; "mostly for verbal uncertainty communication"); Galvão & Mitchell 2021 (two waves; "does not reduce its trust in the statistical office"; the textual qualifier "tends to have little effect", read once); OSR 2022 on dashboards as the weak point.

**6. A rank implies a distance that the data do not measure, and the place instruments read say so in print or refuse to make one.** Any ordering of places ships with a question-and-answer text saying what it can and cannot be used to say — that a lower rank is not "half as" anything, and that editions are snapshots rather than a time series — with the reason; where a composite is made its rank carries an interval; where it is not, the refusal and its reason are printed. *Standard (official statistics); Fixed (requirement)* (the house's; Chapters 5 and 12 hold it). Grounds: the Indices of Deprivation 2025 FAQ and research report; the OECD user guide; County Health Rankings 2024; Arndt 2015; the JRC audit; Cox 2008 (†).

**7. Public data projects end or change hands — four of the five read here have — and the plan for it is written before launch.** Notice with a date; a reason; a frozen archive under a named licence; stable URLs; a named successor or a stated "none"; a gap analysis; and, when a source dies, the whole series replaced from the new source with the last old-source version archived, rather than two sources joined in one line. *Practice; Standard (regulator); Fixed (sequence).* Grounds: JHU 2023; OWID 2023 and 2024; UKHSA 2023; the COVID Tracking Project 2021; OSR's retirement clause; the Audience Agency 2026 as the negative case.

Three things the chapter will not state as truths. That any sector's display is a model for the Vibe Map (each is read for mechanisms, under conditions the studio does not share). That reach can be measured without asking (every count read is a server event or a self-report, and the two evaluations that asked had a 5 per cent response and a sample of survivors). And that the culture sector's own instruments are the house's standard (the pilot evaluation found the data "interesting" rather than "useful", the separate platform's requirement was suspended, and the custodian failed).

### Beneath the dashboard: the precedent record

The *precedent record* holds, for every mechanism the build considers borrowing from another sector — adopted, adapted or declined — where it came from and on what terms, so that a reviewer can tell a borrowed rule from a habit and a habit from a bet, in section 1's senses. | Line | What it holds | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | What is borrowed, in one sentence, and the record it feeds (recipe card, service record, comparison record, face sheet, boundary log, …) | A borrowed rule lives in the record it serves, not in this one |

| Evidence | What was measured about it, by whom, on whom, with n; or "none found" | With a reason but no evidence or named cost, the entry is a bet, and says so |

A filled example, invented, for method only. Mechanism: the marked incomplete-tail band for a counted series with late returns, feeding the face sheet (the band and its sentence) and the recipe card (how the window is set). Source: UKHSA data dashboard, change-log entry "Updated measles chart", 8 August 2024, read 6 October 2026 (the cases metric's documentation page, last updated 12 February 2025, states the same device's window in words for a different metric). Status: awaiting a condition — the lag distribution of the house's own series. Review: the source entry re-read in twelve months, since the dashboard logs changes to the device; owner, the data lead. The example decides nothing about the Vibe Map's data; it shows what an entry looks like when the rule is borrowed and the condition is not yet met.

## 12 · Where the choice is yours

**Which mechanisms to adopt.** Section 9 is an offer with terms rather than a list of requirements; the seven truths name the few that hold regardless. Everything else — which metadata fields beyond the minimum; whether to publish a compliance-style statement; whether to run a trust question at all, and in whose waves; which evidence scale to label contribution claims with — is the studio's, with the precedent record as the place the choice is written. The seven truths are the exceptions, and the rules table marks which procedures serve them.

**Which conditions to wait for.** Several mechanisms cannot be run on a new display: a trend arrow (five annual points); a printed revision size (enough releases); a similar-areas model (enough comparable areas); a base-year index (a stable indicator set). The choice is whether to show nothing and say why, or to show a weaker device and label it as weaker; the sectors do both and the GSS gives the sentence for the first. What the display shows in its first years, under these rules, is therefore the data, its dates, its log and its revisions, with the sentences about change written by hand — which is the state most official series are in for their first five years.

**Whether to measure reach by asking.** The one question that measures use has a 5 per cent response rate in the only evaluation that printed one. Whether to ask it, of whom, and how often, is the studio's; what the chapter holds is that server counts are not reach and are not reported as reach.

**What the ending says.** Truth 7 holds that the plan exists before launch and names its parts; the studio decides their content — what the Vibe Map's archive, licence, successor, notice period and gap analysis would be.

**What to call the sector's instruments.** The Toolkit, Illuminate, the segmentation tool and the national survey are the tools the studio will meet in funding conversations. How to use any of them is the studio's decision, with their documented state — live, suspended, orphaned, replaced with a dotted line — in front of it.

## 13 · A nightlife illustration — method only

The method only. Suppose the studio has a counted series about a place's activity in which records arrive after the event — a count that is complete only some weeks after the week it describes — and it is deciding how the most recent weeks are drawn. The sectors have answered the question, and the walk through the precedent record is the same for any such series.

*First, the property.* This is change over time in both senses: how the display shows that the quantity moved, and how the display's own recent figures will move as late records arrive.

*Second, the mechanisms on offer.* Public health's four-part answer (event date; a stated window; a marked band with the direction of the bias; the by-publication series kept and labelled). Finance's vintages (keep every weekly file, dated, so a reader can see what was known when) and its printed revision size (once there are enough weeks, state the mean and mean absolute revision between the first and the settled figure). Fingertips' arrow rule (no arrow on the recent weeks at all until the series has the independent points and the test to support one; a neutral marker meanwhile, with the reason). The GSS's model sentence for a movement inside natural variation.

*Third, the entries.* One per mechanism, nine lines each, as in section 11. For the band: the rule as printed, the reason, "evidence: none found on readers", the condition (the house's own lag distribution rather than the source's window), what the house changes (window, colour, sentence, each with its reason), status (awaiting the lag data), review (the source re-read in twelve months). For the vintages: the rule (every release kept and addressable by date), the reason (results change when figures do), the evidence (Croushore & Stark), the cost (storage and a naming rule), status (adopted from the first release, because it cannot be adopted later). For the arrow: the rule (five independent points, the test, leave-one-out), the condition (five independent annual points — or, for a weekly series, the house's own analogue, stated), status (awaiting the condition; no arrow meanwhile, with the sentence that says why). For the revision size: status (awaiting enough releases).

*Fourth, the face sheet and the recipe card.* The band's window and sentence go on the face (Chapter 11); the window's derivation, the vintage rule and the arrow rule go in the recipe card (Chapter 5); the retirement of the by-publication series, if it is ever retired, goes in the service record (Chapter 8) and the change log.

*Fifth, the test.* A reader shown the most recent weeks is asked what the data say about the latest week; the pass is that the reader says the latest figure is incomplete and will rise, in the direction the band's sentence states, and does not read a fall. The sectors did not run this test; the house can.

Nothing above names the series, the place, the window's length, the colour or the words. It is the procedure by which the studio decides them with the sectors' documented rules in front of it.

## What proves a break works

A break here is a departure from one of this chapter's rules, or a borrowing from a sector on terms the chapter did not set. Take any rule the build uses for provenance, change over time, reach or an ending, and find its entry in the precedent record. If the entry's nine lines are filled — the rule as printed from a dated source, the reason, the evidence or "none found", the condition, the house's changes with their reasons, the status and the review — the rule was borrowed and the house owns its departures. If the entry is missing, the rule is unexamined; if its reason line is empty, it is a habit; if its evidence and cost lines are empty, it is a bet. Then the reader test from section 13 for any change-over-time device: a reader shown the most recent period says what the data say about it, and the pass is that the reader reports the direction the device states and does not read an artefact as a movement.

## Words this chapter uses

- The *four properties* are the brief's: *trust*, which the chapter treats as measured only where someone asked people a question about it, and distinguishes between trust in a *producer* (a statistics office, a charity) and trust in a *display*; *provenance*, the record of where a figure came from and what was done to it; *change over time*, which here means both how a display shows that a quantity moved and how a display's own figures move after first publication; and *reach*, who read it and whether it informed anything, which the chapter counts in the unit each source counts in and names the unit.
- A *mechanism* is a documented, repeatable rule or practice — a banding rule, a change log, a release calendar, a vintage archive, an evaluation design — as distinct from a design or a look. A mechanism is *borrowable* when its rule is printed, its reason is stated, and its evidence or its cost is known; the chapter's borrowing test is in section 1.
- A *vintage* is the version of a dataset as it stood on a given date; a *revision* is a scheduled change to a published figure and a *correction* an unplanned one, which is the ONS's distinction and the chapter keeps it.
- A *banding rule* is the rule by which a value is coloured or labelled against a comparator; a *direction-of-travel label* is the arrow or word that says a series rose, fell or held.
- A *custodian* is the body that keeps a dataset comparable, available and citable over time; a *sunset* is a dataset's or display's planned ending.
- The *precedent record* holds, for every mechanism the build considers borrowing from another sector — adopted, adapted or declined — where it came from and on what terms.
