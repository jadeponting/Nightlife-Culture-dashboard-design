## Before you start

This is the third chapter of a research book written for Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio led by Carly Heath, about how dashboards work and why. The book exists to support the Vibe Map — a planned public, non-commercial, interactive account of the people, activity, value and support systems that sustain a place's nightlife culture, built first for the West of England. You do not need to have read Chapters 1 and 2 to read this one. Two things from them recur: the working definition — *a dashboard is a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader* — and Chapter 2's finding that there is no reader in general. Where this chapter refers to Carly, it means the studio's founder; "the brief" is her written brief for this book; where it refers to the Vibe Map, it is illustrating, not specifying. The studio's own rulebook for design decisions is called the Decision Book, and its visual rules live in a design system (Style Guide v1.5); this chapter tests the Decision Book's chapter on charts against the evidence. The three user groups the Vibe Map will be tested with are policymakers and politicians, advocates and trade bodies, and funders and investors, with the people who make the night as co-developers.

Two conventions, explained fully in Chapter 0. Every rule in this book carries a **kind** — *Standard* (an external rule nobody here wrote), *Evidence* (a named study), *Reading* (our interpretation, open to challenge), *Practice* (a practitioner's framework or craft knowledge, not tested as research) or *Nightlife Culture* (a decision or finding of the studio's own) — and a **tier**: *Fixed* is a hard line with a right answer; *Adaptable* is a stated default you may argue with and must log if you leave; *Decorative* is free. Flags in square brackets mark loose ends: `[Further research]`, `[Prototype flag]` (something the Vibe Map can settle with its own data), `[Validate with users]`, `[Revisit → Ch n]`, `[Revisit at end]`, `[Rich reference]` and `[Counterbalance → Ch n]`. A dagger (†) after a source means it was read at the authors' published abstract only.

Two statistical conventions, since this chapter reports experiments. *d* is the difference between two groups measured in standard deviations, where 0.2 is conventionally small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. *n* is how many people took part. Where a finding comes from a few dozen students in one laboratory, the chapter says so; where it comes from thousands of people across countries, it says that too, and the difference matters more than the headline.

## The question

**Of everything the field says about how to show data, what has actually been tested, what survived, and which of the survivors are hard lines?**

---

## The 60-second version

> The field that shows data runs on rules, and most of them were never tested. This chapter went looking for the ones that were. The short list that survives is shorter than the textbooks and more useful.
>
> The eye reads *position* most accurately, then length, then angle, and area and colour worst — a 1984 finding replicated three times since. But its authors called the bottom half "partly conjectural"; pie charts turn out not to be read by angle at all; and the gaps between chart types are smaller than the gaps between people. The hierarchy settles which form to use for a precise comparison and nothing about memory, understanding or trust. The eye pulls a statistic from a whole chart in a glance and makes one comparison at a time, slowly; everything in design that works, works by choosing the comparison for the reader.
>
> Words carry the message. People look at the title first and longest, remember it when they say they do not, and let it decide what the chart "showed" — even when it contradicts the picture. Readers want more text on a chart than designers give them, and the trend belongs in the title. Nobody has run the obvious experiment on legends against direct labels; the practice stands on a meta-analysis from instructional design and on how often the eye goes back to the key. In the only large eye-tracking study of real dashboards, the small words beside each tile drew more attention than the big words above everything, and a plain number was the most-looked-at object on the screen.
>
> The first impression forms in fifty milliseconds and is about *appeal*, not understanding; what drives it is simplicity and looking like what you claim to be. Ornament that does not touch the data mark is more memorable and not less accurate; the study that started the argument rested on ten people; and a later experiment found that removing clutter makes a chart look professional while adding a message headline and one highlight is what gets the point remembered. A truncated bar axis exaggerates even when it is labelled and even after people have been taught about it. On a log scale, four readers in ten answered a basic question correctly; on a linear scale, eight in ten. The famous "85% ignore interactives" is one newsroom's click rate on one button; on real public pages, the median use of an optional control is zero, and mobile readers touch less and leave sooner. A default view shifts choices toward it (d = 0.68 across 58 datasets), partly because it is read as the designer's recommendation, and it anchors where people look. "Overview first" is a reminder its author wrote to himself, cited 52 times and validated never; people like overviews reliably and are helped by them unreliably. Animation is enjoyed, slower and less accurate for analysis, and helps in exactly one place — the transition between two states of the same chart.
>
> Uncertainty can be shown in forms people read correctly, and the forms differ: error bars create a region of false certainty; countable dots and animated draws do better; a hard boundary is read as a category; the people who draw error bars misread them themselves. "Likely" means anything until a number sits beside it. A fifth of adults in England struggle with multi-step arithmetic; in Germany and the United States a third of the public has low graph literacy and low numeracy together; and the UK's statistics regulator could find no survey that measures the public's ability to read charts and tables. Of the accessibility standards, five success criteria are the hard lines that bite on charts; plain language is not one of them; and a non-public body is bound by the Equality Act's anticipatory duty with WCAG 2.2 AA as the yardstick.
>
> So the canonical truths are these: choose the comparison, not the chart; put the message in words at the top and labels at the mark; never let colour carry meaning alone; start bars at zero and say so when a line does not; show uncertainty as something countable; assume a glance, a phone and a sceptic; and treat every default as advice you are giving.

## 1 · What showing data is

A chart is a translation. A number becomes a length, a position, an angle, an area, a shade; a reader's eye turns it back into a judgement — this is bigger than that, this went up, this is an outlier — without passing through the number at all. Everything useful that has been learned about showing data is about where that round trip is accurate, where it is fast, and where it quietly fails.

The best current map of that territory is a 2021 review by Steven Franconeri, Lace Padilla, Priti Shah, Jeffrey Zacks and Jessica Hullman, written for a general readership in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*. Its abstract is a fair summary of this whole chapter: "Effectively designed data visualizations allow viewers to use their powerful visual systems to understand patterns in data across science, education, health, and public policy. But ineffectively designed visualizations can cause confusion, misunderstanding, or even distrust—especially among viewers with low graphical literacy. … We describe how the visual system can quickly extract broad statistics from a display, whereas poorly designed displays can lead to misperceptions and illusions. Extracting global statistics is fast, but comparing between subsets of values is slow. Effective graphics avoid taxing working memory, guide attention, and respect familiar conventions." `[Rich reference]`

The review organises the evidence under three headings, and this chapter leans on them throughout, though it runs by topic. A display can be *perceptually accurate*: the eye recovers the right magnitudes. It can be *perceptually efficient*: the eye finds the right comparison quickly. And it can be *understandable*: the reader has the schema to read it and the working memory to hold it. Each is a different kind of failure, and a chart can pass one and fail the others. A bar chart with a truncated axis is perceptually efficient and misleading; a rainbow map is attention-grabbing and inaccurate; a parallel-coordinates plot is accurate and, to most of the public, unreadable.

Why bother with a picture at all? Because of what the eye does in parallel. "Visualizations allow powerful processing of an entire two-dimensional rectangle of information at once, in stark contrast to the limitation of reading handfuls of symbolic numbers per second." The review's first figure is Anscombe's old demonstration: "Four sets of 11 numbers have identical statistics but dramatically different patterns, as revealed by the scatterplots below each column." That is the case for charts. The rest of the chapter is about how often the case is undermined by the way they are drawn.

## 2 · Why people believe what they believe

The folklore of showing data is unusually confident, and most of it has a named source. Each belief is stated fairly here and tested in the sections that follow.

### Belief one: there is a hierarchy of encodings, and it decides the chart

Most data-visualisation courses teach a ranking: position is read best, then length, then angle, then area, then colour. From it follow the rules — bar charts good, pie charts suspect, 3D forbidden, choropleths dangerous. The Decision Book, the studio's own rulebook, opens its chart chapter this way: "Almost every chart rule you have ever been given is downstream of one finding." The belief is that the ranking is a law and the rules are its corollaries.

### Belief two: less ink is better

Edward Tufte's doctrine — maximise the share of ink that carries data; strip "chartjunk" — is the most influential aesthetic in the field and the house style of the statistics offices. The belief is that ornament costs accuracy and that a clean chart is an honest one.

### Belief three: a picture speaks for itself

Charts are made by people who think in charts, and the belief follows that the picture carries the message and the words are furniture: a title to say what the data is, axis labels, a legend. A corollary is "label at the mark, not in a legend", a rule nearly everyone in the field repeats.

### Belief four: you have fifty milliseconds

Designers are told that users judge a page in a twentieth of a second and that eyes read a screen in an F-shape, so the top-left corner is where the message must go. The belief is that the first glance decides comprehension.

### Belief five: overview first, and let them explore

Ben Shneiderman's mantra — "Overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand" — is the most-cited design rule in interactive visualisation, and the belief that follows is that an interactive display should open on everything and let the reader drill in, and that interactivity is itself a good.

### Belief six: never truncate; always start at zero

A rule inherited from statistics offices and style guides, applied to every chart alike, and believed to be about honesty.

### Belief seven: uncertainty confuses people, and animation shows change

Two beliefs about what to leave out and what to add. The first says that readers cannot cope with ranges and that a clean point estimate is kinder. The second says that if something changes over time, the natural way to show it is to make it move.

Each belief has a true core. The sections below say how much.

## 3 · What the eye can read

### The hierarchy, and what it actually says

In 1984 William Cleveland and Robert McGill, statisticians at Bell Labs, set out to give graphics "a scientific foundation" by studying "graphical perception—the visual decoding of information encoded on graphs". They proposed what they were careful to call a hypothesis: "In this section we hypothesize an ordering of the 10 elementary perceptual tasks on the basis of the accuracy with which people can extract quantitative information by using them." From most to least accurate: position along a common scale; positions along non-aligned scales; length, direction and angle; area; volume and curvature; shading and colour saturation. The principle that followed: "A graphical form that involves elementary perceptual tasks that lead to more accurate judgments than another graphical form (with the same quantitative information) will result in better organization and increase the chances of a correct perception of patterns and behavior."

Then they tested it — the top of it. In one experiment 51 usable volunteers judged what percentage one marked value was of another on five kinds of bar chart; in another, 51 compared pie charts with bar charts. The results were clear: "average errors for length judgments are 40%-250% larger than those for position judgments"; position judgements were more accurate than angle judgements by a factor of 1.96; "Seventy-eight percent of the large errors occurred for the length judgments." From this they recommended "radical surgery on these popular graphs": replace pie charts with dot charts, divided bars with grouped dots, shaded maps with framed rectangles. And they said what the theory was not: it "does not provide a complete prescription for how to make a graph", should be applied "with judgment", and if accuracy alone were the goal, "We agree with Ehrenberg (1975) that if this were the only goal, tables would be better."

A year later, restating the work in *Science*, they were plainer still about the bottom of the ladder: "The ordering should be thought of as a tentative working hypothesis, based on current information, that can be expected to evolve." "Aspects of the ordering are partly conjectural in that we have no controlled experimentation to support them." The famous diagram is half experiment and half hypothesis, and its authors said so.

The top held. In 2010 Jeffrey Heer and Michael Bostock reran the experiments on 186 people recruited online, and the results preserved "the rough shape and ranking of judgment types by accuracy"; "The ranking of types by accuracy is consistent between the two experiments." They extended it to area — rectangles and circles were about equally bad, and, "Somewhat surprisingly, comparisons of rectangles with aspect ratio 1 exhibited the worst performance" — and added two practical numbers: gridlines at least eight pixels apart, and little benefit from chart heights beyond 80 pixels on a 0–100 scale. In 2014 Justin Talbot, Vidya Setlur and Anushka Anand took bar charts apart to find out *why* the types differed: separating two bars added about 0.70 percentage points of error; bars that did not share a baseline, as in a stacked chart, added 1.16; and comparisons within divided bars carried an absolute error of 7.38 percentage points, for which the authors offered "One possible explanation … that subjects are influenced by the part-to-whole comparison." Their conclusion: "even for simple visualizations, such as bar charts, we still do not have a complete understanding of what impacts chart perception."

Two later findings change how the hierarchy should be used. Robert Kosara and Drew Skau showed in three studies that pie charts are not read by angle. Distort a pie so that its angles change but its areas do not, and people read it as well as ever; isolate the angle alone and they do worst of all: "angle cannot be the only way we read a pie or donut chart"; "Area emerges as the most likely cue used to read pie charts." Donuts were not worse than pies. The rule "pies are bad because angle is low on the hierarchy" is a true conclusion with a false reason. And in 2022 Russell Davis and colleagues reran Cleveland and McGill with 109 people and 130,800 judgements, modelling each person separately. The population ranking survived, but "some people show patterns of accuracy that credibly deviate from the canonical rankings": the canonical order held for about a fifth of participants, "around 20-25% of people consistently perform better with another chart type" than bars, and the "expected differences in performance across pie, bubble, and stacked-bar charts (1-1.5pp) is smaller than the expected differences between people (1.5-3pp)." The authors argue against treating the ranking as a design rule.

So here is what the hierarchy settles, stated plainly. For a reader estimating the ratio between two values, position on a common scale beats length, which beats angle; that is replicated and real, and the effect is a few percentage points of error. The hierarchy does not settle anything about what is remembered, understood, trusted or liked; it was never tested below its third rank; it does not say how pies are read; and the gaps between the lower chart types are smaller than the gaps between readers. The Decision Book's first rule — "Use the highest encoding on the perceptual ranking that carries the comparison" — survives as a default for precise comparison. Its gloss that 3D is "banned because volume is fifth" and pies constrained "because angle and area sit two and three ranks down" rests on the conjectural half, and section 8 returns to both.

### What is seen before you look

Some visual differences are found without searching. Christopher Healey and James Enns, reviewing the perception literature for visualisation, give the working definition: "Typically, tasks that can be performed on large multi-element displays in less than 200-250 milliseconds (msec) are considered preattentive." Hue, intensity, orientation, size, curvature, motion and flicker all pop out; a red dot among blue ones is found at once however many blue ones there are. The limit is as useful as the list: "When a target is defined by the joint presence of two or more visual properties, it often cannot be found preattentively." A red circle among red squares and blue circles has to be searched for. Anne Treisman's 1980 theory, from which this descends, put it as: attention "must be directed serially to each stimulus in a display whenever conjunctions of more than one separable feature are needed", while single features can be "detected in parallel with no attention limits."

Two design consequences follow and both are evidence, not taste. "Feature hierarchies suggest that the most important data attributes should be displayed with the most salient visual features, to avoid situations where secondary data values mask the information the viewer wants to see." And the features interfere asymmetrically: "random variations in shape have no effect on a viewer's ability to see colour patterns" but colour variation masks shape — which is why a chart that uses colour for decoration makes its shapes harder to read.

Grouping is the other thing the eye does unasked. A century of Gestalt research, reviewed by Johan Wagemans and colleagues, lists the principles — "proximity, similarity, common fate, good continuation, closure, symmetry, parallelism" and newer ones such as "common region" ("elements that lie within the same bounded area") and "element connectedness" ("distinct elements that share a common border"). Franconeri's review makes the bridge to charts in one sentence: "The same classic grouping cues studied in the perception literature can control which values are selected and compared", through "connecting lines, position proximity, and similarity in either color or shape". A dashboard's tiles, borders and gaps are grouping instructions whether or not anyone wrote them deliberately.

### Statistics are fast; comparisons are slow

This is the review's central mechanism and the one most worth a first-time reader's attention. "Faster than an eyeblink, we can pull statistics from data encoded as positions, one-dimensional lengths, two-dimensional areas, angles, or intensities" — the average, the spread, the outlier, the trend of a whole field of marks, at once. But "Comparisons include local comparisons between elements, groups of elements, or global trends", and "Each of those verbal sentences describes a single visual comparison that must be extracted serially, as part of a sequence"; "comparisons are already highly capacity limited". A reader can see that a scatterplot slopes upward instantly, and must then work, one pair at a time, to see which two points to compare.

Everything in chart design that works, works by choosing that comparison for the reader. "A visualization designed to guide viewers to make the 'right' visual comparisons can lead those viewers to make meaningful insights." "Visualization designers will often deliberately control visual salience to bring the viewer's eye straight to the critical comparison." "One technique is to add salient color highlighting a single group of items to ensure that viewers process that comparison first." Section 7 reports the experiment that measured this.

Three illusions travel with the mechanism and are worth knowing by name; all three are from the review. The *line-graph illusion*: two curves of identical shape, one shifted upward, look as if they converge where they steepen — "it is difficult to see that the vertical distance between the two lines is the same across their entire horizontal span", so a chart of two series invites a false reading of their difference; the remedy Cleveland and McGill proposed in 1984, in the same paper as the hierarchy, is to plot the difference itself. *Which channel is read*: a pictogram scaled by height to a 1:2 ratio is read by area, "leading to something closer to a 1:4 ratio". And *categorical perception* of colour: the visual system "systematically bins continuous wavelengths into one of several perceptual categories, exaggerating metric differences", which is the mechanism behind section 4.

### Working memory and the schema

The third heading is about what the reader brings. Reading a legend is a working-memory task: "Interpreting the graphs … requires individuals to map the symbols and colors in the graphs to their referents in the legends below. This task is highly demanding of limited working memory resources." The review cites one study in which "people answered questions about data both faster and more accurately when the data were directly labeled in graphs compared with when there was a legend", and its advice is unqualified: "instead of legends, use direct labels whenever possible". Section 5 says how thin that single study makes the evidence, and why the rule stands anyway.

Reading any chart at all requires a *schema*: "a knowledge structure that includes default expectations, rules, and associations." "Reading a bar graph requires a schema that allows the viewer to do what you do when viewing a bar graph: to focus instantly on the positions of the bar tops, because they signal the numbers depicted by the bars." Conventions are part of the schema — "Larger values tend to be mapped to higher vertical positions" — and "When these mappings are violated, viewers can become confused." The sentence in the review that a public-facing designer should pin up is this one, which rests on a Pew survey rather than an experiment: "Even relatively common visualizations such as scatterplots are misunderstood by 37% of adults in the United States." Expert communities "have developed idiosyncratic visualizations that serve important data-analysis or -communication purposes, but they require learning new graph schemas." Section 11 returns to what the public can read; the point here is that familiarity is a perceptual resource, not a failure of ambition.

## 4 · Colour

Colour is the channel designers reach for first and the eye reads worst, and the evidence sorts into three things: what colour can carry, what it cannot, and the one rule about it that is a hard line.

### Three kinds of scheme

Cynthia Brewer's ColorBrewer, built for maps and used everywhere since, distinguishes three jobs. *Sequential* schemes are for "ordered data that progress from low to high. Lightness steps dominate the look of these schemes, with light colors for low data values to dark colors for high data values." *Diverging* schemes "put equal emphasis on mid-range critical values and extremes at both ends of the data range", with a light middle and dark, contrasting-hue ends. *Qualitative* schemes "do not imply magnitude differences between legend classes, and hues are used to create the primary visual differences between classes." This is design logic rather than experiment, but it encodes what the perception work below shows: the eye reads *lightness* as order and *hue* as category, and a scheme that uses hue for order asks the reader to learn an arbitrary sequence.

### The rainbow

The rainbow colour map — blue through green and yellow to red — is the standard case. Kenneth Moreland's summary of the objections: "The rainbow colors do not follow any natural perceived ordering. Although the order of the hues can be learned, there is no innate sense of higher or lower." "The perceptual changes in the rainbow colors are not uniform. The colors appear to change much faster in the yellow region than the green region." And "It is sensitive to deficiencies in vision". Franconeri's review supplies the mechanism — the seven bands of a rainbow "are not present in the rainbow itself"; the eye bins a continuous scale into categories and "These additional hues create new color-category boundaries that can dramatically exaggerate the differences". Yang Liu and Jeffrey Heer tested nine colour maps on about 166 people making distance judgements and found that the rainbow "performs the worst among all colormaps considered", that the modern lightness-ordered maps (viridis and its family) did best, and that "a combination of perceptual color space and color naming measures more accurately predict user performance than either alone."

The complication, which a careful chapter has to state: Khairi Reda and colleagues, testing 381 people on continuous maps, found that "rainbow schemes afforded the highest accuracy in quantity estimation irrespective of spatial complexity", while "divergent colormaps significantly outperformed other schemes in tasks requiring the perception of high-frequency patterns." More hues help a reader *name a value* at a point because colour names are a lookup aid; lightness-ordered maps help a reader *see a pattern*. The rule against the rainbow is a rule about pattern maps and about order; it is not a universal.

### Who cannot see it, and how many colours there are

Jennifer Birch's review of population surveys gives the prevalence of red–green colour deficiency: "about 8% in men and about 0.4% in women" of European descent, and "between 4% and 6.5% in men of Chinese and Japanese ethnicity".† Franconeri's review gives a global all-population figure of about 4%; the two are different denominators, not a disagreement, and this book uses Birch's. The design consequence is the one sentence everyone agrees on: "The simplest way to make visualizations accessible to viewers with color blindness is to avoid using hue as the only encoding channel or allow viewers to change the color palette."

How many categories can colour carry? The only experimental number found is Healey's from 1996: "seven isoluminant colours is the maximum we can display at one time" for rapid, accurate search, when colour distance, linear separation and colour category are all controlled. The practitioner limits are tighter — the UK Government Analysis Function recommends "a limit of four categories as best practice for basic data visualisations"; the Office for National Statistics says "Always use five colours or fewer" and "Never use more than nine" — and the Decision Book's "four series, maximum" descends from them. Those are Practice and voluntary Standard, not Evidence, and they are sensible precisely because real palettes are not Healey's controlled seven. Two further findings refine them. Danielle Szafir showed on 461 people that "perceived color difference varies inversely with size": the smaller the mark, the bigger the colour difference it needs to be told apart, and lines and elongated marks are easier to tell apart than points of the same thickness — so a small legend dot or a scatter point needs bigger colour steps than a bar. And Sharon Lin and colleagues found that colours which *mean* something to the reader — blue for oceans, green for parks — let people answer questions about 10% faster than a default palette, a small effect with a large constituency.

### The hard line

One rule about colour is a Standard, and it is worth quoting in full. WCAG 2.2, Success Criterion 1.4.1, *Use of Color*, Level A: "Color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information, indicating an action, prompting a response, or distinguishing a visual element." Level A is the floor of conformance. A chart whose series are told apart by colour alone, with a legend, fails it — not as a matter of taste but as a measurable breach. Two companion criteria at Level AA set the contrast floors: 1.4.3 requires text at "a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1" (3:1 for large text), and 1.4.11, *Non-text Contrast*, requires "Parts of graphics required to understand the content" to have "a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against adjacent color(s)". The W3C's own explanatory text for 1.4.11 is the only place WCAG speaks about charts by name: for a line graph, "The lines should have 3:1 contrast against their background, but as there is little overlap with other lines they do not need to contrast with each other or the graduated lines." A careful reader will notice what that means: 1.4.11 does *not* require two data series to contrast with each other. The requirement that they be distinguishable comes from 1.4.1 — not by colour alone — which direct labelling satisfies and a legend does not. That is the logical root of the Decision Book's rule that "direct labelling is the mechanism by which the chart is compliant at all", and section 12 says who is bound by it.

One more word on maps, since belief one listed choropleths among the dangerous forms. A choropleth encodes a value in colour — the bottom of the hierarchy — and is a poor way to read one region's figure; it is the right form when the finding is a pattern across space, provided the colour scale is ordered by lightness and the values are also available as a table. Chapter 2 found that the choice between a map of counts and a map of rates moved readers' perception by nine points; that, not the colour, is the choropleth's most persuasive decision.

## 5 · Words

### People read the title first, longest, and instead

Michelle Borkin and colleagues tracked the eyes of 33 people looking at 393 visualisations and then asked them to describe what they had seen. "People spend the most amount of time looking at the text in a visualization, and more specifically, the title." Titles were fixated 59% of the time when present, 76% when at the top; the elements fixated longest during recognition were titles, followed by recognisable objects; the elements people kept returning to were "the legend, table header row … and title." When asked afterwards what a visualisation had shown, "46% of the time the participants described the contents, or rewording, of the title." Visualisations whose message was stated in words as well as pictures — what the authors call *message redundancy* — were described better (a quality score of 1.99 against 1.59), and 57% of the best-described charts had it. Their conclusion: "Titles and text are key elements in a visualization and help recall the message."

Laura Matzen and colleagues at Sandia reanalysed the same eye data and ran their own study, dividing each image into regions of interest ("ROIs"). In the memory task, "When the Title ROI was present in a visualization, it was one of the first three visited in 87.8% of the scan paths", and "59.9% … of the participants' fixations fell into ROIs containing text relative to 30.0% … in the data". In free viewing the data took over — "Fixations to the Data ROI surpassed looks to the Title beginning ~1500 ms after trial onset" — but text still drew about 40% of all looks. The first second and a half goes to the words.

For dashboards specifically there is one large study, by Yiming Yang and colleagues, the only one of its kind this chapter found. Sixty people viewed 36 dashboards each, from a pool of 1,216 real ones, for 45 seconds each. Text objects ("TOs") "account for 42.96% of the overall attention intensity whereas VOs [visual objects — charts] constitute 52.02%" — but text is small and charts are large, and by the study's measure of how much of each object's area drew attention, "The average saliency coverage for TOs is 47.21% much higher than 14.37% for VOs". Text draws attention far out of proportion to the space it takes. "Number gains the highest saliency coverage, serving as the primary focal point" — a plain figure is the most-looked-at object class on a dashboard. "Subtitles show significantly higher saliency coverage than titles" — the small label under each tile outdraws the heading above everything. And attention "is concentrated in the upper left corner", starting at "title and filter". The study is free viewing without a task and its sample is aged 18 to 32, and those are real limits; it is still the best picture anyone has of where eyes go on the thing this book is about.

### The title decides what the chart showed

Ha-Kyung Kong, Zhicheng Liu and Karrie Karahalios asked what a title does to a reading. In a nationally representative US sample, they put the same chart under titles slanted one way or the other. "The analysis of the perceived main messages revealed that 65% of the answers (N=136) reflected the material covered in the titles"; "the slant of visualization titles resulted in a significant difference in the perceived main message", with "viewers deriving opposing messages from the same visualization." The readers did not notice: "Similar ratios of participants answered that the information was neutral whether they saw an attitude-consistent (80%), attitude-inconsistent (80%), or attitude-irrelevant (89%) visualization." And they did not think they had read the title — "33% of the participants (N=34) stated that they did not remember any of the titles" — while writing its content down as the chart's message. The authors "highlight the danger of subtle statistics frames and viewers' unwarranted conviction of the neutrality of visualizations."

Their follow-up pushed the title further from the picture. Even when the title contradicted the chart, "the majority of the people (72-87%) in all conditions reported that the information was neutral"; "People's recall of the visualization's message more frequently aligned with the titles than the visualization". And the trust ordering is the finding to keep: "data was perceived as the most credible, followed by the visualization, then the title." Readers trust the title least and remember it most. A study by Dae Hyun Kim, Vidya Setlur and Maneesh Agrawala with 2,168 people adds the limit: a caption redirects a reader to a feature the chart already makes visually prominent, and when the caption "describes a low-prominence chart feature, readers rely on the chart and report a higher-prominence feature as the takeaway." Words win when they agree with what the picture makes salient; title and chart must point at the same thing.

### How much text, and where

The first controlled study of how much text readers want on a chart is Chase Stokes and colleagues' in 2023. 302 people ranked line charts carrying from no text to a full paragraph with no chart at all. "We find that heavily annotated charts were not penalized. In fact, participants preferred the charts with the largest number of textual annotations over charts with fewer annotations or text alone." The sparsest charts ranked lowest — "participants least preferred the no-text variant … and the title-only variant" — so a title alone is not enough. "a sizable minority — 42, or 14% of participants — ranked the all-text option as their first choice". And placement follows meaning: a statement of the overall trend was "most likely to be matched by participant takeaways when … positioned as the title", while statistics and context worked better placed at the data points. Their guidelines, which transfer directly to a dashboard tile: annotate with several text elements rather than minimally; put the trend in the title; put the numbers and the context next to the marks; and consider a text-only alternative.

The ONS's practice is consistent with this and often cited for it. Since 2019 its chart titles "sum up the main message shown in the chart in 10 words or less", with a subtitle for the data, geography and period; its service manual now says "The chart title should describe the main trend you want the chart to show to the user" and allows "15 words or fewer". The blog post that announced the change claimed "compelling evidence" without naming a study, and its only number was about reading, not titles: bulletins "take 9.5 times longer to read than people are spend on the page" [sic]. It is a well-documented Practice, backed by the Evidence above, not Evidence itself.

### Label at the mark — the rule nobody tested

"Label at the mark; legends are a last resort" is a Fixed rule in the Decision Book and a commonplace in every style guide. This chapter searched for the experiment behind it and did not find one. There is no controlled study in the visualisation literature that pits direct labels against a legend on an ordinary chart and measures accuracy, speed or recall. What exists is indirect and, taken together, strong. Lynn Huestegge and Andrea Philipp tracked eyes across graphs with legends: readers made "more frequent" transitions between data and legend when the legend's order did not match the lines ("4.37" against "3.63"), and matching the two "reduced the time needed to understand graphs". Borkin and Matzen both found the legend among the most re-fixated elements — a lookup table the eye keeps returning to. Franconeri's review cites one study where direct labels were faster and more accurate. And the real evidential root is a meta-analysis from educational psychology: Paul Ginns pooled 50 studies of *spatial contiguity* — placing words next to the thing they explain rather than in a separate key or caption — and found an effect of d = 0.72 across 37 comparisons, larger for complex material. None of that is a legend-versus-label experiment on a chart. The rule stands because the mechanism is understood (the round trip costs working memory), the analogous evidence is large, and the accessibility logic of section 4 requires it. The kind is *Practice backed by analogous Evidence*, and the book should say so rather than cite a study that does not exist. `[Further research]`

## 6 · The glance

### Fifty milliseconds, and what it measures

Gitte Lindgaard and colleagues showed people screenshots of web pages for 500 milliseconds, and then for 50, and asked how appealing they were. The ratings were stable: "visual appeal can be assessed within 50 ms, suggesting that web designers have about 50 ms to make a good first impression." The samples were 22, 31 and 40 students, and the measure was a rating from ugly to beautiful. Alexandre Tuch and colleagues then found what drives it. Across 141 people and 120 real sites, two factors predicted the snap judgement: *visual complexity* and *prototypicality*, defined as "the amount to which an object is representative of a class of objects". Both were detectable at 17 milliseconds; by 50, prototypicality was as strong as simplicity; "Overall, websites with low VC and high PT were perceived as highly appealing." Their implication: "Designs that contradict what users typically expect of a website may trigger a suboptimal first impression and impair users' expectations."

The caveat that must travel with every repetition of "fifty milliseconds" is that the thing measured was appeal. Nothing in these studies shows that any information was read. What the first glance decides is whether the reader is disposed to continue, and the two things that dispose them are simplicity and looking like what you claim to be. For a dashboard that means the first glance rewards convention — a point the chartjunk literature never makes and section 7 complicates.

### The real cost of a glance

Tanja Blascheck and colleagues measured how long a two-value comparison takes on a tiny screen. Bars needed about a quarter of a second to be read reliably (245–285 ms), donuts slightly less (159–216 ms), and radial bar charts — the decorative wrapped form — about six times longer (1,548–1,772 ms). "Bar and donut charts should be the preferred encodings when quick comparisons are necessary." A dashboard built from slow-glance forms spends its first impression losing, and the penalty for the ornamental form is not a few per cent but a multiple.

### Where eyes go, and the F that is not a rule

The Nielsen Norman Group's F-shaped pattern is repeated as a layout law and is nothing of the kind. Its own 2017 restatement says "People scan in an F-shape when all of these 3 elements are present", the first being "A page or a section of a page includes text that has little or no formatting for the web", the others a goal of efficiency and low commitment — and that "the F-shaped scanning pattern is bad for users and businesses: it means that users may skip important content". It was measured on text-heavy pages, not dashboards, and it describes a failure of formatting, not a target to design to. The nearest evidence for dashboards is the upper-left bias and title-first start in the dashboard eye-tracking study above, which is consistent with reading order rather than with any letter. Jakob Nielsen's other famous number — that users read "at most 28% of the words" on a page, "20% is more likely" — is a regression of dwell time on word count against an assumed reading speed; nobody measured which words were read.

## 7 · Ornament and memory

### The doctrine and the study that started the argument

Tufte's doctrine, in the standard reproduction: "A large share of ink on a graphic should present data-information, the ink changing as the data change." Ornament that does not — "chartjunk" — is to be erased. The doctrine is Practice; it was asserted, not tested, and for twenty years it was the field's taste.

In 2010 Scott Bateman and colleagues tested it with Nigel Holmes's heavily illustrated charts against plain redrawings. With 20 students, immediate description accuracy did not differ; after two to three weeks, the ten people in the long-term group recalled the subject and categories of the illustrated charts better. The paper concluded, cautiously, that "Our results question some of the premises of the minimalist approach to chart design". It was over-read for a decade. The long-term condition had ten people; one of its four significant results is significant only one-tailed; the charts were single-series and the embellishment extreme. Stephen Few's critique — "The plain charts used in this study were in fact plain ugly" — was intemperate, and the sample was small. The study is the trigger of a debate, not a settled result.

### What memorability is and is not

Borkin's 2013 study, the largest of its kind, had 261 people play a memory game with 2,070 visualisations shown for one second each. Memorability was a stable property — people agreed on which images they had seen before — and it was driven by the things Tufte dislikes: pictograms (1.93 against 1.14), more colours, a low data-ink ratio ("bad" 1.81 against "good" 1.23), and unusual forms; "common graphs are less memorable than unique visualization types." Bar and line charts, the dashboard's staples, are the least memorable images. The authors said in the same paper what this does not mean: "Clearly, a more memorable visualization is not necessarily a more comprehensible one"; the study "examined the memorability of visualizations as if they were images and not memorability based on engagement and comprehension". Their 2015 follow-up closed some of the gap: visualisations memorable at a glance did convey their message, and "Pictograms do not hinder the memory or understanding of a visualization."

Three studies then drew the useful line. Steve Haroz, Kosara and Franconeri tested pictographs across five experiments: "superfluous images can distract. But we find no user costs – and some intriguing benefits – when pictographs are used to represent the data." Pictures that *are* the data are free or helpful; "including an unnecessary background image in a visualization appears to be distracting." Skau, Harrison and Kosara tested the "styles" business tools offer — rounded, triangular, capped bars — on about 94 people and found that "For nearly all tested chart embellishments, even small changes like rounding the top of a bar, led to higher error rate" for relative judgements. Ornament that touches the data mark costs accuracy. And Rita Borgo and colleagues found embellishment helped memory and slowed visual search.† People also simply prefer the ornamented version: Ohad Inbar and colleagues showed 87 students Tufte's own minimal redrawing of a bar chart beside the original, and "People did not like Tufte's minimalist design of bar-graphs; they seem to prefer 'chartjunk' instead."†

### The experiment the debate needed

Kiran Ajani and colleagues — a perception laboratory working with the practitioner Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic — separated the two things that "clean up this chart" bundles together. *Declutter*: remove backgrounds, borders, gridlines, extra labels, colour variety; *focus*: add "a clear headline that describes the relevant data pattern", highlight one subset in a distinct colour, and connect it to a short annotation. Twenty-four people — students and community members at one university — redrew and recalled the charts and rated them. Decluttering made charts look more "professional" (up 1.11 points) and that was most of what it did; "we found weaker but generally positive evidence for the benefits of the declutter guideline". Focusing raised aesthetics and clarity ratings and changed what people took away: "Participants were 2.5-3x more likely to recall a focus-relevant conclusion for focused designs relative to the non-focused designs". And "neither decluttering alone nor the addition of focusing showed strong evidence for improving trustworthiness of a graph". The sample is small, the tasks are memory tasks, and the result has not been replicated; it is reported here as the best single test of the question, not as a settled size. What Tufte got right is that clutter competes with data for attention. What he missed is that the thing that makes a chart communicate is not the ink you remove but the message you add — a headline, one highlight, one sentence — and that clean reads as professional, not as honest.

## 8 · Axes, scales, and the forms the style guides argue about

### Truncating the bar

The rule "start bars at zero" is believed to be about honesty, and the experiments show it is about something stranger. Michael Correll, Enrico Bertini and Franconeri truncated bar and line charts by 0, 25 and 50% and asked people how severe the depicted difference was. "Increased y-axis truncation results in increased perceived severity" (p < 0.0001), equally for bars and lines. Then they tried the remedies the style guides offer — a broken-axis marker, a gradient fade at the base: "There was no significant difference between perceived severity among visualization designs" (the test was borderline, p = 0.05, with no pairwise difference). People could read the numbers correctly and still felt the difference as larger: "Surprisingly, the accurate estimation of values does not seem to counteract the visual magnification of difference." Their conclusion: "Merely indicating that truncation has occurred, even in a prominent and unambiguous way, may not be sufficient to 'de-bias' viewers of truncated charts." Brenda Yang and colleagues found the same across five studies: the effect "persisted after viewers were taught about the effects of y-axis truncation", and "83.5% of participants across all 5 studies showing a truncation effect."† A 2024 study by Long and Kay adds that objective task accuracy for comparing bar lengths is often unaffected — it is the *impression* of effect size that inflates. People read the right numbers and feel the wrong size. Correll's authors "reject the unequivocal dichotomy of 'honest' and 'dishonest' charts" and put the responsibility where it belongs: designers should "consider the scale of the meaningful effect sizes and variation they intend to communicate, regardless of the visual encoding." "There is no obvious way for designers to relinquish the responsibility of considering effect size in their charts."

So the Decision Book's Fixed rule — "Never break the numerical axis on a bar chart" — holds, and the Analysis Function's 2023 checklist already gives the better reason: "Even if a break in the axis is clearly signposted, the relative size of bars is still displayed very strongly on bar charts." The experiments confirm it. Its Adaptable rule that a line chart's axis "may be broken where necessary, and labelled" needs the same caveat: the label does not neutralise the impression; the designer is choosing an effect size and must know it.

### The forms with less evidence than their reputations

*Dual axes.* The prohibition on two series sharing a chart with two y-axes is universal in UK government guidance ("they can be easily misinterpreted") and rests, as far as this chapter could find, on no controlled study at all. The experiment usually cited — Petra Isenberg and colleagues, 2011 — is about *dual-scale* charts, one series shown at two resolutions, and found the superimposed design "very confusing"; it is not about two series. The only recent paper is a defence by Richard Brath and colleagues from financial-services practice, noting dual axes in "19% of 944 timeseries charts". The absence of evidence is itself a finding: the rule is Practice, plausible, and untested. `[Further research]`

*3D.* The study everyone cites for banning 3D bars, by Jeffrey Zacks, Barbara Tversky and colleagues, found the depth cues cost about half a percentage point of accuracy and that "the effect of the bar height manipulation on accuracy was about one order of magnitude larger than the effect of adding depth cues." Their words: "warnings about accuracy decrements due to 3D shading may be overstated, whereas distortions due to neighboring elements should be of more concern." 3D is still a bad idea — it adds nothing, obscures, and fails accessibility — but the Decision Book's reason ("moves the encoding to rank 5") is the conjectural half of the hierarchy again.

*Pies.* Read by area, not angle (section 3); donuts no worse than pies; the Analysis Function's "five categories or fewer" with "a dominant category" is sound Practice for a form whose real weakness is comparing slices that are nearly equal.

*Stacked bars.* The one form the evidence does condemn for a specific task: comparing segments that do not share a baseline forces a length judgement and carries the largest error Talbot measured (Cleveland & McGill; Talbot's divided-bar result). Fine for totals and for the bottom segment; wrong for comparing the middle.

### Scales that change the reader's mind

*Log scales.* Alessandro Romano and colleagues showed about 2,000 Americans COVID deaths on a log or a linear scale. On the log scale "Only 40.66% of them could respond correctly to a basic question about the graph (whether there were more deaths in one week or another), contrasted to 83.79% of respondents on the linear scale"; the log group also predicted more deaths and worried less; the authors recommend that public communicators "should always describe the evolution of the pandemic using a graph on a linear scale, at least as a default option."† A log scale is a specialist's tool; on the one question tested, it halved the share of the public who could read it.

*Cumulative against daily.* Nicholas Reinholtz and colleagues, in a preregistered study of 596 people, showed the same twenty days of pandemic data as new cases per day or as a running total. When daily cases were falling, people shown the falling flow judged current risk lower (4.72) than people shown the ever-rising stock (6.58; d = 0.74); when daily cases were rising there was no difference. "The choice of how to present pandemic data can lead people to different conclusions about risk". A cumulative curve always goes up; it is a persuasive choice whether or not it was meant as one, and Chapter 2's finding that the denominator is the persuasive unit has a sibling here in the choice of stock or flow.

### Tables, numbers and precision

*Tables against graphs.* Iris Vessey's cognitive-fit theory, the standard warrant, says that tables suit "symbolic" tasks — looking up a value, reading a point — and graphs suit "spatial" ones — trends, comparisons, shape — and that performance improves when the representation fits the task. The companion experiment with 128 students supports it; for reading individual points "accuracy was higher and reaction time faster with tables than with line graphs", for comparing differences "Performance was better with graphs than with tables".† Cleveland and McGill said the same in 1984. A dashboard that shows a value the reader wants to *know* as a chart has chosen the wrong form.

*The big number.* The "KPI tile" — one large figure with a label — is the most common object on a business dashboard and this chapter could find no controlled study of whether it is read accurately, trusted or misread against a chart or a table cell. The only direct evidence is that it is looked at: in Yang's dashboard study, "Number gains the highest saliency coverage". Cognitive fit predicts it is right for a point-value question. Its evidential basis is Practice, and the gap is logged. `[Prototype flag]`

*Rounding.* Andrew Ehrenberg's 1977 "Rudiments of Numeracy" gave the rules every statistician learned — round to two effective digits, order rows by size, put figures to be compared in columns, give averages — and, as far as this chapter can find, they were "asserted without experimental results" and never tested with readers. The nearest empirical echo is incidental: in Talbot's bar-chart experiment people answered in "multiples of 10, followed by multiples of 5" and gave 50% "nearly 5 times as often as expected" — readers round to about two digits whether or not the chart does. Tim Cole, a medical statistician, summarises the modern consensus: "the general principle is to use two or three significant digits for effect sizes, and one or two significant digits for measures of variability".

*Precision as a signal.* Three consumer studies, each read at abstract or press-summary level only, agree that precise numbers are read as confident and trustworthy — "precision is used as a confidence signal"; estimates "expressed in finer granularity" are judged "more precise" and held with "more confidence" — until the reader doubts the communicator's good faith, at which point the effect disappears. This is the mechanism behind the Decision Book's "Never round to imply precision you do not have": spurious precision works on readers, which is exactly why it is a trust hazard when the data cannot bear it.

*Frequencies against percentages.* Gerd Gigerenzer and Ulrich Hoffrage showed that people reason about chance far better when it is stated as natural frequencies — "9 in 100" rather than "9%" — than as probabilities; Michelle McDowell and Perke Jacobs's 2017 meta-analysis of 226 estimates puts correct Bayesian reasoning at 24% with frequencies against 4% with probabilities, which is both a large improvement and a reminder that three quarters still fail. Section 11 takes this up with risk.

## 9 · Interaction, defaults and animation

### How many readers touch anything

The most-quoted statistic about interactive graphics is that 85% of readers ignore them. Its origin is a footnote. Gregor Aisch, then at the *New York Times*, wrote in 2017: "The number comes from me measuring the percent of readers who clicked on a prominent button in a couple of graphics we published in 2015. I mentioned the number in a conference talk, from where it ended up in another talk, then on Medium and finally on FastCo.Design. However, the 85% of readers didn't ignore the graphic, they just didn't click the button." His own rule from it: "you should not hide important content behind interactions. If some information is crucial, don't make the user click or hover to see it"; and "not everything is crucial and 15% of readers aren't nobody." His editor Archie Tse's three rules, from a 2016 talk whose slides contain no percentage at all: "1. If you make the reader click or do anything other than scroll, something spectacular has to happen. 2. If you make a tooltip or rollover, assume no one will ever see it. If content is important for readers to see, don't hide it. 3. When deciding whether to make something interactive, remember that getting it to work on all platforms is expensive." The reason given: "readers weren't getting to all of the content"; "Readers just want to scroll."

The best public measurement replaces the anecdote. Matthew Conlen, Alex Kale and Heer instrumented three live interactive articles read by tens of thousands of people. A central one-tap control was used by 85%; a text hover by 54%; and for the optional controls, "The median number of times a reader hovered over an artwork was 4, the median number of interactions with the algorithm details button and with the slider was zero." About three quarters of readers reached the end of two of the articles, under half of the third. On mobile, "Only 10% of mobile readers triggered one of the example audio clips"; median time on a page was 20–41 seconds against 78–116 on desktop; "Mobile readers utilized fewer of the available interactive features than their desktop counterparts, but still largely followed the presented narratives." Their line for designers: "If details were only revealed after a click, readers may never see it." Two further findings complete the picture. Jeremy Boy and colleagues found that readers of charts embedded in text do not know they are interactive unless told, and only one of three visual cues designed to signal it worked.† And left to explore freely, crowd participants in Mi Feng's experiments visited about a quarter of twenty available charts and 8% of 255. People finish; they do not fiddle; and on a phone, where hover does not exist, they do less of both.

### The default is advice

Chapter 1 cited, from version 1 of this book, the finding that a default is read as a recommendation. Re-verified for this chapter: Jon Jachimowicz and colleagues' meta-analysis of 58 datasets from 55 studies, 73,675 people in all, found a default effect of d = 0.68 (95% confidence interval 0.53–0.83), an opt-out default raising uptake "by an absolute average of 27.24%", and — citing McKenzie and colleagues — that "Individuals commonly perceive defaults as conveying an endorsement by the choice architect": "the more decision-makers believe that the default reflects a trusted recommendation, the more effective the default is likely to be." The domain is policy and consumer choice, not dashboards; the mechanism transfers. Hullman and Diakopoulos named the design-side consequence: "Default views provide an initial point of interpretation anchored to the default visual configuration", and "Choosing a default view, for example, may be unavoidable, but the choice of a single default view for all users is not a given."

The anchoring is measurable. Isaac Cho and colleagues trained 81 people on a multi-view analytics tool starting from either the map or the calendar; the view they started in dominated the first fifth of their session and shaped their estimates, and a high or low number shown first anchored their final answers (52.7 against 21.1 for the same question). Across five studies and 726 people, André Calero Valdez and colleagues found that the chart you saw a moment ago shifts how you judge the next one — a small, reliable priming effect. And Cindy Xiong, Lisa van Weelden and Franconeri documented the designer's blind spot: people told what to see in a chart rated that pattern as more salient and assumed naive viewers would see it too — "The inability to separate one's own knowledge and expertise from that of their audience can make visual data communication more difficult and less clear than presenters realize." The person who chose the default cannot see the chart as a newcomer does. `[Validate with users]`

### "Overview first" audited

Shneiderman's mantra is printed ten times in his 1996 paper, followed by: "Each line represents one project in which I found myself rediscovering this principle and therefore wrote it down as a reminder. It proved to be only a starting point". Nine years later Brock Craft and Paul Cairns audited the literature citing it: "At the time of this writing, there are at least 52 citations of the 1996 paper", sorted into implementations, methods, evaluations and taxonomies, and "although numerous authors cite the Mantra, there are no reasonably obvious studies that have validated Shneiderman's recommendations." Kasper Hornbæk and Morten Hertzum then reviewed 60 papers on overviews: "In 16 of the experimental papers an overview is compared with a baseline interface, for a total of 23 such comparisons." "A near unanimous preference for overviews over the baseline interfaces is found" where people were asked; "tasks are solved faster with overviews in half of the comparisons that measure task completion time … but slower in four comparisons"; "only two of 17 comparisons find that overviews are more accurate than baseline interfaces"; "We see a disappointing effect of support for overviewing with overviews". The counter-mantra, Frank van Ham and Adam Perer's "search, show context, expand on demand", argues that "there are many situations where such an overview is not relevant or practical for users, as analyzing the global structure may not be related to the main task" — from case studies, with no controlled test. People like overviews reliably and are helped by them unreliably; a reader who arrives with a place in mind is not an overview reader.

### Animation

Barbara Tversky, Julie Morrison and Mireille Bétrancourt reviewed twenty years of studies and found that where animation seemed to beat static graphics, "scrutiny reveals lack of equivalence between animated and static graphics in content or procedures"; "Students in the animated graphic conditions did not outperform those in the equivalent text or static graphic conditions." Animations fail the *apprehension principle* — "often too complex or too fast to be accurately perceived" — and "many continuous events are conceived of as sequences of discrete steps." Franconeri's review gives the capacity limit: "Our capacity for tracking the motion of objects that move in arbitrary directions is highly limited, to as few as one or two objects at a time."

George Robertson and colleagues tested the Gapminder style directly — animated bubbles against static traces against small multiples — with 36 people for analysis and presentation. "Results indicate that trend animation can be challenging to use even for presentations; while it is the fastest technique for presentation and participants find it enjoyable and exciting, it does lead to many participant errors. Animation is the least effective form for analysis; both static depictions of trends are significantly faster than animation, and the small multiples display is more accurate." Accuracy "averaging 65%" overall; enjoyment 4.3 for animation against 3.7. Their recommendation: "examining their data with a small multiples view, but then presenting it with animation." Brehmer and colleagues replicated the time result on phones with 96 people: small multiples were consistently faster. Waqas Javed and colleagues' comparison of overlaid lines against small multiples found overlay best for local tasks and split charts best for dispersed ones, and that more series meant more errors for everyone.

Animation reliably helps in one place. Heer and Robertson tested animated transitions between states of the same chart — a bar chart becoming a pie, a scatterplot filtering — and animation beat static cuts in every condition for tracking which mark became which, and for estimating how a value changed; staged animation beat direct. "Animation improved graphical perception at both syntactic (object tracking) and semantic (change estimation) levels of analysis", by preserving "object constancy for changing objects". Motion that carries data is a working-memory tax; motion that preserves identity across a change is a working-memory aid.

On scrolling stories, Sean McKenna and colleagues' study of 240 people found that visuals and animated transitions raised self-reported engagement, that choosing to step or to scroll made no measurable difference, and that comprehension was flat across all conditions. "Engagement" was a questionnaire score. The newsroom's "readers just want to scroll" is a true report of behaviour and not yet evidence that scrolling teaches. More generally, belief five's second half — that interactivity is itself a good — has no support in this literature: Boy and colleagues found a narrative lead-in did not make people explore more, McKenna found comprehension unchanged by any flow, and no study found here shows that a reader who interacts understands more than one who does not. `[Further research]`

## 10 · Uncertainty

Chapter 1 established that uncertainty shown as a number does not cost trust, and Chapter 2 that vague hedging does. This chapter asks the design question: in what *form* do people read uncertainty correctly?

### The taxonomy and the known misreadings

Lace Padilla, Matthew Kay and Jessica Hullman's review sorts the options into graphical annotations of a distribution (means, intervals, moments), encodings of probability to a visual channel (colour, position, transparency), hybrids, and frequency-based formats, of which the review reports that "Other work proposes that showing distributional information in a frequency format (e.g., 1 out of 10 rather than 10%) more naturally matches how people think about uncertainty". It names the errors. *Deterministic construal*: "When individuals attempt to substitute visual uncertainty information for deterministic information." *Boundary* or *containment*: "Ranges that are represented by boundaries lead people to believe that data inside and outside the boundary are categorically different." *Within-the-bar bias*: a point inside a bar is believed to belong to the population and an equidistant point just outside it not to. Its verdict on intervals is firm — "visualizations of intervals have widely documented issues … such as provoking viewers to incorrectly think of distributional data as categorical" — and its recommendation plural: "If a designer needs to show an interval, we also recommend displaying information that is more representative, such as a scatterplot, violin plot, gradient plot, ensemble plot, quantile dotplot, or HOP" — a HOP being a hypothetical outcome plot, an animation of individual draws, described below. "There is no one-size-fits-all uncertainty visualization approach". `[Rich reference]`

### The experiments

Correll and Michael Gleicher's "Error Bars Considered Harmful": "A bar in a bar chart creates a region of false certainty" — "values visually within the bar are perceived as likelier data points than values outside of the bar" — and bar-with-whisker charts led people to "predict outcomes that were significantly larger than with other encodings" with "unjustified" confidence; gradient and violin plots aligned lay judgements with the statistics. Hullman, Paul Resnick and Eytan Adar's *hypothetical outcome plots* — "animates a finite set of individual draws" — on 288 people: "With HOPs, people made much more accurate judgments about plots of two and three quantities", with no advantage for questions about one. Kay and colleagues' *quantile dotplots* for bus arrival times on about 540 people: twenty countable dots "reduce the variance of probabilistic estimates by ~1.15 times compared to density plots and facilitate more confident estimation"; a hundred dots did no better than a smooth curve, "consistent with people estimating area instead of counting dots". The gain is modest; the lesson is precise — a countable frequency beats an uncountable shape.

Jake Hofman, Daniel Goldstein and Hullman's finding is the one most relevant to a dashboard that puts a confidence interval round an average. Across 1,743 and 1,830 people, showing the uncertainty in the *estimate* (a 95% confidence interval) rather than the spread of *outcomes* (a prediction interval) made readers overestimate a treatment's effect on individuals: "participants who saw 95% CIs thought that they would win 86% of the time … whereas those who were shown 95% PIs reported an average probability of superiority of 67%", against a true value of 57%; they would pay "80 Ice Dollars compared to 50" for the same small advantage. "Because scientists often use CIs and publish small effects, many scientific results are likely exaggerated in the minds of some readers." A tight error bar round a mean tells the reader the mean is well known; the reader hears that everyone gets the mean.

Boundaries are read as categories and as shapes. Padilla, Ian Ruginski and Sarah Creem-Regehr found that viewers of the hurricane "cone of uncertainty" believed the storm itself grew — they read "the growing diameter of the cone boundaries" as size — and that places inside the cone were judged categorically differently from places just outside; spaghetti-line ensembles fixed that and created a new error, with viewers over-weighting any town a drawn line happened to cross. "decisions about visualization methods should be informed by the viewer's task." On maps generally, Alan MacEachren and colleagues found that *fuzziness*, *lightness* and positional jitter were read as "uncertain" almost without instruction and that hue, shape and orientation were not — though their participants were GIS professionals, and the two cartographic reviews of lay readers conclude only that showing uncertainty usually changes decisions, rarely slows people, and "is context dependent and still thoroughly up for debate".

And the experts cannot do it either. Sarah Belia and colleagues asked 473 published researchers in psychology, neuroscience and medicine to read error bars: "Only 22% of respondents set the means so the p value was between .025 and .10"; "31.5% … used the incorrect rule that error bars just touch when means are just statistically significantly different". "We conclude that very many researchers … have fundamental and severe misconceptions about how CIs and SE bars can justifiably be used." If the people who draw error bars misread them, the public will.

### Words for uncertainty

"Likely" is not a number. David Budescu and colleagues, across "25 samples in 24 countries and 17 languages", found that "Laypeople interpret IPCC statements as conveying probabilities closer to 50% than intended by the IPCC authors", and that "An alternative presentation format supplementing the verbal terms with numerical ranges increases the correspondence". Bonnie Wintle and colleagues, with 924 members of the public, measured the fix: agreement with the intended band was 32% for the word alone and 66% when the number sat in brackets beside it; a separate glossary did not help — "Correspondence … is substantially improved only when numerical guidelines are bracketed in text." The negative words were the loosest ("Very Unlikely", "Unlikely"). A dashboard that labels confidence with words puts the number next to the word, every time; even then a third of readers miss the band, so the word is never the only carrier.

David Spiegelhalter's review of risk communication closes the section with rules that transfer whole: "Use absolute risks"; "express chance as a proportion, a frequency, or a percentage—it is crucial to be clear about the reference class"; "provide percentages or frequencies both with and without the outcome"; "Keep the denominator fixed when making comparisons"; "Be explicit about the time interval"; "Consider a good summary table as a visualization"; "Use multiple formats, because no single representation suits all members of an audience"; and "Have the humility to admit uncertainty."

### The counterbalance from Chapter 2

Markant and colleagues' 2023 study is the one finding in this section that pulls against the rest, and it is reported with the authors' own caution. Among 331 people shown correlations with or without uncertainty (a bare line; animated draws; a static ensemble), "the effect of global attitude was more pronounced in visualization conditions with uncertainty representations … compared to visualizations with only the scatterplot and correlation line"; for readers with strong prior attitudes, "visual uncertainty representations may amplify this effect" of discounting contrary data. Their reading: a motivated reader "attend[s] to the uncertainty representation … because they are motivated to find support for their existing belief", using the spread of plausible lines as material for doubt. Their caveat: "alternative models achieved comparable performance" and the result "warrants further investigation to evaluate its robustness." Shown uncertainty is not neutral; for the reader who already disagrees, honesty may be read as weakness. That does not license hiding it — Chapters 1 and 2 are clear about what hiding costs — but it means the form matters for the sceptic as much as for the novice. `[Counterbalance → Ch 2 §5]` `[Further research]`

## 11 · Risk formats, and what the public can read

### Risk

The health-communication literature has done the experiments a civic dashboard needs. Gigerenzer and colleagues' review: with conditional probabilities, 21% of 160 gynaecologists could say what a positive mammogram meant; with natural frequencies, 87%. A relative risk reduction of "25%" was heard as hundreds of lives per thousand when the absolute figure was about one. Elie Akl and colleagues' Cochrane review of 35 studies confirms the pattern: a relative risk reduction "was perceived to be larger and more persuasive" than the same absolute figure, with "little or no difference in understanding" and no difference between professionals and the public. Stefania Pighin and colleagues' seven experiments add the denominator trap: "A 1-in-X format (e.g., 1 in 200) is subjectively perceived as bigger and more alarming than an N-in-XN format (e.g., 5 in 1000)", and the effect "disappears with an icon array visual aid."† Rocio Garcia-Retamero and Edward Cokely's systematic review of 36 studies and 27,885 people in 60 countries found that "Eighty-eight percent of the studies showed that static visual aids tend to be beneficial", that icon arrays "can effectively reduce or eliminate denominator neglect, especially in people with low numeracy and/or high graph literacy" — and, from Yasmina Okan's work, that they help the graph-literate most and the graph-illiterate least.† A visual aid is not a universal fix. Their design heuristics transfer almost line for line: one message per visual; depict both the affected individuals and the whole population at risk; keep denominators the same size when comparing; "Use absolute rather than relative risks"; "Use frequencies rather than decimals, fractions, or percentages"; validate the visual aid with the audience before using it. Angela Fagerlin's ten steps add the ones about order and quantity: the order in which risks and benefits are presented changes perception; less information may be more effective; and the time interval over which a risk occurs should be stated again and again.

### Literacy

Who is the reader? Mirta Galesic and Garcia-Retamero's graph-literacy scale, run on nationally representative samples in Germany (495) and the United States (492): people got "approximately 9 of 13 items" right, and "Approximately one third of the population in both countries had both low graph literacy and low numeracy skills."† Katy Börner and colleagues asked 273 science-museum visitors — a more educated and interested public than average — to name twenty visualisations: charts were recognised most, network layouts least; "very few correctly identified the specific visual (technical label: 14%)"; "less than 50% of respondents gave answers that would be considered at least partially accurate"; and when asked how to read them, most "pointed to superficial features such as color, lines, or text". The study measured naming, not reading. Sukwon Lee and colleagues' visualisation-literacy test gives adult internet workers about half of 53 items right, with stacked area charts and treemaps the hardest.

For England, the official numeracy figure is the OECD's 2023 adult skills survey (PIAAC): 21% of adults at Level 1 or below, people who "struggle to complete mathematical tasks with distracting information or carry out multi-step mathematical processes" — better than the OECD average of 25%, and improving since 2012. The famous "half of UK adults have the numeracy of a primary-school leaver" is a different measurement with a different threshold: the 2011 Skills for Life survey found "49.1% were Entry level 3 or below" (the attainment expected at ages 9–11). Both are true; they should not be presented as the same scale. And the Office for Statistics Regulation's own review of statistical literacy is the one a dashboard builder should sit with: it found numeracy surveys, and "No evidence was found that aimed to capture the multidimensional nature of statistical literacy"; it calls for "more up-to-date, large-scale surveys". Nothing it found measured the public's ability to read a chart or a table. `[Prototype flag]` — the Vibe Map could.

## 12 · The accessibility standards, classified

Carly asked that the W3C documents be sorted into hard lines and interpretable guidance. Chapter 13 is the accessibility chapter; this section does the sorting, because the one rule this chapter owns (colour never alone) sits inside it, and because a first-time reader needs to know which of these documents can be broken and which cannot.

**WCAG 2.2** — W3C Recommendation, 12 December 2024 — is the hard line. Its success criteria have conformance levels; Level AA means "the web page satisfies all the Level A and Level AA success criteria"; the W3C itself says "It is not recommended that Level AAA conformance be required as a general policy for entire sites". The criteria that bite on charts, in their normative words:

- **1.1.1 Non-text Content (A).** "All non-text content that is presented to the user has a text alternative that serves the equivalent purpose". A chart is non-text content; "equivalent purpose" is the operative phrase, and a one-line alt is not equivalent for a chart carrying data.
- **1.3.1 Info and Relationships (A).** "Information, structure, and relationships conveyed through presentation can be programmatically determined or are available in text." The table behind a chart needs real header cells; a dashboard's grouping of tiles must be structural, not only visual.
- **1.4.1 Use of Color (A).** Quoted in section 4. A legend that distinguishes series only by colour fails it.
- **1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum) (AA)** and **1.4.11 Non-text Contrast (AA).** 4.5:1 for text; 3:1 for "Parts of graphics required to understand the content". The only WCAG text that names charts is 1.4.11's explanatory page.
- **1.4.10 Reflow (AA).** Content must work "at a width equivalent to 320 CSS pixels" without two-dimensional scrolling — except "Images required for understanding (such as maps and diagrams)" and "data tables". A map is exempt; a dashboard as a whole is not.
- **2.1.1 Keyboard (A).** "All functionality of the content is operable through a keyboard interface". A hover-only tooltip delivers information no keyboard user can reach.
- **2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum) (AA).** Pointer targets "at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels", with an exception the W3C illustrates with map pins: "It is essential to show the pins at the correct map location, therefore the Essential exception applies."
- **3.1.5 Reading Level** is **AAA**: text above lower-secondary reading level needs a simpler version or supplement. Because it is AAA, plain language is *not* a WCAG hard line; it is guidance, and in this studio a voluntary standard adopted from the design system and the Decision Book.

**WAI-ARIA 1.2** is a normative specification of roles and properties, not a conformance target for content. It has no chart role — none exists in ARIA — and the 2015 proposal for chart roles was never standardised. The *Graphics Module* adds `graphics-document`, which "authors should use … for structured graphics such as charts, maps, diagrams". Practically: an SVG chart without `role="img"` (or `graphics-document`) and an accessible name is invisible to a screen reader.

**The WAI tutorial on Complex Images** is guidance — educational material, not a standard — but it is the W3C's own reading of how 1.1.1 applies to charts: "A two-part text alternative is required. The first part is the short description to identify the image … The second part is the long description – a textual representation of the essential information conveyed by the image", which for charts should cover "scales, values, relationships and trends". The Decision Book's rule that alt text "states the finding, not the chart type" is this guidance made house rule.

**COGA — *Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities*** — is a Working Group Note and says so: "The objectives and patterns presented here provide supplemental guidance beyond the requirements of WCAG"; "Following this guidance is not required for conformance to WCAG." Its patterns that speak to a numbers-heavy screen — "Provide Alternatives for Numerical Concepts"; "Do Not Rely on Users Calculations or Memorizing Information"; "Use Clear Words"; "Provide Summary of Long Documents and Media" — are the closest thing the W3C has to cognitive-accessibility rules for dashboards, and they are interpretable. There is no WAI document on charts or data visualisation; a community group formed to write one closed in 2023 with no deliverables.

**Who is bound.** The Public Sector Bodies Accessibility Regulations 2018 require public bodies to meet the accessibility requirement — WCAG 2.2 AA, in the government's current guidance — and publish a statement; the Regulations exempt "non-governmental organisations, unless they provide services that—(i) are essential to the public; or (ii) specifically address the needs of, or are meant for, persons with disabilities", which the GOV.UK guidance renders as charities being exempt "unless they are mostly financed by public funding, provide services that are essential to the public or aimed at disabled people". Nightlife Culture is not a public body, so the Regulations do not reach it directly. The Equality Act 2010 does: section 20's duty to make reasonable adjustments, which for information includes ensuring "the information is provided in an accessible format", is, in the Act's explanatory notes, "an anticipatory duty which means service providers … must anticipate the needs of disabled people and make appropriate reasonable adjustments." WCAG 2.2 AA is the standard a court or the Equality and Human Rights Commission would take as the measure of reasonable. So: WCAG 2.2 AA is a hard line for public bodies by regulation and the de facto yardstick for everyone else; the Analysis Function's and ONS's chart rules — no legends, label at the end of the line, bars start at zero, four lines at most, data download with every chart, titles that state the message — are voluntary Standards the studio has, in effect, already adopted through the Decision Book. Chapter 13 takes it from here. `[Revisit → Ch 13]`

## 13 · What holds true regardless

The brief asked for "the short list that survives". Here it is in two parts: the canonical truths — seven, each a rule a dashboard can be held to — and, beneath them, the findings of record that justify them, which are true statements about the evidence rather than rules in their own right. Later chapters test both; the closing chapter confirms or demotes.

### The seven

1. **Choose the comparison, not the chart.** The eye pulls statistics from a whole display at once and makes comparisons one at a time, slowly; every design that works chooses the comparison for the reader — by grouping, by one highlight, by words. *Evidence (Franconeri et al. 2021; Ajani et al. 2022, small). Fixed.*
2. **Words carry the message: the trend in the title, the numbers beside the marks, and title and picture pointing at the same thing.** The title is looked at first and longest, decides what the chart "showed", is remembered when the reader says it is not, and is believed over a picture it contradicts. *Evidence (Borkin et al.; Matzen et al.; Kong et al. 2018, 2019 — nationally representative; Stokes et al., n = 302; Kim et al., n = 2,168). Fixed.*
3. **Colour is never the only carrier of meaning, and labels sit at the mark.** The first is a Level A success criterion; the second is how a chart meets it, and rests on a working-memory mechanism and analogous evidence rather than a direct test. *Standard (WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.1) and Practice backed by analogous Evidence (Ginns; Huestegge & Philipp). Fixed.*
4. **Bars start at zero, and a broken line axis is a chosen effect size, declared.** No label or lesson undoes the exaggeration. *Evidence (Correll et al.; Yang et al.†; Long & Kay) and Standard (Analysis Function). Fixed.*
5. **Uncertainty is shown as something countable or continuous, never as a hard boundary; outcome spread where individuals are the subject; and a word for probability carries its number.** *Evidence (Correll & Gleicher; Hullman et al.; Kay et al.; Hofman et al., n > 3,500; Padilla et al.; Belia et al.; Budescu et al.; Wintle et al.). Fixed.*
6. **Nothing crucial lives behind a click, a hover or a tooltip; the display is read on a phone, in a glance, by someone at PIAAC Level 1.** *Evidence from adjacent domains (Conlen et al. — interactive articles; PIAAC; Galesic & Garcia-Retamero†) and Standard (WCAG 2.1.1). Fixed as the planning assumption.*
7. **Every default is advice the designer is giving, and it anchors; it is chosen, written down and shown as chosen.** *Evidence from an adjacent domain (Jachimowicz et al., policy defaults) and in visualisation (Cho et al.; Valdez et al.; Hullman & Diakopoulos). Fixed as a stance.*

### The record beneath them

These are what the evidence says; they are not rules, and several come from small or adjacent-domain studies, which is noted.

- For a precise ratio comparison, position beats length beats angle; the bottom of the hierarchy was never tested by its authors; pies are read by area; and the gaps between lower chart types are smaller than the gaps between people. *(Cleveland & McGill; Heer & Bostock; Talbot et al.; Kosara & Skau; Davis et al.)* As a design rule the hierarchy is a default for precise comparison, Adaptable otherwise.
- Lightness carries order and hue carries category; the rainbow exaggerates by binning on pattern maps and helps when reading a single value; about 8% of men of European descent have a red–green deficiency; seven is the experimental ceiling for searchable colours and small marks need bigger differences. *(Liu & Heer; Reda et al.; Franconeri; Birch†; Healey 1996; Szafir.)*
- The first glance judges appeal, not meaning, on simplicity and prototypicality — from website screenshots and small student samples. *(Lindgaard et al.; Tuch et al.)* A radial bar takes six times longer to read than a bar — from 36 people on a smartwatch. *(Blascheck et al.)*
- Ornament that is the data is free; ornament that touches the mark costs accuracy; ornament behind the data costs time; clutter removed reads as professional, and a headline with one highlight is what is remembered — the last from 24 people, unreplicated. *(Haroz et al.; Skau et al.; Borgo et al.†; Ajani et al.)* The data-ink doctrine itself is Practice.
- A log scale halved correct reading of one question; a cumulative curve raised perceived risk when daily cases fell; a relative risk persuades without informing; "1 in 200" alarms more than "5 in 1,000"; frequencies are reasoned about better than percentages, and three quarters still fail. *(Romano et al.†; Reinholtz et al.; Akl et al.; Pighin et al.†; Gigerenzer & Hoffrage; McDowell & Jacobs.)* Linear, daily, absolute, fixed-denominator are the public defaults.
- Two or three significant figures; precision is read as confidence. *(Ehrenberg — Practice; Cole; three consumer studies†.)*
- Most readers touch nothing optional; mobile readers touch less and leave sooner — from interactive articles, not dashboards. *(Conlen et al.; Aisch; Tse.)*
- "Overview first" is a reminder, not a tested law; people prefer overviews reliably and are helped unreliably. *(Shneiderman; Craft & Cairns; Hornbæk & Hertzum.)*
- Animation that carries data taxes memory and is less accurate for analysis; animation that preserves identity across a change helps; small multiples for analysis. *(Tversky et al.; Robertson et al., n = 36; Heer & Robertson, n = 24; Brehmer et al., n = 96.)*
- Shown uncertainty may harden a reader who already disagrees — one study, with the authors' own caveat. *(Markant et al.)* `[Counterbalance → Ch 2 §5]`
- Five WCAG success criteria are the hard lines on charts — 1.1.1, 1.3.1, 1.4.1, 1.4.11, 2.1.1 — with 1.4.3, 1.4.10 and 2.5.8 beside them; plain language (3.1.5) is AAA and so guidance; a non-public body is bound through the Equality Act's anticipatory duty with WCAG 2.2 AA as the yardstick. *(Standard.)*

## 14 · Where the choice is yours

**Which chart, for most purposes.** Below a precise ratio comparison, the hierarchy does not decide; convention, the reader's schema, and the comparison you want made do. A donut is not worse than a pie; a treemap is defensible for part-of-whole with labels; a radial bar is six times slower to read. *Adaptable.*

**How many colours.** Seven is the experimental ceiling for search; four or five is the house limit of the UK statistics bodies; small marks need bigger steps. Where to set the Vibe Map's limit is a design-system decision informed by section 4. *Adaptable.*

**How much text.** The evidence says more than designers give and the trend in the title; how much more, and in what voice, is the studio's. *Adaptable.*

**Convention or novelty at first glance.** Prototypicality rewards resemblance to the thing claimed. Whether the Vibe Map claims to be a dashboard, a map, a flyer or something else — and so what it should resemble in fifty milliseconds — is the creative decision this book never makes. *Adaptable; Carly's.*

**Which uncertainty form.** Dotplots, HOPs, gradients, violins, ensembles each read differently and each has a failure mode; the choice depends on the question (one quantity or two; a value or a comparison) and the reader (novice or sceptic). *Adaptable; the prohibition on bare intervals and hard boundaries is Fixed.*

**Breaking a line axis.** Permitted, and a chosen effect size; declare it. *Adaptable.*

**Overview or search first; animation or small multiples; scroll or step.** The reader's arriving question, the task, and the device decide; none is a law. *Adaptable.*

**Rounding.** Two or three significant figures; which, by column, is a judgement. *Adaptable within the Fixed ceiling of the data's own precision.*

## 15 · What this means for a nightlife dashboard

*An illustration, not a specification.*

The brief's own failure register says a dashboard fails if "a viewer reads it and asks 'what am I looking at'", if it is "a fancy spreadsheet with some charts that tell you lots of big numbers, but not what actually happens", and if "people dont trust it". Read through this chapter, those three failures have mechanisms.

"What am I looking at" is a schema failure. The public can name a bar chart and cannot name a treemap; scatterplots are misunderstood by more than a third of American adults; the forms expert communities invent require a schema that most of the public — the dancefloor included — does not have. A nightlife dashboard that wants to be read by a promoter and a committee at once builds, at the level of the chart, from forms both already know, and spends its invention — the brief asks for nightlife data to be as exciting as a club flyer — in layout, hierarchy, words and register, where the Decision Book already says a dashboard's creativity should live. The evidence on the first glance says the same thing from the other side: appeal in fifty milliseconds comes from simplicity and from looking like what you claim to be. What the Vibe Map claims to be is Carly's decision; this chapter only says that whatever it claims, it should look like that at once.

"Big numbers, but not what actually happens" is a comparison failure. A big number is the most-looked-at object on a dashboard and this chapter found no evidence that it is understood. The eye pulls a statistic in a glance and makes a comparison slowly; a tile that shows a count and no comparison has given the reader the fast thing and withheld the slow one. The fix is in words and in one highlight: a title that states the trend, a number beside its context, a single comparison chosen for the reader — the design that, in the one small experiment that tested it, was what people remembered. And the numbers themselves: two or three figures, a frequency not a percentage, a fixed denominator, the time interval stated.

"People don't trust it" has, in this chapter, three design mechanisms. A truncated axis, a cumulative curve, a log scale or a relative risk each move the reader without the reader noticing, and a sceptical reader who later notices does not forgive it; the defaults are linear, daily, absolute, from zero. A bare interval or a tight error bar round an average overstates what is known about individuals; the uncertainty is shown as something countable, with the number beside any word. And for the reader who arrives already against the night time economy, Chapter 2's plain table and this chapter's Markant finding point the same way: the form of the uncertainty display is itself something to test with sceptics, because the handsome spread of possibilities can be read as doubt.

Three warnings travel with the illustration. Every default on the first screen — place, measure, period, sort — will be read as the studio's advice and will anchor where the reader looks; there is no neutral default, only a declared one. Most readers will touch nothing, and on a phone, where hover does not exist, they will touch less; the finding lives on the surface or it does not live. And colour is never the only carrier of meaning: for the Vibe Map that is not a style preference but a Level A success criterion, met by labelling at the mark before a series colour is chosen.

## What proves a break works

If you must break a Fixed rule above — a legend instead of labels, a truncated bar, a rainbow, an error bar, a finding behind a hover — the test is the same: **give the display to the reader the rule protects, for the time the rule assumes, and ask them what it says.** Print the legend chart in greyscale and give it to someone with protanopia — the commonest red–green deficiency. Give the truncated bar to someone for five seconds and ask how big the difference is, then ask them to read the numbers, and notice that the two answers disagree. Give the error bar to a statistician and ask what it means for one person. Give the hover to someone on a phone. If the reading survives, log the break in the four-line record Chapter 16 describes (what rule, why, for whom, what proved it). If it does not, the rule was right.

- [ ] Linear, daily, absolute, fixed denominator, interval stated — or the departure justified
- [ ] Point values as numbers or tables; trends and comparisons as charts
- [ ] Two or three significant figures; frequencies for chance; no precision beyond the data
- [ ] Nothing crucial behind click, hover or tooltip; keyboard and phone reachable
- [ ] Every default written down and shown as a choice
- [ ] Overview or search chosen from the arriving question, not the mantra
- [ ] Small multiples for analysis; animation only for same-chart transitions or presentation
- [ ] No bare intervals or hard boundaries; outcome spread shown where individuals are the subject
- [ ] Every probability word has its number beside it
- [ ] Uncertainty displays tested with sceptical readers
- [ ] Tested with readers at PIAAC Level 1 and low graph literacy
- [ ] WCAG 2.2 AA met; two-part text alternative per chart; SVG charts carry a role and a name

## Looking back

Against Chapters 0, 1 and 2, the archived version 1, and the studio's Decision Book.

- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's truth 8 and Chapter 2's truth 12 (numeric uncertainty costs no trust) by supplying the *forms* — and **counterbalances** both with Markant: for the strongly opposed, shown uncertainty may be read as grounds for doubt. `[Counterbalance → Ch 2 §5]`
- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's truth 11 (rankings are screens, show intervals) with the caveat that a bare interval is itself misread; the interval must be shown as something countable.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's "scale by a denominator" rule and Chapter 2's "the denominator is the persuasive unit" with the stock/flow and 1-in-X findings: the choice of scale, baseline and denominator moves the reader whether or not it was meant to.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 2's finding that charts persuade through information, not aura: ornament behind the data costs time and buys memorability of the image, not of the finding; what gets the finding remembered is words and one highlight.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 2's "plain table for the hostile reader" with cognitive fit (tables for point values) and with the title-trust ordering: readers trust the data most and the title least while remembering the title. The plain table is the most-trusted component made the whole display.
- **Opens a thread** for Chapter 2's "name each reader": the schema a reader brings is part of what defines them; a form the officer reads fluently (a stacked area chart) is the one the public fails most.
- **Weakens** version 1's Chapter 1 (archived) in one detail: its "label at the mark" rule was tiered Fixed on a display audit, and this chapter finds it rests on no direct experiment and should carry that status. And a verification note: version 1 quoted Jachimowicz et al. for "individuals commonly perceive defaults as conveying an endorsement by the choice architect" as "read at source"; this chapter's research agent reported the sentence absent from the authors' PDF, and both the orchestrator's re-check and the independent verifier found it present in both the author PDF and the published text, citing McKenzie et al. 2006. The quotation stands; the episode is logged because a research agent's correction can itself be wrong, and the chapter's own first draft repeated the error before the verifier caught it.
- **Chapter 0.** The *Practice* kind earns its place again (Tufte; Ehrenberg; Few; the Analysis Function; Tse; the F-pattern), and a sub-kind is worth naming in the legend: *Practice backed by analogous Evidence* — a rule nobody has tested directly whose mechanism is understood and whose neighbours are well evidenced. "Label at the mark" is the type specimen.
- **The Decision Book.** Its Chapter 6 opens with the perceptual hierarchy as the finding "almost every chart rule" descends from, and this chapter tests it. **Agrees:** bars start at zero (and the Analysis Function checklist it cites already gives the better reason); label at the mark (with the evidential status now stated); never round beyond the data; alt text states the finding; show uncertainty on modelled values; four-series limits as voluntary Standard; creativity in layout not encoding. **Conflicts:** the reasons given for banning 3D ("volume is fifth") and constraining pies ("angle … two and three ranks down") rest on the conjectural half of the hierarchy — the rules survive on other grounds (3D adds nothing and obscures; pies fail on near-equal slices), and the Decision Book should cite those grounds instead. The rule "Use the highest encoding on the perceptual ranking" is Fixed there and should be Fixed only for precise comparison, Adaptable as a general form rule, given Davis et al. **Silent on:** the title's power to decide the reading; how much text a chart should carry; the truncated-axis finding that labels do not help; the choice of stock or flow; the forms of uncertainty display beyond "show it"; the first-glance evidence; interaction use rates; defaults as advice. These are logged for Carly as candidate additions to the Decision Book's data chapters. `[Counterbalance → Decision Book Ch 6]`

## Design system (Style Guide v1.5) — conflict log

**Agrees.** "Strong design must never make weak evidence look stronger than it is" is, in this chapter, the truncation finding, the stock/flow finding and the within-the-bar bias. The guide's provenance-on-every-chart rule and its evidence components are consistent with the two-part text alternative and the data-download rule. The guide's one-type-rule and status chips are not contradicted by anything here.

**Conflicts.** One, inherited from Chapter 2 and sharpened. The guide's joy-first register and its licence for expressive form sit against this chapter's first-glance evidence (appeal rewards simplicity and prototypicality), its ornament evidence (anything that touches the mark costs accuracy; anything behind the data costs time), and the Decision Book's own rule that in dashboards creativity lives in layout and hierarchy, not encodings. This is not a conflict in principle — the design system already says meaning never lives only in the decorative layer — but it is a conflict in practice every time a chart is styled, and the guide may want a line that says where on a dashboard the joy goes: words, hierarchy, register, imagery around the chart; never the mark, the axis or the colour that carries a value. `[Counterbalance → design system]`

**Silent on.** The title as the message-carrier (the guide governs headings, not chart titles); the amount and placement of text on a chart; the forms of uncertainty display; truncation; stock against flow; the public defaults for scale and risk; interaction use rates and the no-hover phone; defaults as advice. All candidates for the design system's dashboard section when it exists.
