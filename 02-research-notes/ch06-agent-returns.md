4a. Stirling (2007) "A general framework for analysing diversity in science, technology and society", J R Soc Interface 4(15): 707–719. Read at source in two copies: SPRU Electronic Working Paper 156 (https://www.sussex.ac.uk/webteam/gateway/file.php?name=sewp156&site=25) and the published open-access copy at PMC (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2373389/, PMID 17327202 — ID located via OpenAlex). All of the following string-verified in BOTH copies:
- "In short, diversity concepts employed across the full range of sciences mentioned above, display some combination of just three basic properties. - "Variety is the number of categories into which system elements are apportioned."
- "Balance is a function of the pattern of apportionment of elements across categories."
- "Disparity refers to the manner and degree in which the elements may be distinguished."
- On indices embedding a weighting: "it is difficult indeed to contemplate any single general index of diversity that could aggregate properties of variety, balance and disparity in a uniquely robust fashion" and "the logarithm base taken in Shannon and the value of the exponent taken in Simpson-Herfindahl can each have implications for the relative weightings assigned to variety and balance" (published copy uses an en-dash: "Simpson–Herfindahl").
- Abstract (via Crossref record, matches published): "It proposes a framework recognizing three necessary but individually insufficient properties of diversity. ... This allows the systematic exploration of diversity under different perspectives, including divergent conceptions of relevant attributes and contrasting weightings on different diversity properties."

UNEXPECTED THREADS (max 8)
====================================================================
U1. Gelman's Lac Qui Parle County sentence (item 5) — a county with two observations producing "an implausibly high estimate" — is the most compact real example in the whole haul of why a raw small-n figure should not be shown as is.
U2. ONS income QMI: about 40% of MSOAs have no survey respondents at all (item 3c). For such areas the only honest dashboard number is a modelled one, and the QMI says the CIs "should not be used to measure change" — a direct warning against trend arrows on modelled small-area figures.
U3. Business deaths are provisional for two years because of reactivations (item 6b). Any "closures" early-warning indicator built on register deaths is both late and revised downward later — a structural source of false alarms.
U4. Official rules hide small numbers for two different reasons — identification risk (NHS 1–7; Analysis Function 2/3/5) and precision (OPN base >10; ASHE CV bands). The same asterisk can mean "secret" or "unreliable"; a dashboard should say which.
U5. SAIPE states the shrinkage weight in one line: weight on the model = sampling variance / (sampling + lack-of-fit variance) (item 3e). This is the whole of "borrowing strength" in words a reader can follow.
U6. Eurostat defines a small area by precision, not size: "the sample size is not large enough for a direct design-based estimation of sufficient precision" (item 3b). A large borough can be a "small area" for a rare outcome.
U7. Scott's forestry failure surfaced only "in the second generation" (Plochmann via Scott, item 9a) — the hollow success is partly a timing problem: the proxy looks like success for one full cycle.
U8. Berg & Pattillo is IMF Staff Papers 46(2), 1999, not 45(1); KLR is 45(1), 1998. The KLR abstract carries the typo "This papers" at source.
