## 1. HM Treasury — The Green Book

### 1a. The Green Book 2026 (current)
**Citation:** HM Treasury (2026). *The Green Book: UK Government Guidance on Appraisal* (2026 edition). London: HM Treasury, 5 February 2026. 88 pp.
**URL read:** https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-green-book-appraisal-and-evaluation-in-central-government/the-green-book-2026 (HTML) and the publication page https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-green-book-appraisal-and-evaluation-in-central-government. PDF: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/698dbcd17da91680ad7f4308/The_Green_Book_2026.pdf
**Fetched:** 2026-10-05 (full HTML read).
**Quotations (verbatim; paragraph numbers as given in the PDF):**
- Appraisal (para 2.1): "Appraisal is the process of assessing the costs, benefits and risks of different options for achieving government objectives."
- Evaluation (para 1.10): "Evaluation involves assessing an intervention after it has been approved and implemented."
- Social value (para 2.5): "The social value of a proposal is the value it brings to residents of the United Kingdom. This is not limited simply to financial returns, but all the benefits, costs and risks that affect people's wellbeing." And: "Social value includes economic prosperity, justice, security, the climate, the environment, people's health and wellbeing, as well as distributional effects."
- Five Case Model (para 3.12): Strategic case — "This sets out the rationale and objectives for the intervention" (contents listed: case for change, theory of change, SMART objectives, business as usual, strategic fit). Economic case — "This sets out the longlist and shortlist of options for achieving the objectives set out in the strategic case. It identifies the preferred option that delivers the best value for money." Commercial — "This describes how the government will source the inputs required for delivering the preferred option." Financial — "This is concerned with the affordability of the proposal for the public bodies involved." Management — "This sets out the practical arrangements for implementation. It demonstrates that the preferred option can be delivered successfully." The management case includes a "Monitoring and evaluation plan".
- Theory of change (section 4): "A theory of change explains how the inputs and outputs that are defined in the proposal will lead to the desired outcomes." Objectives "must be specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-limited (SMART)."
- Evidence: "All four stages of appraisal must be informed by evidence. This can come from existing research or from new research that is commissioned specifically for the appraisal." And: "The evidence that supports appraisal is often incomplete or inconclusive. Appraisal also involves assumptions about the future, which may or may not materialise in reality."
- Monetise / quantify / qualitative ladder (paras 6.26–6.29): "Practitioners should aim to monetise social costs and social benefits where possible. If a social cost or social benefit cannot be monetised, then practitioners should aim to quantify it and express it in numerical terms." "A social cost or social benefit is qualitative if it cannot be feasibly expressed in numerical terms and can only be understood using words." "Other social costs and social benefits are unmonetisable. They can only be described in quantitative or qualitative terms…" (6.29) "It may be disproportionate to monetise or quantify every impact."
- BCR not decisive (para 2.30): "The UK government does not choose projects solely on the basis of which have the highest benefit-cost ratios…" (sentence continues to list "unmonetisable costs and benefits, risks and uncertainties" as also weighed).
- Switching values: "These are the values that a key assumption would need to change to, in order to make an option no longer value for money."
- Optimism bias (para 2.25): "Optimism bias is the proven tendency for appraisals to be over-optimistic about key assumptions." Adjustment: "increasing their estimates of social costs and project durations, and decreasing their estimates of social benefits."
- Distributional / place-based (para 2.28; Chapter 7 "Distributional analysis and place-based analysis"): "Practitioners should assess how different groups or places are affected by a proposal. In particular, distributional analysis should be used to illustrate the effects of a proposal on those groups with the protected characteristics." Distributional weighting and equivalisation are discussed (paras 7.49–7.50) but the fetch indicated weights are "considered where relevant", not mandated — treat that characterisation as the fetch tool's gloss, not a quotation.
- Longlist/shortlist/do-minimum/BAU: "A shortlist should generally include around five options." Do minimum: "This is the option that just achieves the proposal's objectives and goes no further in generating additional benefits." Business as usual: "This is the outcome that is expected if current arrangements continue and the proposal is not implemented."
- Proportionality (para 1.8): "Green Book guidance should be used proportionately. Business cases should contain sufficient detail to support sound decision making, but should not take up more time and resources than is necessary."
- Reach: "It is mandatory for UK government departments and arm's length bodies to follow the Green Book…" "Many local authorities in England stipulate in their local assurance frameworks that appraisal must be conducted in line with the Green Book." "Non-government organisations, such as charities, may be required to adhere to the Green Book when bidding for central government funding."
- **WELLBY / life satisfaction / "subjective": no mentions in the 2026 Green Book text** (word search on the full HTML).
**Establishes:** the reader's grammar — options not projects; social value not financial return; the monetise→quantify→qualitative ladder; a case is five cases; evidence must be present but is expected to be incomplete; BCR is not the decision.
**Strength:** official, mandatory guidance for central government; adopted by devolved governments and widely by local assurance frameworks.
**Role:** LOAD-BEARING.

## 2. HM Treasury — The Magenta Book

**Citation:** HM Treasury (2026). *The Magenta Book: Central Government guidance on evaluation*. May 2026 (replaces March 2020 edition; 160 pp.).
**URLs read:** https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5e96cab9d3bf7f412b2264b1/HMT_Magenta_Book.pdf and …/file/879438/HMT_Magenta_Book.pdf (both now serve the May 2026 text — confirmed by two independent reads); publication page https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-magenta-book.
**Fetched:** 2026-10-05. **2020 edition: NOT readable at source this session** (National Archives 403; mirrors found were the 2011 edition).
**Quotations (verbatim, page numbers of the 2026 PDF):**
- Definition (p. 8): "Evaluation is a systematic assessment of the design, implementation and outcomes of an intervention." It "involves understanding how an intervention is being or has been implemented and what effects it has, for whom and why. It identifies what can be improved and estimates its overall impacts and cost-effectiveness."
- Process (s.1.8.1, p. 28): "Process evaluations tend to examine activities involved in an intervention's implementation and the pathways by which the policy was delivered." Question: "What can be learned from how the intervention was delivered?"
- Impact (s.1.8.2, p. 28): "Impact evaluations focus on the changes caused by an intervention; measurable achievements which either are themselves, or contribute to, the objectives of the intervention." Question: "What difference has an intervention made?" Also: "How much of these outcomes can be attributed to the intervention?"
- Value for money (s.1.8.3, p. 29): "Value for money evaluation considers such issues, including whether the costs of the policy are outweighed by the benefits. It can also assess whether the intervention remains the most effective use of resources." Question: "Is this intervention a good use of resources?"
- Theory of change (s.2.2.1, p. 44): "A Theory of Change captures all of the detail listed above, including the theory of how the intervention is expected to work (setting out all the steps expected to be involved in achieving the desired outcomes), the assumptions made, the quality and strength of the evidence supporting them, and wider contextual factors." (p. 46) tools include "Theory of Change mapping, logic model, outcomes mapping and system mapping."
- Counterfactual (p. 26): "An evaluation is often needed to understand the additional impact of the intervention, by considering what would have happened in the absence of the policy ('the counterfactual') and assessing the extent to which any observed changes can be attributed to the intervention." (p. 56) "This unaffected group acts as a proxy for what would have happened to the affected group in the absence of the intervention and is commonly called the counterfactual."
- Quality criteria (s.1.9, pp. 30–31): Useful — "An evaluation is high quality when it is designed to meet the needs of the many stakeholders involved…and produces useful, usable outputs at the right point in time." Credible — "To be useful, evaluations need to be credible. This is often achieved through ensuring a degree of objectivity…Transparency is crucial for credibility." Robust — "An evaluation should be well designed, with an appropriate evaluation approach and methods, and well executed…The approach to establishing impact should involve rigorous comparison either in time, between groups or to alternative theories." Proportionate — "Proportionality is a key concept in evaluation. Not all interventions will require the same level of scrutiny or have the same learning needs."
- No single hierarchy (p. 54): "No one evaluation approach can appropriately evaluate all types of intervention – each design has its advantages and disadvantages." Theory-based (p. 59): "Theory-based impact evaluations draw conclusions about an intervention's impact through rigorous testing of whether the causal chains thought to bring about change are supported by sufficiently strong evidence."
- Monitoring vs evaluation (Fig 1.1, p. 18): Monitoring — "Data collection to answer the questions: Did we do what we said we would do? How are our success metrics changing over time?" Evaluation — "Research and analysis to answer the questions: Did the intervention work as expected? What was the impact, on who, and why? Was it cost-effective?" (p. 9) "a typical evaluation will rely heavily on monitoring data."
- Use (s.1.3.1, p. 16): "Fundamentally, learning is about good decision-making. Evaluation can provide evidence to inform decisions on whether to continue an intervention, how to improve it, how to minimise risk, or whether to stop and invest elsewhere." (pp. 21–22) "During implementation is when there is the greatest opportunity for the evaluation to influence decisions…" (p. 48) "it is important to be clear from the outset what these questions are, how the findings from them are expected to be used, by whom, and when."
- Word searches: "hierarchy", "Maryland", "dashboard" — no mentions.
**Establishes:** for the official reader, a dashboard is *monitoring* ("did we do what we said; how are metrics changing"), not *evaluation*; the leap to "what difference did it make" requires a counterfactual or a tested causal chain. The 2026 collection also now includes "Quality in policy impact evaluation (QPIE)" and "Transparency in Government Evaluation Research (TIGER)" — not read.
**Strength:** official guidance. **Role:** LOAD-BEARING.

## 3. Evidence hierarchies used by UK "what works" bodies

### 3a. Maryland Scientific Methods Scale — What Works Centre for Local Economic Growth
**Citation:** What Works Centre for Local Economic Growth (2016). *Guide to scoring evidence using the Maryland Scientific Methods Scale*. June 2016; and the Centre's resource page "The Maryland Scientific Methods Scale (SMS)".
**URLs read:** https://whatworksgrowth.org/resources/the-scientific-maryland-scale/ ; https://whatworksgrowth.org/wp-content/uploads/16-06-28_Scoring_Guide.pdf **Fetched:** 2026-10-05.
**Quotations (verbatim):**
- Level 1: "Either (a) a cross-sectional comparison of treated groups with untreated groups, or (b) a before-and-after comparison of treated group, without an untreated comparison group."
- Level 2: "Use of adequate control variables and either (a) a cross-sectional comparison of treated groups with untreated groups, or (b) a before-and-after comparison of treated group, without an untreated comparison group."
- Level 3: "Comparison of outcomes in treated group after an intervention, with outcomes in the treated group before the intervention, and a comparison group used to provide a counterfactual (e.g. difference in difference)."
- Level 4: "Quasi-randomness in treatment is exploited, so that it can be credibly held that treatment and control groups differ only in their exposure to the random allocation of treatment."
- Level 5: "Reserved for research designs that involve explicit randomisation into treatment and control groups, with Randomised Control Trials (RCTs) providing the definitive example."
- Scoring guide: "The credibility with which evaluations establish causality is the criterion on which this review assesses the literature." "We define SMS 3 methods to be the minimum standard for our reviews." "Robustness, as judged by the Maryland SMS, is the extent to which the method deals with the selection biases inherent to policy evaluations." "the ranking of individual studies is not an exact science and often involves a degree of judgement".
**Establishes:** a before/after on one group with no comparator — i.e. most dashboards — is Level 1 and is screened out of the Centre's reviews.
**Strength:** methodological standard of a government-funded What Works Centre (originally Sherman et al. 1997). **Role:** LOAD-BEARING.

### 3b. Nesta Standards of Evidence (Puttick & Ludlow 2013)
**Citation:** Puttick, R. & Ludlow, J. (2013). *Standards of Evidence: An Approach That Balances the Need for Evidence with Innovation*. London: Nesta, October 2013.
**URL read:** https://media.nesta.org.uk/documents/standards_of_evidence.pdf (full PDF). **Fetched:** 2026-10-05.
**Quotations (verbatim, the short level statements):**
- Level 1: "You can describe what you do and why it matters, logically, coherently and convincingly"
- Level 2: "You capture data that shows positive change, but you cannot confirm you caused this"
- Level 3: "You can demonstrate causality using a control or comparison group"
- Level 4: "You have one + independent replication evaluations that confirms these conclusions"
- Level 5: "You have manuals, systems and procedures to ensure consistent replication and positive impact"
- Longer Level 1 wording: "You can give an account of impact. You should be able to do this yourself, and draw upon existing data and research from other sources." Level 3 longer: "You can demonstrate that your intervention is causing the impact, by showing less impact amongst those who don't receive the product/service."
- Provenance: "There are numerous examples used in academia, such as the Maryland Scientific Methods Scale, or the Project Oracle Standards of Evidence used in youth services, which were the starting point for our own Standards."
- Caveat: "Even if a product, service or intervention successfully reaches Level 5 we do not see this as an end point. Evidence may only ever be partial or timebound, and needs to be seen as an ongoing exercise…"; purpose is to "enable innovation and evidence to co–exist".
**Establishes:** the funder's ladder in plain English — a dashboard that shows change is Level 2 ("you cannot confirm you caused this"); a theory of change alone is Level 1.
**Strength:** funder framework, widely copied (Project Oracle, Big Lottery, EIF). **Role:** LOAD-BEARING.

### 3c. Education Endowment Foundation padlock ratings
**Citation:** Education Endowment Foundation (2023). *Teaching and Learning / Early Years Toolkit Guide*, v3.0.0 (dated February 2023 on the document).
**URL read:** https://d2tic4wvo1iusb.cloudfront.net/production/documents/toolkit/toolkit_guide_2025_v3.0.0.pdf **Fetched:** 2026-10-05.
**Quotations (verbatim):**

**Strength:** official methodology of a What Works Centre. **Role:** SUPPORTING.

### 3d. Critique of hierarchies — Nutley, Powell & Davies 2013
**Citation:** Nutley, S., Powell, A. & Davies, H. (2013). *What counts as good evidence? Provocation paper for the Alliance for Useful Evidence*. London: Alliance for Useful Evidence / Nesta, February 2013.
**URL read:** https://media.nesta.org.uk/documents/What-Counts-as-Good-Evidence-WEB.pdf (full). **Fetched:** 2026-10-05.
**Quotations (verbatim):**
- (p. 11) "Hierarchies based on study design neglect too many important and relevant issues around evidence". (p. 12) "Hierarchies based on study design tend to underrate the value of good observational studies".
- (p. 15) matrix approach: "types of research design are differentially rated according to the research question being addressed".
- (p. 18) "What counts as good enough evidence depends on how we envisage it being used".
- (p. 20) policymakers "are interested in persuasive and actionable evidence but these qualities are not necessarily linked to particular study designs".
- (p. 24) "There is no simple answer to the question of what counts as good evidence. It depends on what we want to know, for what purposes, and in what contexts we envisage that evidence being used". (p. 25) recommends "a range of standards of evidence schemes—one size will not fit all the purposes and perspectives".
- **Box 6 (p. 21), "One insider's view of policymakers' hierarchy of evidence"**, attributed to Phil Davies, former Deputy Chief Social Researcher (2007): "1. Expert evidence (including consultants and think tanks). 2. Opinion–based evidence (including lobbyists/pressure groups). 3. Ideological evidence (party think tanks, manifestos). 4. Media evidence. 5. Internet evidence. 6. Lay evidence (constituents' or citizens' experiences). 7. Street evidence (urban myths, conventional wisdom). 8. Cabbies' evidence. 9. Research evidence."
**Establishes:** the counter-frame — hierarchies answer "does it work?" only; and the (half-joking, from a senior insider) inversion in which research sits last among what actually reaches a minister.
**Strength:** authoritative review by the leading UK research-use scholars; non-peer-reviewed provocation paper. **Role:** LOAD-BEARING (Box 6 is the chapter's best single image).

## 4. The evidence-informed-policy research

### 4a. Oliver, Innvar, Lorenc, Woodman & Thomas (2014)
**Citation:** Oliver, K., Innvar, S., Lorenc, T., Woodman, J. & Thomas, J. (2014). "A systematic review of barriers to and facilitators of the use of evidence by policymakers." *BMC Health Services Research* 14:2. https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6963-14-2
**URLs read:** https://bmchealthservres.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1472-6963-14-2 (full text) and the Europe PMC full-text XML https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC3909454/fullTextXML (for Table 1). **Fetched:** 2026-10-05.
**Quotations (verbatim):**
- Abstract: "The most frequently reported barriers to evidence uptake were poor access to good quality relevant research, and lack of timely research output. The most frequently reported facilitators were collaboration between researchers and policymakers, and improved relationships and skills."
- Scope: 145 studies, searches July 2000–September 2012; "the majority of which were or included semi-structured interviews (n = 79), or included a survey (n = 44)"; "Most studies reported perceptions or experiences of respondents (n = 109; n = 64 respectively), rather than documentary proof or observational results about the use of evidence in policy (n = 14; n = 11 respectively)."
- **Table 1 counts (number of studies):** Barriers — "Availability and access to research/improved dissemination" 63; "Clarity/relevance/reliability of research findings" 54; "Timing/opportunity" 42; "Policymaker research skills" 26; "Costs" 25. Facilitators — "Availability and access to research/improved dissemination" 65; "Collaboration" 49; "Clarity/relevance/reliability of research findings" 46; "Relationship with policymakers" 39; "Relationship with researchers/info staff" 37.
- "Contact, collaboration and relationships are a major facilitator of evidence use, reported in over two thirds of all studies." "Timing and opportunity was the most prominent barrier (n = 42) within this theme." Policymakers' "research skills and awareness (or lack of) reported as a barrier in 34 studies." "Political pressures, finances, and competing priorities were all discussed (n = 12)…"
- Conclusion: "Timely access to good quality and relevant research evidence, collaborations with policymakers and relationship- and skills-building with policymakers are reported to be the most important factors in influencing the use of evidence." "Despite this increase in research attention, there is still a remarkable dearth of reliable empirical evidence about the actual processes and impacts of research and other evidence use in policy." "Most studies in this review are descriptive."
- Self-report caveat: "These were all based on self-reports, however, so given the contentious nature of the topic combined with understandable fear of audit/performance monitoring these results may not be reliable."
**Establishes:** the three things readers say move them: access/timeliness, relevance/clarity, relationships. And that the field knows this mostly from what people *say*.
**Strength:** peer-reviewed systematic review (145 studies), update of Innvaer et al. 2002. **Role:** LOAD-BEARING.

### 4b. Paul Cairney — bounded rationality and persuasion
**Citation (open-access article in lieu of the 2016 book):** Cairney, P. & Oliver, K. (2017). "Evidence-based policymaking is not like evidence-based medicine, so how far should you go to bridge the divide between evidence and policy?" *Health Research Policy and Systems* 15:35. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12961-017-0192-x. Also Cairney, P. & Kwiatkowski, R. (2017). "How to communicate effectively with policymakers: combine insights from psychology and policy studies." *Palgrave Communications* 3:37. https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-017-0046-8
**URLs read:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12961-017-0192-x ; https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-017-0046-8 **Fetched:** 2026-10-05 (full texts). The 2016 Palgrave book itself was not read.
**Quotations (verbatim):**
- Cairney & Oliver: policymakers "employ two shortcuts – 'rational', pursuing clear goals and prioritising certain sources of information, and 'irrational', drawing on emotions, gut feelings, beliefs and habits to make decisions quickly."

### 1.1 Pandey, Manivannan, Nov, Satterthwaite & Bertini (2014) — LOAD-BEARING
**Citation:** Pandey, A.V., Manivannan, A., Nov, O., Satterthwaite, M., Bertini, E. (2014). "The Persuasive Power of Data Visualization." *IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics* 20(12), 2211–2220 (InfoVis 2014).
**URL read (author copy, full text):** https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5502f56fe4b0aa4bfbdae0a8/t/5da3d77afe2f655194b13312/1571018619600/persuasion-tvcg2014.pdf (found via Bertini's dossier https://enricobertini.github.io/). **Design:** Charts (bar/line) vs tables carrying identical data; three pre-tested topics chosen *not* to evoke extreme attitudes — Corporate Income Tax ("Lowering federal corporate income tax rate creates jobs"), Incarceration ("Incarceration does not reduce crime rates"), Video Games ("Violent video games do not contribute towards youth violence"). n = 720 (240 per topic), Amazon Mechanical Turk, US residents, ≥99% approval. **Quotations:**
- "Attitude change is measured as the change in user's self-reported attitude, i.e., post-treatment attitude minus pre-treatment attitude, when exposed to a persuasive message."
- NWP aggregate: "Chi-square test (χ2(2,323)= 20.7915, p = 0.000031), with 67.29% participants positively persuaded through charts and 42.68% through tables." Mean attitude-change difference, NWP aggregate: "Mean(charts - tables) = 0.381, 95% CI = [0.150, 0.611], p = 0.0008". - Per-topic NWP persuasion likelihood: CIT "66.67% for charts and 44.44% for tables"; INC "72% for charts and 41.21% for tables"; VG "63.63% for charts and 36.51% for tables" (p = 0.024, 0.035, 0.006). - **The moderator (reversal among the negatively polarized):** "Figure 6 shows a reverse trend in terms of likelihood, that is, the table conditions have a higher percentage of participants with positive change than the charts across all three topics." NP aggregate: "42.55% participants positively persuaded through charts and 78.72% through tables" (p = 0.00063); "Mean(charts - tables) = -1.063, 95% CI = [-1.637, -0.475], p = 0.001".
- Conclusion: "Our experiments suggest that the persuasive power of data visualization is dependent on the initial attitude of the participant." / "We found consistent results that charts lead to higher persuasion when participants do not possess a strong initial attitude about the topic. Tables, on the other hand, seem to outperform charts when the participants have strong initial attitude against the persuasive message; although these results are much more uncertain and need further validation."
- Limitations: "we do not know if the more persuasive effect of charts over tables, that we observed in the not polarized segment is mostly due to having more information available or just because the medium itself (its visual appearance) is more persuasive." / "Due to our experimental set up, where we purposefully aimed at having a higher number of participants in the NWP category, we have much higher uncertainty in NP."

**What it establishes:** For readers without a strong prior, a chart beats a table of the same numbers by roughly 20–27 percentage points in likelihood of moving toward the message; for readers already against the message the effect reverses (tables do better), though the NP cells are small (e.g. NP video-games n ≈ 12 vs 13). The effect is on self-reported attitude, measured minutes later.
**Strength:** Single MTurk study, n = 720, one-item measure, no delay, no behaviour. NP finding under-powered. No direct replication found; conceptually extended by Markant et al. (1.4 below). Treat the headline as robust-ish, the reversal as suggestive.

### 1.2 Tal & Wansink (2016) — SUPPORTING (with heavy caveat) — **ABSTRACT-ONLY**
**Citation:** Tal, A. & Wansink, B. (2016). "Blinded with science: Trivial graphs and formulas increase ad persuasiveness and belief in product efficacy." *Public Understanding of Science* 25(1), 117–125 (online 15 Oct 2014).
**URLs read:** SAGE abstract page https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963662514549688 ; SSRN working-paper page https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2500965 (full PDF exists there but was not readable through the tool). PubMed and ResearchGate blocked.
**Quotations (abstract):** "The appearance of being scientific can increase persuasiveness. Even trivial cues can create such an appearance of a scientific basis. In our studies, including simple elements, such as graphs (Studies 1–2) or a chemical formula (Study 3), increased belief in a medication's efficacy." SSRN version: "This appears to be due to the association of such elements with science, rather than increased comprehensibility, use of visuals, or recall," and Study 2 effects "are moderated by a person's belief in science."
**What it establishes (as claimed):** A redundant graph beside ad text raised belief that a (fictional) drug works; the mechanism offered is "looks scientific", not comprehension. The commonly cited Study 1 numbers (≈97% vs ≈68% believing the drug effective, n ≈ 61) I could NOT verify at source — do not print them without checking the PDF.
**Strength:** Small student samples; co-author Brian Wansink later had numerous papers retracted for data problems (this one, to my knowledge, has not been retracted — unverified here). The replication below did not reproduce the effect.

### 1.3 Dragicevic & Jansen (2018) — replication of Tal & Wansink — LOAD-BEARING as a corrective — **ABSTRACT/PROJECT-PAGE ONLY**
**Citation:** Dragicevic, P. & Jansen, Y. (2018). "Blinded with Science or Informed by Charts? A Replication Study." *IEEE TVCG* 24(1), 781–790 (VIS 2017). DOI 10.1109/TVCG.2017.2744298.
**URLs read:** authors' experimental-material page https://aviz.fr/blinded (abstract, N, links); LISN record https://www.lisn.upsaclay.fr/publications/blinded-with-science-or-informed-by-charts-a-replication-study-2/?lang=en (citation). HAL full text (hal-01580259) blocked by Anubis; IEEE, OSF, Semantic Scholar blocked.
**Quotations (authors' page):** "the chart's contribution to understanding was clearly larger than its contribution to persuasion"; they "were unable to replicate the original study's findings, as text with chart appeared to be no more persuasive – and sometimes less persuasive – than text alone"; "the effect may not be as robust as claimed and may need specific conditions to be reproduced." Total N = 623 across four experiments (two conditions each, chart vs no-chart); materials, data, R scripts open (CC-BY; OSF https://osf.io/zh3f4/, GitHub https://github.com/Yvonne-Jansen/blinded).
**What it establishes:** The "trivial graph = persuasion" result does not reproduce in four pre-registered-style online experiments; charts helped comprehension, not persuasion. Read together with Pandey: charts add persuasive force when they carry information to an open mind, not as decoration.
**Strength:** Four experiments, N = 623, open materials; stronger than the original.

### 1.4 Markant et al. (2023), "When do data visualizations persuade?" — SUPPORTING — **ABSTRACT-ONLY**
**Citation:** CHI 2023, DOI 10.1145/3544548.3581330; arXiv 2302.03776 (author list not rendered in my fetch — verify; Charlotte Ribarsky Center group, Dou lab).
**URL read:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03776
**Quotation:** "We find that strong prior attitudes are associated with smaller belief changes when presented with data that contradicts existing views, and that visual uncertainty representations may amplify this effect. Finally, even when participants' beliefs about correlations shifted their attitudes remained unchanged, highlighting the need for further research on whether data visualizations can drive longer-term changes in views and behavior."
**What it establishes:** Confirms the Pandey moderator with a different design (scatterplots, COVID-vaccine attitudes) and adds a sobering split: belief about the data can move while attitude does not.

## 2. NARRATIVE VERSUS STATISTICS

### 2.1 Small, Loewenstein & Slovic (2007) — LOAD-BEARING (but see 2.3)
**Citation:** "Sympathy and callousness: The impact of deliberative thought on donations to identifiable and statistical victims." *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 102(2), 143–153.
**URL read (author copy, full text):** https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/SympathyCallous.pdf
**Design/findings:** Study 1 (N = 121 students, real money earned then donated): identifiable victim (Rokia) no intervention **$2.83** (SD 2.10); identifiable + taught about the bias **$1.26**; statistical victims **$1.17** / **$1.36**. Study 3: identifiable alone **$2.38**, identifiable + statistics **$1.43**, statistics only **$1.14**. Study 4 (N = 165): priming "calculate" vs "feel": identifiable/feel $2.34, identifiable/calculate $1.45; statistical/feel $1.54, statistical/calculate $1.19.
**Quotations:**
- Abstract: "teaching or priming people to recognize the discrepancy in giving toward identifiable and statistical victims has perverse effects: individuals give less to identifiable victims but do not increase giving to statistical victims, resulting in an overall reduction in caring and giving."
- On adding statistics to the story: "statistical information dampens the inclination to give to an identifiable victim. This result is consistent with the tendency to give less to an identifiable victim after learning about the discrepancy in giving."
- General Discussion: "In sum, our results demonstrate that sympathy for identifiable victims diminishes with deliberative thought, but remains consistently low for statistical victims. This pattern holds with various manipulations of deliberative thought, including explicit debiasing interventions, providing statistics, and priming an analytic mindset."
**What it establishes (as originally claimed):** Putting numbers next to a story reduces the story's pull without raising the pull of the numbers — the "drop in the bucket" mechanism relevant to dashboards that juxtapose case studies and aggregates.
**Strength:** Small student samples, real donations; the field's canonical demonstration — now contested (2.3).

### 2.2 Jenni & Loewenstein (1997) — SUPPORTING
**Citation:** "Explaining the Identifiable Victim Effect." *Journal of Risk and Uncertainty* 14(3), 235–257.
**URL read (author copy, full text):** https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/identifiableVictim.PDF
**Design:** Two scenario studies (Study 1 n = 127 students and mall visitors; Study 2 121+ airport visitors) testing four explanations: vividness; certainty; proportion of reference group saved; ex post vs ex ante.
**Quotation:** the identifiable victim effect "per se, may be wholly attributable to" the proportion-of-reference-group effect — 25 lives that are all of a small at-risk group beat 25 lives out of 50,000. Vividness, certainty and ex-post timing showed no reliable effects. Framed by Schelling (1968) on identified vs statistical lives.
**What it establishes:** The persuasive unit is "what fraction of the group you can save" — a denominator effect dashboards manipulate every time they choose a base.

### 2.3 Lee & Feeley (2016) meta-analysis — SUPPORTING — **ABSTRACT-ONLY**
**Citation:** Lee, S. & Feeley, T.H. (2016). "The identifiable victim effect: a meta-analytic review." *Social Influence* 11(3), 199–215.
**URL read:** https://pure.skku.edu/en/publications/the-identifiable-victim-effect-a-meta-analytic-review/ (T&F PDF 403).
**Quotation:** Across 41 studies "an overall significant yet modest IVE (r = .05)"; more reliable with single victims and "a photographed child suffering from poverty, bearing little responsibility for the need, and/or associated with monetary requests."
**What it establishes:** The pooled effect is tiny; it lives in specific conditions (one child, photo, poverty, money ask).

### 2.4 Maier, Wong & Feldman (2023) pre-registered replication of Small et al. — LOAD-BEARING corrective
**Citation:** "Revisiting and Rethinking the Identifiable Victim Effect: Replication and Extension of Small, Loewenstein, and Slovic (2007)." *Collabra: Psychology* 9(1), 90203. (The fetch tool mis-labelled the journal once; the URL is Collabra.)
**URL read (full text):** https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/9/1/90203/199223/Revisiting-and-Rethinking-the-Identifiable-Victim
**Quotations (abstract):** "Our reanalysis of a meta-analysis on the effect by Lee and Freely (2016) [sic] using robust Bayesian meta-analysis suggested publication bias in the literature and the need to revisit the phenomenon." / "We found no empirical support for the identifiable victim effect (ηp2 = .000, 95% CI [.000, .003]) and subsequently no support for debiasing such a phenomenon (ηp2 = .001, 95% CI[.000, .012]). These findings suggest that the identifiable victim may be better framed in terms of 'scope-insensitivity'. In other words, rather than providing more to a single identified victim, participants seem to be insensitive to the number of victims affected. However, our study involved only hypothetical donations rather than a real-effort real-donation paradigm as in Small et al. (2007)." N = 1,004 MTurk; statistics-alongside-victim: F(2,499) = 0.61, p = .541, BF₀₁ = 25.03.
**What it establishes:** The strong version ("story beats number") is not safe. The surviving claim is scope-insensitivity: people don't scale response with magnitude — which is itself an argument that big numbers on dashboards do less work than their authors think.
**Strength:** Pre-registered, n = 1,004, but hypothetical money; authors themselves call for real-money replications.

### 2.5 Zebregs, van den Putte, Neijens & de Graaf (2015) meta-analysis, statistical vs narrative evidence — LOAD-BEARING
**Citation:** *Health Communication* 30(3), 282–289. DOI 10.1080/10410236.2013.842528.
**URL read (publisher PDF via UvA repository, full text):** https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/2680645/167737_497142.pdf
**Numbers:** k = 15; participants 1,546 (beliefs), 1,850 (attitude), 1,338 (intention). Beliefs ES = −0.16, 95% CI [−0.29, −0.03], p = .014 (negative = statistical stronger); attitude ES = −0.11, CI [−0.23, 0.00], p = .058; intention ES = 0.10, CI [0.00, 0.20], p = .061.
**Quotations:** "Statistical evidence was found to have a stronger influence than narrative evidence on beliefs and attitude, whereas narrative evidence had a stronger influence on intention." / "Statistical evidence, beliefs, and attitude all relate primarily to cognitive responses, whereas both narrative evidence and intention relate more specifically to affective responses."
**What it establishes:** The split the chapter needs: numbers move what people *believe*; stories move what they *intend to do* — and two of the three pooled effects are borderline, so neither side wins big.

### 2.6 Narrative Policy Framework — Jones & McBeth (2010) — LOAD-BEARING (theory)
**Citation:** "A Narrative Policy Framework: Clear Enough to Be Wrong?" *Policy Studies Journal* 38(2), 329–353.
**URL read (full text copy):** https://scispace.com/pdf/a-narrative-policy-framework-clear-enough-to-be-wrong-lk3lbpopiw.pdf
**Quotations:** A policy narrative is "a story with a temporal sequence of events unfolding in a plot that is populated by dramatic moments, symbols, and archetypal characters that culminates in a moral to the story" (p. 329). Micro-level: "Narratives are theorized to persuade to the extent they comport with that individual's understanding of the world or life experience" (p. 343); "The plausibility of a story is conditioned by the extent to which individuals trust the source of the narrative" (p. 343); on breach: "Stories that change the way we view the world do violence to the norm, breach banality, and rend our expectations" (p. 342). Meso-level Hypotheses 5–6 (p. 345): losers "will use narrative elements to expand the policy issue to increase the size of their coalition"; winners "will use narrative elements to contain the policy issue to maintain the coalitional status quo." Narratives are "a seemingly universal device for individuals to sharpen certain elements of reality while leveling others" (p. 330).
**Note:** Jones & McBeth do *not* claim narrative trumps facts; the "homo narrans" assumption appears in later NPF statements.

## 3. MAPS AND RANKINGS AS PERSUADERS

### 3.1 Espeland & Sauder (2007) — LOAD-BEARING
**Citation:** "Rankings and Reactivity: How Public Measures Recreate Social Worlds." *American Journal of Sociology* 113(1), 1–40.
**URL read (full text copy):** https://www.academia.edu/86804076/Rankings_and_Reactivity_How_Public_Measures_Recreate_Social_Worlds
**Basis:** 136 interviews (two waves, N = 66 and N = 70) at ~70 US law schools.
**Quotations:** Reactivity: "Individuals alter their behavior in reaction to being evaluated, observed, or measured." Self-fulfilling prophecy: "Processes by which reactions to social measures confirm the expectations or predictions that are embedded in measures or which increase the validity of the measure by encouraging behavior that conforms to it." Commensuration works "mainly by transforming cognition; it changes the locus and form of attention, both creating and obscuring relations among entities." Rankings "reduce the disparate and variable characteristics that might influence the quality of a law school to a single number," yielding "decontextualized, depersonalized numbers that are highly portable." Dean: "[Rankings] create inequality among schools that are rather hard to distinguish. They create artificial lines that then have the danger of becoming real."
**What it establishes:** A ranking does not describe a field; it re-makes it, and readers scrutinise tiny rank differences "as if it had meaning."

### 3.2 Marshall, Shekelle, Leatherman & Brook (2000) — LOAD-BEARING — **ABSTRACT-ONLY**
**Citation:** "The Public Release of Performance Data: What Do We Expect to Gain? A Review of the Evidence." *JAMA* 283(14), 1866–1874.
**URL read:** https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/192586
**Quotations:** "Consumers and purchasers rarely search out the information and do not understand or trust it; it has a small, although increasing, impact on their decision making." / "Hospitals appear to be most responsive to the data." / "use of the information by provider organizations for quality improvement may be the most productive area for further research."

### 3.3 Metcalfe et al. (2018) Cochrane review — LOAD-BEARING — plain-language summary read
**Citation:** "Impact of public release of performance data on the behaviour of healthcare consumers and providers." *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* CD004538.pub3.
**URL read:** https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD004538_can-public-release-performance-data-health-care-influence-behaviour-consumers-healthcare-providers (Cochrane Library full text robots-blocked).
**Quotations:** "12 studies that analysed data from more than 7570 providers"; "low-certainty evidence that public release of performance data may make little or no difference" to patients' choice of provider; it "may slightly improve some patient outcomes"; "the existing evidence base is inadequate to directly inform policy and practice."
**What it establishes:** Two decades after Marshall, the verdict on league tables as a *consumer* persuasion device is unchanged: little effect on choice; the effect, where it exists, is on the ranked, not the readers.

### 3.4 The "Dr Foster effect" — NOT VERIFIED. I found no evaluated study isolating the Dr Foster Hospital Guide's effect on patient choice or trust behaviour; searches returned HSMR methodology disputes and trust-level case reports (e.g. PMC6519425) rather than impact evaluations. Do not assert a measured "Dr Foster effect" without a source; Marshall 2000 and Cochrane 2018 are the defensible stand-ins.

### 3.5 Hastings & Weinstein (2008) — LOAD-BEARING (rank-ordered presentation changing real decisions)
**Citation:** "Information, School Choice, and Academic Achievement: Evidence from Two Experiments." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 123(4), 1373–1414.
**URL read (NBER WP 13623, full text):** https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w13623/w13623.pdf ; abstract page https://www.nber.org/papers/w13623
**Design:** Charlotte-Mecklenburg; field experiment, 6,328 non-NCLB students in 46 school zones plus 10,134 NCLB students; treated families got a one-page sheet "specialized for each child" listing their choice set with scores, where "The schools in each information table were sorted by test score" — versus the existing three-page official spreadsheet "sorted alphabetically by name."
**Quotations:** "We find that receiving information significantly increases the fraction of parents choosing higher-performing schools." Effects: 7 percentage-point rise in choosing a non-guaranteed school (31% baseline); 0.10 SD rise in chosen-school average score; attending a school 1 SD higher raised own scores by 0.409 SD. Mechanism: "If lower-income families face higher costs of gathering and interpreting statistics on academic achievement, they may choose schools based on easier-to-determine characteristics such as proximity, instead of school test scores."
**What it establishes:** The same public numbers, re-sorted into a ranking on one page, changed where thousands of families sent their children. This is the cleanest "presentation changes decisions" field result I found.

### 3.6 Engel, Rodden & Tabellini (2022) — choropleth maps steer lay perception — LOAD-BEARING
**Citation:** "Policies to influence perceptions about COVID-19 risk: The case of maps." *Science Advances* 8(11), eabm5106.
**URL read (PMC full text):** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8932671
**Design:** Representative survey, Georgia USA, N = 1,751, June 12–19 2020; random assignment to a county map of case *counts* vs case *rates per 100,000*.
**Quotations:** "Respondents who saw case rate maps were less likely to perceive COVID as mostly an urban problem and reported higher levels of concerns about the virus." 53.2% vs 44.5% saw COVID as "mostly urban" (p < 0.001); worry up ~0.5–0.6 on a 10-point scale; policy-support effect about half the size of partisanship effects; no significant effect on self-reported behaviour. "Better maps might not only convey valuable information to rural residents about the risks of becoming infected but can also help combat geographic and partisan polarization about virus response."

### 3.7 Thorpe et al. (2021) — maps did not improve knowledge or intentions — SUPPORTING
**Citation:** "Exposure to Common Geographic COVID-19 Prevalence Maps and Public Knowledge, Risk Perceptions, and Behavioral Intentions." *JAMA Network Open*, 6 Jan 2021.
**URL read (PMC):** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7788466
**Design:** Online survey, 2,676 US adults, May 2020; six real published maps (CDC, KFF, UC Davis, NYT, two Johns Hopkins) vs no-map control.
**Quotations:** Total-case maps gave better knowledge than per-capita maps (0.60 vs 0.46); map viewers were *more* optimistic the pandemic would improve; adherence intentions ~86/100 in all groups. "Simply providing maps with COVID-19 case information was not necessarily associated with improved public knowledge, risk perception, or reported intent."
**Together with 3.6:** The normalisation choice (count vs rate) moves perception; the map as such does not reliably move knowledge or behaviour.

## 4. UNCERTAINTY AND PERSUASION

### 4.1 van der Bles, van der Linden, Freeman, Mitchell, Galvao, Zaval & Spiegelhalter (2019) — LOAD-BEARING
**Citation:** "Communicating uncertainty about facts, numbers and science." *Royal Society Open Science* 6: 181870.
**URL read (full text PDF mirror):** https://tethys.pnnl.gov/sites/default/files/publications/rsos.181870.pdf (RS site 403).
**Quotations:** Direct uncertainty is "about the fact, number or scientific hypothesis… communicated either in absolute quantitative terms, say a probability distribution or confidence interval, or expressed relative to alternatives"; indirect uncertainty is "in terms of the quality of the underlying knowledge." On trust: "research into how the communication of scientific uncertainty impacts trust and credibility is very sparse"; range estimates signalled "honesty and competence for sizeable portions of participants across studies (25–49%) but also dishonesty and incompetence for non-negligible minorities (8–20%)." Own experiments: "introducing uncertainty about a range of numbers (e.g. the number of tigers in India, unemployment in the UK, global temperature rise) reduces trust in the number but not necessarily in the messenger," with "much greater reduction in trust in the number for verbal/less precise expressions of uncertainty (e.g. 'somewhat higher or lower') than for numerical expressions (e.g. point estimate with range)." Overall: "while there is some evidence that communicating epistemic uncertainty does not necessarily affect audiences negatively, impact can vary between individuals and communication formats."

### 4.2 Kerr et al. (2023) — international extension — SUPPORTING (the PNAS 2020 paper itself was captcha-blocked on three attempts)
**Citation:** "The effects of communicating uncertainty around statistics, on public trust: an international study." *Royal Society Open Science* 10(11): 230604.
**URL read (PMC full text):** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10663791/
**Design:** Study 1 N = 10,519 across 12 countries; Study 2 N = 2,309 UK.
**Quotations:** numeric ranges caused only small decreases in number trustworthiness and no significant effect on source trustworthiness; "Explicitly acknowledging that uncertainty exists, without any quantification, consistently lowered the perceived trustworthiness" of numbers and sometimes sources; results "broadly confirms the findings of our previous work in other contexts."
**What 4.1–4.2 establish:** A numeric range costs little credibility; a vague verbal hedge costs more. Showing uncertainty does not destroy persuasive force if it is quantified. (van der Bles et al. 2020 PNAS, 5 experiments incl. a BBC News field test, is the canonical cite — unverified here.)

### 4.3 Hullman (2020) — why authors leave uncertainty off — LOAD-BEARING for the chapter's "producer" angle
**Citation:** "Why Authors Don't Visualize Uncertainty." *IEEE TVCG* 26(1), 130–139.
**URL read (author copy, full text):** https://users.eecs.northwestern.edu/~jhullman/Value_of_Uncertainty_Vis_CR.pdf
**Sample:** survey n = 90 visualization authors; 13 interviews with prominent designers/journalists.
**Quotations:** "Clear presentation of uncertainty is an exception rather than rule in media articles, data-driven reports, and consumer applications." Reasons: "not wanting to confuse or overwhelm viewers (53/85; 62%)"; "not having access to uncertainty information (40/85; 47%)"; "not knowing how to calculate uncertainty (22/85; 26%)"; "not wanting to make data seem questionable (15/85; 17%)". Rhetorical model: "in communicative visualization, visualizations are representations of a message or signal that an author wishes to convey"; "uncertainty functions as a question or seam in the message that threatens the validity of the signal"; "a visualization author engages in a rhetorical process involving editorial decisions to omit, emphasize, and otherwise transform results to guide an audience toward intended interpretations."

### 4.4 Joslyn & LeClerc (2012) — decision-makers with uncertainty — SUPPORTING — **ABSTRACT-ONLY**

**URL read:** https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ968865
**Quotations:** three experiments, road-salting decisions from temperature forecasts; "uncertainty information improved decision quality overall and increased trust in the forecast"; combining advice with uncertainty "resulted in the best performance overall."

### 5.3 Gateway Belief Model — the evaluated "killer stat" — LOAD-BEARING
**Citation:** van der Linden, S., Leiserowitz, A., Maibach, E. (2019). "The gateway belief model: A large-scale replication." *Journal of Environmental Psychology* 62, 49–58.

### 5.4 The £350m case — anchoring by one number — COLOUR, measured not evaluated

## THREADS PULLED
1. **Prior attitude is the master moderator.** Pandey (charts help the open-minded, tables the hostile), Markant (strong priors shrink belief change; attitude does not follow belief), Kahan (numeracy amplifies motivated reading). A dashboard persuades the persuadable.
2. **Charts persuade through information, not aura.** Tal & Wansink's "trivial graph" effect failed to replicate (Dragicevic & Jansen); charts aided understanding instead. Pandey's own open question (information vs medium) leans the same way.
3. **The story/number dichotomy has collapsed into two smaller claims.** Zebregs: numbers move beliefs, stories move intentions, both weakly. The identifiable-victim effect is r = .05 pooled and failed a pre-registered replication; what survives is scope-insensitivity — people do not scale with magnitude.
4. **Rankings act on the ranked, not the reader.** Marshall 2000 → Cochrane 2018: public performance data barely shifts consumer choice; Espeland & Sauder: it reorganises the institutions being ranked. Exception: when ranked information is made cheap and personal (Hastings & Weinstein), lay readers do use it.
5. **Normalisation is the persuasive lever in maps.** Count vs rate maps changed who thought COVID was "urban" by 9 points (Engel et al.); maps as such did not raise knowledge or intentions (Thorpe et al.).
6. **Quantified uncertainty is cheap; vague uncertainty is expensive.** Numeric ranges barely dent trust in number or source (van der Bles; Kerr, N > 10,000); verbal hedges do. Authors omit uncertainty anyway, mostly fearing confusion (62%), and Hullman's model names the omission as rhetoric.
7. **Field effects are real but small.** 2% (Allcott), 7 points (Hastings & Weinstein), d ≈ 0.09 on policy support from the best-tested single statistic (GBM). Nothing in the field literature supports the "one chart changed everything" register.

## WHAT SURPRISED ME

### 1.2 Markusen (2013) — "Fuzzy concepts, proxy data" — CENTRAL
- **Citation:** Markusen, A. (2013). "Fuzzy concepts, proxy data: why indicators would not track creative placemaking success." *International Journal of Urban Sciences*, 17(3), 291–303. DOI 10.1080/12265934.2013.836291.
- **URLs read:** citation/abstract via https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/rjusxx/v17y2013i3p291-303.html (publisher page returned 403). Full text read as the **open copy**: Markusen, A., guest post "Fuzzy Concepts, Proxy Data: Why Indicators Won't Track Creative Placemaking Success," *Createquity*, November 2012, https://createquity.com/2012/11/fuzzy-concepts-proxy-data-why-indicators-wont-track-creative-placemaking-success/ . - **Abstract (journal, paraphrased by IDEAS page):** "fuzzy concepts and a reliance on data external to the funded projects" undermine evaluation; terms like vitality and vibrancy lack consistent definitions; available data rarely align with grantees' target areas; generic indicators risk alienating applicants and favouring already high-performing places; funders should adopt program-specific criteria co-designed with grantees, peer learning networks and technical support.
- **Quotes (Createquity open copy):**
  - "Terms like 'vitality,' 'vibrancy,' and 'livability' are great examples of fuzzy concepts."
  - "A fuzzy concept is one that means different things to different people, but flourishes precisely because of its imprecision."
  - On ArtPlace's own definition — "'we define vibrancy as places with an unusual scale and intensity of specific kinds of human interaction.' Pretty vague and….vibrancy are places?"
  - "Probably no worthy concept, as quality of life is, has been the subject of so many disappointing and conflicting measurement exercises."
  - "One person's quality of life can be inimical to others'. Take the young live music scene in cities: youth magnet, older resident nightmare." *(note for the author: Markusen's own example of contested 'vibrancy' is a music scene — use with care given the chapter's no-nightlife brief, but it is her illustration, not mine)*
  - Proxy data: "efforts based on fuzzy concepts and indicators designed to rely on data external to the funded projects are bound to disappoint"; "most good secondary data series are not available at spatial scales corresponding to grantees' target places"; "we could not make reliable estimates of artist populations at anything near a neighborhood scale."
  - On indicators rewarding the already-successful: "funders may begin favoring grants to places that already look good on the indicators."
  - On the reader's reaction: "It could be baffling, even angering, to be served up a book of cooked indicators with very little fit to one's project."
  - On individual indicators: "Home ownership does not insulate you against displacement, because as property values rise, property taxes do as well, driving out renters and homeowners alike on fixed or lower incomes." / "Think of the large areas of new non-mixed use upscale high-rise condos where the mostly young professional people living there commute daily to jobs and nightly to bars and cafes outside the neighborhood.   - Attribution: "In any particular place, changes in the proposed indicators will not be attributable to the creative placemaking intervention alone."
  - Position: "I embrace the project of evaluation, but am skeptical of relying on indicators for this purpose." She urges funders to "commit to real evaluation" with grantee-specific criteria, 5–7 year horizons, annual cohort convenings and a technical-assistance consortium modelled on the Manufacturing Extension Partnership.
- **Establishes:** the strongest articulated case that place-level secondary indicators cannot evidence an intervention — because the concept is fuzzy, the data are proxies at the wrong geography, the direction of some indicators (property values) is normatively contested, and the indicator rewards places that were already winning. Strength: peer-reviewed, by the field's founding author. **LB.**

## 2. Cultural value frameworks

### 2.1 DCMS Culture and Heritage Capital (2021–2025)

## THREADS PULLED

1. **Two routes out of "cost".** Faced with being read as a cost line, sectors took one of two routes: (a) *monetise the benefit* with Green Book-sanctioned wellbeing/WTP methods (Fujiwara → HACT → Fields in Trust → Sport England → DCMS CHC), or (b) *refuse the single number and define the construct instead* (Urban Institute 2006, Markusen 2013, Impact & Insight, Centre for Cultural Value, BOP's "holy grail"). The first route wins the press release; the second wins the methods appendix. A dashboard author must know which reader they are facing.

2. **Markusen's three failure modes are general, not arts-specific.** Fuzzy concept; proxy data at the wrong geography; indicators that reward already-successful places and punish the places an intervention is meant to help. Every place-indicator set reviewed here (Centre for Cities, ONS, TPI) is built from secondary data at LA/PUA scale and would fail her test as *evidence of an intervention* while remaining perfectly good as *description of a place*. The distinction — descriptive dashboard vs evaluative claim — is the pivot of the chapter.

3. **The economic-development reader's native frame is residual arithmetic.** Viability (GDV − costs − land − profit), Centre for Cities' 17 indicators and ONS's 38 contain no cultural or public-life measure. Non-market value enters that reader's decision only when (i) it moves GDV (rents, prices, footfall) or (ii) it is imposed as a policy cost (Social Value Act consideration → TOMs line item). Fields in Trust and Sport England therefore translated into the reader's currency (£, NHS savings, "£4.20 per £1"); Gehl translated into the reader's *habit* (counts, like traffic counts).

4. **Property value is the contested indicator.** It is Markusen's prime example of a measure that reads as success to the investor and as displacement to the resident; What Works Growth's public realm briefing and sport/culture review both find property price the *most* likely measurable effect. The one number most likely to move an inward-investment reader is the one most likely to be read as harm by a community reader.

5. **Headline-to-caveat decay.** Every valuation report carries the hedges (not cashable; not WTP; correlation; conservative by assumption); every downstream release drops them. Sport England's 2025 release has no caveat; the 2020 report has two pages. Corry: "extraordinarily high values." Schoemaker: 13× spread for the same activity across studies. A dashboard inherits the caveats whether or not it displays them.

6. **The debate ended by abandonment, not resolution.** ArtPlace dropped vibrancy indicators within two years and moved to "community-defined success measures." The UK state meanwhile *institutionalised* monetisation (CHC, 2021–25). Both are responses to the same critique.

## 2. Critiques of impact measurement

### 2.1 Ebrahim & Rangan (2014)
- **Citation:** Alnoor Ebrahim and V. Kasturi Rangan, "What Impact? A Framework for Measuring the Scale and Scope of Social Performance," *California Management Review* 56(3), Spring 2014, 118–141.
- **URL read:** https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/CMR5603_07_Ebrahim_e3316477-8965-4287-be95-04642982b638.pdf (at source, full open PDF on HBS site)
- **Quotations:**
  - "Not all organizations should measure their long-term impact, defined as lasting changes in the lives of people and their societies. Rather, some organizations would be better off measuring shorter-term outputs or individual outcomes." (p. 118)
  - "This attention to impact, following on the heels of accountability, is mainly driven by funders who want to know whether their funds are making a difference or might be better spent elsewhere." (p. 118)
  - "Every organization should at least measure and report on its activities and outputs, as these results are largely within its control." (p. 122)
  - "It is at this level—where the funder is able to oversee hundreds of operating organizations—that it is possible to measure societal impacts." (p. 129)
  - "Impacts on systemic societal problems are unlikely to be achieved by organizations acting alone; it thus makes more sense for funders rather than operating organizations to take on the challenge of measuring those impacts." (p. 135)
- **Establishes:** A contingency argument: the appropriate measurement level depends on operational strategy and theory of change; ecosystem-level impact belongs to funders/coalitions, not single organisations. Directly supports the chapter's claim that a shared, place- or sector-level evidence base is the right locus for "impact" claims.
- **Strength:** Peer-reviewed, heavily cited, read in full. **Load-bearing.**

### 2.2 Toby Lowe (2013), "The paradox of outcomes"
- **Citation:** Toby Lowe, "New development: The paradox of outcomes—the more we measure, the less we understand," *Public Money & Management* 33(3), 2013, 213–216.
- **URL read:** https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/pubmmg/v33y2013i3p213-216.html (**abstract-only**; T&F and ResearchGate blocked)
- **Quotation (abstract):** "The use of 'outcomes' as a concept to measure the effectiveness of social policy interventions is inherently flawed and creates unwelcome paradoxes. ### 6.1 Smith-Merry (2020)

## THREADS PULLED

1. **The grammar is shared; the weight is not.** IMP's five dimensions sit under IRIS+, under the PBII reporting framework, and under Better Society Capital's reporting. But the first component in the PBII framework is "Overall Impact Goal and Narrative", and Nesta's Level 1 is "You can describe what you do and why it matters, logically, coherently and convincingly." Investor and funder ladders start with an account, not a number.
2. **Funders require more than they use.** NPC 2012/2013: measurement is funder-driven; 38% of funders use impact data to select grantees; <42% aggregate. CEP 2021: ~40% of grantees never discuss their report with anyone at the funder. Esmée and TNLCF have responded by making the conversation the unit of reporting.
3. **Ebrahim & Rangan hand the "impact" job to the collective.** Single organisations should measure outputs/outcomes; systemic impact is for funders and ecosystems. This is the strongest academic warrant for a shared, sector- or place-level evidence base rather than every venue proving its own impact.
4. **Lowe's paradox and gaming.** Outcomes data distorts practice; practitioners admit manipulation; the alternative is learning capacity, which maps onto what Esmée/TNLCF now ask for.
5. **Coalitions learn on their own terms (Sabatier via Cairney).** A shared evidence base changes secondary beliefs across coalitions only in professional forums where both sides accept the data — relevant to how a sector's "common narrative" becomes credible to officials, who (IfG) rely on relationships and ministerial tone.
6. **Lived experience is admitted, then re-filed.** TNLCF and ACE name it; Scottish Government cases (Hill O'Connor & Bennett) show it being converted into "qualitative data to supplement official evidence" or "user-testing". Smith-Merry's fix: validity from context, not method. Geddes: Parliament wants it but has not defined its purpose.
7. **Recognition as validation is defensible only as credibility, and only as dialogue.** Lincoln & Guba call member checking "the most crucial technique for establishing credibility"; Birt et al. show it is often "merely a nod"; Mahajan et al. show community data earns standing when standards and the route to decision are co-agreed first.

## WHAT SURPRISED YOU
- The IMP "What" dimension already asks "how important the outcome is to the people and communities experiencing it" — the investor consensus text contains the lived-experience test that the policy literature says is missing.
- NPC's 2013 funder survey: 88–89% of funders believe measurement makes charities and funders more effective, yet only 38% use the data to choose grantees. The gap between belief and use is the finding.
- Nesta's Level 1 is a convincing *description*. The most "rigour-first" UK funder ladder legitimises a well-told account as evidence.
- The disability slogan "Nothing About Us Without Us" was imported into English-language activism from Eastern European activists via South Africa in 1993 — not coined in the UK/US.
- Arts Council England's own equality analysis describes a *portfolio balancing* stage after scoring: geography and artform can override an individual score. The decision is made twice.
- The academic literature on how foundation officers actually decide (relationships vs reports) is much thinner than the practitioner literature suggests; most of what circulates is CEP survey data and advocacy.
