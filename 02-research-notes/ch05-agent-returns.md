1e. Weighting (strong; load-bearing). Section 1.6, p. 31 — full paragraph, cross-checked:
"Most composite indicators rely on equal weighting (EW), i.e. all variables are given the same weight. This essentially implies that all variables are "worth" the same in the composite, but it could also disguise the absence of a statistical or an empirical basis, e.g. when there is insufficient knowledge of causal relationships or a lack of consensus on the alternative. In any case, equal weighting does not mean "no weights", but implicitly implies that the weights are equal. Moreover, if variables are grouped into dimensions and those are further aggregated into the composite, then applying equal weighting to the variables may imply an unequal weighting of the dimension (the dimensions grouping the larger number of variables will have higher weight). This could result in an unbalanced structure in the composite index."

1f. Aggregation and compensability — Part I passage (strong; load-bearing). pp. 32–33, full paragraph cross-checked twice:
"In both linear and geometric aggregations, weights express trade-offs between indicators. A deficit in one dimension can thus be offset (compensated) by a surplus in another. This implies an inconsistency between how weights are conceived (usually measuring the importance of the associated variable) and the actual meaning when geometric or linear aggregations are used. In a linear aggregation, the compensability is constant, while with geometric aggregations compensability is lower for the composite indicators with low values. In terms of policy, if compensability is admitted (as in the case of pure economic indicators), a country with low scores on one indicator will need a much higher score on the others to improve its situation when geometric aggregation is used. Thus, in benchmarking exercises, countries with low scores prefer a linear rather than a geometric aggregation. On the other hand, the marginal utility from an increase in low absolute score would be much higher than in a high absolute score under geometric aggregation. Consequently, a country would have a greater incentive to address those sectors/activities/alternatives with low scores if the aggregation were geometric rather than linear, as this would give it a better chance of improving its position in the ranking (Munda & Nardo, 2005).
To ensure that weights remain a measure of importance, other aggregation methods should be used, in particular methods that do not allow compensability. Moreover, if different goals are equally legitimate and important, a non-compensatory logic might be necessary."
Also p. 33: "A non-compensatory multi-criteria approach (MCA) could assure non-compensability by finding a compromise between two or more legitimate goals."

"As discussed previously, an undesirable feature of additive aggregations is the full compensability they imply: poor performance in some indicators can be compensated by sufficiently high values of other indicators. For example if an hypothetical composite indicator was formed by inequality, environmental degradation, GDP per capita and unemployment, two countries, one with values (21,1,1,1) and the other with (6,6,6,6) would have equal composite indicator value (=6) under an additive aggregation. Obviously the two countries would represent very different social conditions that would not be reflected in the composite. Geometric aggregation (i.e. the product of weighted indicators) is a less compensatory approach. In our simple example the first country would have a much lower composite indicator value (=2.14) than the second country (=6.00) under the geometric aggregation."

ITEM 5. BECKER, SAISANA, PARUOLO & VANDECASTEELE (2017), ECOLOGICAL INDICATORS 80
==========================================================
ABSTRACT ONLY. ScienceDirect blocked; read the abstract on the JRC Publications Repository record https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/handle/JRC105460 (institutional copy). Strength strong for the abstract; role supporting.
"Composite indicators are very popular tools for assessing and ranking countries and institutions in terms of environmental performance, sustainability, and other complex concepts that are not directly measurable. Because of the stakes that come with the media attention of these tools, a word of caution is warranted. One common misconception relates to the effect of the weights assigned to indicators during the aggregation process. This work presents a novel series of tools that allow developers and users of composite indicators to explore effects of these weights. First, the importance of each indicator to the composite is measured by the nonlinear Pearson correlation ratio, estimated by Bayesian Gaussian processes. Second, the effect of each indicator is isolated from that of other indicators using regression analysis, and examined in detail. Finally, an optimisation procedure is proposed which allows weights to be fitted to agree with pre-specified values of importance. These three tools together give developers considerable insight into the effects of weights and suggest possibilities for refining and simplifying the aggregation. The added value of these tools are shown on three case studies: the Resource Governance Index, the Good Country Index, and the Water Retention Index."

They quote the sentence as it actually appears, i.e. as a subordinate clause in which Goodhart names the law after himself: "Ignoring Goodhart's law, that any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes". They characterise it as a "(jocular) aside" rather than the paper's main point, and give the reprint trail as Courakis (ed.) 1981 and Goodhart 1984, Monetary Theory and Practice (Macmillan). They also contrast it with the Lucas critique (Goodhart's law has "institutional application" to monetary policy; Lucas is about econometric method generally).

Corroboration of the standalone sentence: Thornton, FRB St Louis WP 2007-039D (https://files.stlouisfed.org/files/htdocs/wp/2007/2007-039.pdf, institutional copy) quotes it as "any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes", citing "Goodhart (1975)". Manheim & Garrabrant (arXiv, see item 9) quote the same sentence and cite Goodhart as source. Wikipedia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart%27s_law) gives the location as Papers in Monetary Economics vol. 1, pp. 1–20, and a 1981 reprint at p. 116; Michael McIntyre's DAMTP page (https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/mem2/papers/LHCE/goodhart.html) gives the 1984 book location as p. 96. I could not verify either page number at source.

QUOTATION DIFFERENCES TO REPORT: (i) The sentence is almost always quoted as a free-standing aphorism; in the original it is a dependent clause introduced by "Ignoring Goodhart's law, that…" — Goodhart coined the name himself, half in jest. (ii) McIntyre's page records a quite different "economic" wording from Pears Cyclopaedia 1990–91: "As soon as the government attempts to regulate any particular set of financial assets, these become unreliable as indicators of economic trends." (iii) The popular form (1b) is not Goodhart's.
Strength: wording strong (three independent copies agree); page numbers unverified. Role: load-bearing.

4. BEVAN & HOOD 2006 and HOOD 2006

4a. Bevan & Hood, "What's measured is what matters", Public Administration 84(3): 517–538. Read at a third-party hosted PDF (http://straty.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Whats-Measured-is-What-Matters-Bevan-Hood-2006.pdf); the LSE eprint record (https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/16211/) confirms the abstract's first sentence: "In the 2000s, governments in the UK, particularly in England, developed a system of governance of public services that combined targets with an element of terror."
Two assumptions, p. 520: "One is that measurement problems are unimportant, that the part on which performance is measured can adequately represent performance on the whole" / "The other is that this method of governance is not vulnerable to gaming by agents". Synecdoche, p. 520: "What underlies these assumptions is the idea of synecdoche (taking a part to stand for a whole)."
Gaming defined, p. 521: "'Gaming' is here defined as reactive subversion such as 'hitting the target and missing the point' or reducing performance where targets do not apply."
Three kinds, p. 521: "Ratchet effects refer to the tendency for central controllers to base next year's targets on last year's performance" (hence "managers who expect still to be in place in the next target period have a perverse incentive not to exceed targets even if they could easily do so"). "Threshold effects refer to the effects of targets on the distribution of performance among a range of, and within, production units" (creating "a perverse incentive for those doing better than the target to allow their performance to deteriorate to the standard"). "Attempts to achieve targets at the cost of significant but unmeasured aspects of performance result in output distortions."
Four interpretations of any reported success, pp. 524–525 (fetch returned them abbreviated with ellipses — check): "All is well; performance is exactly what central controllers would wish in all performance domains"; the organisation performs as wished on measured dimensions "but this outcome has been at the expense of unacceptably poor performance" elsewhere; "Although performance as measured appears to be fine...actions are quite at variance with the substantive goals"; "There has been a failure to meet measured-performance targets...but this outcome has been concealed by strategic manipulation".
Headline evidence. Ambulances, p. 531: "A study by the Commission for Health Improvement (2003c) found evidence that in a third of ambulance trusts, response times had been 'corrected' to be reported to be less than eight minutes." and the distribution showed "a curious 'spike' at 8 minutes – with the strong implication that times between 8 and 9 minutes have been reclassified to be less than 8 minutes." A&E four-hour target, p. 531: "a fourth practice was to require patients to wait in queues of ambulances outside A&E Departments until the hospital in question was confident that that patient could be seen within four hours"; also "the drafting in of extra staff and the cancelling of operations scheduled for the period over which performance was measured." Waiting lists, p. 532: "The National Audit Office (2001) reported evidence that nine NHS trusts had 'inappropriately' adjusted their waiting lists...affecting nearly 6000 patient records."
Real improvements acknowledged, p. 526: "In 2002, 23 per cent of patients spent over four hours in A&E departments, but in the three months from April to June 2004 only 5.3 per cent stayed that long." pp. 527–528: "at the end March of 2003, when virtually no patient in England was reported as waiting more than 12 months for an elective admission, the equivalent figures for Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland were 10, 16 and 22 per cent."
Their own verdict, p. 533: "Although there were indeed dramatic improvements in reported performance, we do not know the extent to which these were genuine or offset by gaming". Conclusion opens: "We have argued that the implicit theory of governance by targets requires two sets of heroic assumptions to be satisfied: of robust synecdoche, and game-proof design." Remedies, pp. 533–534: "introduce more uncertainty into these specifications...by making them transparent in process and in retrospect but not in real time"; fill "the 'audit hole'"; "a greater face-to-face element in the overall control system."
Strength: strong (full text read; page numbers visible), but copy is third-party. Role: load-bearing — this is the chapter's central taxonomy.

4b. Hood, "Gaming in Targetworld", PAR 66(4): 515–521 (Crossref confirms pagination). Read at a third-party hosted PDF (https://www.squawkpoint.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/02/Targetworld.pdf). UNEXPECTED THREADS (max 8)
====================================================================
1. Goodhart named the law after himself, as a joke, inside a subordinate clause — the "law" was never stated as a free-standing sentence by its author (Chrystal & Mizen).
2. The Orlando Wilson/Chicago crime example routinely pinned to Campbell's law is not in the 1976/1979 paper; it is from Campbell 1969 "Reforms as Experiments".
3. Hood 2006 observes that central managers' reluctance to build independent verification of performance data "might itself be interpreted as a form of gaming by the central managers" — gaming runs upward as well as downward.
4. Bevan & Hamblin: the ambulance 8-minute target was met only in the one country (England) where it carried reputational sanctions — the same mechanism that produced the gaming produced the delivery.
5. The Wales natural experiments (league tables abolished 2001; no hospital sanctions) are the cleanest counter-evidence that publishing measures changes outcomes, not just numbers (Burgess et al.; Besley et al.; Bevan & Wilson).

UNEXPECTED THREADS (found, not asked for)
====================================================================
1. Legatum's aggregation is a graded set of power means (p = –1 at the top, rising to 1 at the bottom) chosen explicitly to "punish weakness" — a published example of encoding "parts are not substitutes" in the arithmetic rather than the weights. Directly relevant to a nightlife measure where, e.g., licensing conditions and venue stock should not cancel.
2. The IMD's exponential transformation exists for the same reason ("to reduce 'cancellation effects'"), and the IMD is "specifically constructed to identify deprivation and not affluence" — i.e. a one-tailed instrument by design. Worth a line on whether a cultural/place measure is one-tailed or two-tailed.
3. UNECE's September 2025 well-being guidelines — newer than anything on your list — land on "composite indices should be accompanied by dashboards... that give information about the development in the underlying components", and define a dashboard as the form in which the reader "can (and indeed must)" do the deduction. That is the recipe-on-the-face argument in official statistical language.
4. UNESCO Culture|2030 explicitly chooses same-place change over time over cross-city ranking: "evidence of change over time in the same place, rather than global comparability or ranking between different countries or cities" — a third form beside composite and scoreboard: the longitudinal self-profile.
5. Crossick & Kaszynska's reframe — "instead of 'measuring' it is more appropriate to speak of 'evaluation', of which measurement is but one component" — is a cleaner anchor for the chapter than the usual "not commensurable" line.
6. The Culture Counts lineage runs back to a 2011 Western Australian government framework (PVMF), then Manchester 2012, then the 2015–16 national test; the 2016 report already uses "Benchmarking" language while warning against "clapometer" readings and "apples, pears and pineapples" comparisons — the tension between profile instrument and benchmarking dashboard was present from the start.
7. Creative Footprint's 15 criteria have changed names and contents across editions (early PDF v. website v. 2024 report) with no published changelog, while the reports compare scores across editions. That is a reproducibility problem independent of the undisclosed algorithm.
8. SPI's rationale for equal weights is an explicit null: "there is no clear theoretical or empirical reason to weight any of the components more highly than any other." Equal weights are themselves a declared judgement — useful to quote against anyone who treats equal weighting as neutral.
