## Facet A — The dashboard literature proper

All fetches 2026-10-05. ResearchGate and ScienceDirect refused all requests; HBR's article body is paywalled.

### 1a. Few, "Dashboard Confusion" (2004)
- Few, S. (2004). "Dashboard Confusion." *Intelligent Enterprise*, 20 March 2004. Author's copy: https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/ie/dashboard_confusion.pdf
- Definition (p. 3): "A dashboard is a visual display of the most important information needed to achieve one or more objectives; consolidated and arranged on a single screen so the information can be monitored at a glance."
- (p. 1): "Everyone in the business intelligence (BI) space is wild about dashboards, but few proponents are talking about the same thing."
- (p. 4): "A dashboard is a type of display or style of presentation, not a specific type of information or technology."
- Strength: practitioner trade-press article; the most-cited definition in the literature. Load-bearing for the definition. **Orchestrator spot-check at source: both quotations confirmed.**

### 1b. Few, *Information Dashboard Design* (2006; 2nd ed. 2013)
- 2006 §1.3.1 read via an online reproduction (flylib). Definition identical to 2004. "Single screen": information "must fit on a single screen, entirely available within the viewer's eye span so it can all be seen at once, at a glance." Primary function: alerting that action may be needed (paraphrase).
- 2013 revised definition — "a predominantly visual information display that people use to rapidly monitor current conditions that require a timely response to fulfill a specific role" — verified ONLY as quoted by Sarikaya et al. 2019. One reviewer quotes the 2006 wording for the 2nd edition; discrepancy unresolved. Treat as "as quoted in Sarikaya et al."

### 1c. Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design" (2006)
- https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf (vendor-sponsored whitepaper)
- (p. 2) "most dashboards say too little, and what they do say requires far too much effort to discern. This is a failure more of design than technology." Vendor examples feature "flashy displays showing off cute gauges, meters, and traffic lights, but rarely will you find dashboards that really communicate."
- The thirteen pitfalls (pp. 3–14): exceeding a single screen · inadequate context · excessive detail or precision · expressing measures indirectly · inappropriate display media · meaningless variety · poorly designed display media · encoding quantitative data inaccurately · arranging data poorly · ineffectively highlighting what's important · cluttering with useless decoration · misusing colour · unappealing visual display.
- Note: entirely about display errors, not about purpose or use.

### 1d. Other Few pieces at source
- "Dashboard Design: Taking a Metaphor Too Far" (2005): gauges "can be understood with little instruction. That's the advantage—the only advantage—of the dashboard metaphor." "They've forgotten that the goal of a dashboard—the only goal—is communication."
- "Dashboard Confusion Revisited" (2007): "the term dashboard continues to be used in reference to almost any type of screen-based display that combines more than a single chart, no matter what its purpose." Introduces "Faceted Analytical Display": "a set of interactive charts … on a single screen, each of which presents a somewhat different view of a common dataset, and is used to analyze that information." Monitoring displays "must be designed and must function quite differently than displays that are used to analyze data."
- "Why Most Dashboards Fail" (2007): "Most dashboards that are used in businesses today fail." "The root of the problem is not technology … but poor data presentation." No evidence base for "most".
- Blog, 20 Jan 2010: "a dashboard is used for monitoring purposes … Its effectiveness should be judged on the basis of its ability to help people monitor what's going on—that is, to maintain situation awareness." "what people expect of a dashboard's design is often quite different from what they actually need."

### 2. Sarikaya, Correll, Bartram, Tory & Fisher (2019)
- "What Do We Talk About When We Talk About Dashboards?" *IEEE TVCG* 25(1), 682–692 (InfoVis 2018). Preprint read: https://alper.datav.is/assets/publications/dashboards/dashboards-preprint.pdf
- "Visualization dashboards are ubiquitous. They are built and employed by nearly every industry, non-profit, and service organization to support data-driven decision making."
- "Even the definition of a dashboard is in flux." Resolution: "We distinguish between the visual genre of dashboards (a visual data representation structured as a tiled layout of simple charts and/or large numbers) and the functional genre (an interactive display that enables real-time monitoring of dynamically updating data)."
- §5.3.2 Social impact: "Dashboards often restrict the scope of interpretation and decision making." Constrained scorecard views "can impose mechanistic, unsophisticated decision-making regimes susceptible to misinterpretation." A community-safety dashboard shifted framing "from an asset-based model of neighborhood health (social cohesion, resilience)" to "a deficit-based model (crime, code violations)." Reports of "dysfunctional impact...related to concerns of surveillance and anxiety about what data were being captured, who had access, and how they were interpreted."
- §6.2: "As dashboards venture into information presentation territories previously considered infographics or narrative visualization, designers must become mindful of storytelling, rhetoric, and persuasion."
- Method: 83 dashboards, convenience sample (business dashboards under-represented, "usually confidential"); κ = 0.64. Design space: Purpose (strategic/tactical/operational decision support; communication & learning), Audience, Visual features, Data semantics. Seven clusters: Strategic Decision-Making (16), Quantified Self (7), Static Operational (10), Static Organizational (8), Operational Decision-Making (14), Communication (13), "Dashboards Evolved" (15).
- Strength: peer-reviewed, top venue; corpus analysis not user study. Load-bearing for the definition problem and typology.

### 3. Bach et al. (2023), "Dashboard Design Patterns"
- *IEEE TVCG* 29(1), 342–352 (VIS 2022). Open copy: https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/170047/1/WRAP-dashboard-design-patterns-Turkay-2022.pdf
- Abstract: "Despite a growing number of public examples, case studies, and general guidelines there is surprisingly little design guidance for dashboards." "Based on a systematic review of 144 dashboards, we report on eight groups of design patterns…"
- Adopts Few's definition and quotes Kitchin & McArdle: a dashboard is "a translator, not simply a mirror, setting the forms and parameters for how data are communicated."
- Tradeoff: "Reducing one of the parameters (screenspace, abstraction, number of pages, interaction) requires an increase in one of multiple of the other parameters." Workshop participant: "can a dashboard ever contain too much information?"
- 42 patterns in 8 groups. Genres (share of 144): Static 21%, Analytic 73%, Magazine 2%, Infographic 6%, Repository 17%, Embedded Mini 4% (not mutually exclusive). Workshop: 23 participants; recurring problem "information overload".
- Establishes: the single-screen non-interactive dashboard Few defined is a minority genre (21%) in a curated corpus.

### 4. Yigitbasioglu & Velcu (2012)
- "A review of dashboards in performance management." *Int. J. Accounting Information Systems* 13(1), 41–59. Abstract only (full text blocked).
- "Dashboards are expected to improve decision making by amplifying cognition and capitalizing on human perceptual capabilities." "Despite dashboards' popularity, little is known about the extent of their effectiveness." Recommends "high data-ink ratio and drill down features" and "some level of flexibility".
- Companion (read in full): Velcu-Laitinen & Yigitbasioglu (2012), *Int. J. Digital Accounting Research* 12, 39–58. Finnish sales managers, N = 145 responses, 36 dashboard users. "only about a quarter of the sales managers surveyed in Finland used a dashboard … Dashboards were used for four distinct purposes: (i) monitoring, (ii) problem solving, (iii) rationalizing, and (iv) communication and consistency." (p. 50) "Dashboards are most intensively used for communication and consistency purposes, even more than for self-monitoring. This highlights the fact that dashboards are to be seen as collaborative tools rather than individual decision support systems."

### 5. Eckerson, *Performance Dashboards* (2nd ed. 2011)
- Publisher sample chapter read. Definition (p. 10): "A layered information delivery system that parcels out information, insights, and alerts to users on demand so they can measure, monitor, and manage business performance more effectively."
- Three applications: Monitoring, Analysis, Management. Three layers: graphical metrics view → multidimensional view → detailed/operational view. Three types: Operational (monitoring), Tactical (analysis), Strategic (management).
- EIS history (pp. 5–6): the 1980s executive information systems "never gained traction" because "systems were geared to so few people in each company and were built on mainframes or minicomputers that made them costly to customize and maintain."
- Failure symptoms (pp. 8–9): "too flat", "too manual", "too isolated". (p. 10) "performance dashboards are only as effective as the organizations they seek to measure."
- Establishes: Eckerson's dashboard is a layered system, the opposite pole from Few's single screen.

### 6. Origins
- Kaplan & Norton (1992), "The Balanced Scorecard—Measures That Drive Performance," *HBR* 70(1), 71–79. HBR body paywalled; cockpit passage read in a secondary reproduction (not verified against HBR): "Think of the balanced scorecard as the dials and indicators in an airplane cockpit. For the complex task of navigating and flying an airplane, pilots need detailed information about many aspects of the flight. They need information on fuel, air speed, altitude, bearing, destination, and other indicators that summarize the current and predicted environment. Reliance on one instrument can be fatal." The cockpit — not the car — is the original managerial instrument-panel metaphor, and its point was plurality of measures.
- Pre-history (Wikipedia, data not instructions): the French *tableau de bord*; GE's 1950s measurement project; Schneiderman's 1987 Analog Devices scorecard. Epstein & Manzoni (1998) *European Management Journal* 16(2) is the scholarly account (citation only).
- Watson, Rainer & Koh (1991), "Executive Information Systems," *MIS Quarterly* 15(1), 13–30 (read). EIS: "a computerized system that provides executives with easy access to internal and external information that is relevant to their critical success factors." Survey of 50 firms: "current status access" dominant (88%).

### 7. Endsley — situation awareness
- Endsley (1995), "Toward a Theory of Situation Awareness in Dynamic Systems," *Human Factors* 37(1), 32–64. Abstract at source; definition via Endsley (2015) restatement and secondaries: "the perception of the elements in the environment within a volume of time and space, the comprehension of their meaning, and the projection of their status in the near future." Levels: perception; comprehension ("the decision maker forms a holistic picture of the environment"); projection.
- Few's own whitepaper *Dashboard Design for Real-Time Situation Awareness* (2007) cites Endsley, Bolte & Jones (2003): "Basically, situation awareness is being aware of what is happening around you and understanding what that information means to you now and in the future."
- Criticism: Flach (1995) circularity; Dekker & Hollnagel (2004) "folk model".
- Establishes: the only serious cognitive theory behind "monitor at a glance"; its Levels 2–3 are comprehension and projection, which a display of current values does not itself supply.

### 8. Why dashboards fail or go unused
- Dowding et al. (2015), *Int. J. Medical Informatics* 84(2), 87–100. Author manuscript read. 122 full texts → 11 included; "one study was rated as being of high quality, four as being fair-good quality and five as low quality." "overall the results of these two studies indicated no difference with dashboard use, because of the proportion of individuals who opted not to use them." Screen-saver deployment: "clinicians had access to the information constantly. These studies also indicated positive outcomes." "it remains unclear exactly what characteristics of dashboards are related to improved outcomes."
- Rossi et al. (2025), "From glitter to gold," *Implementation Science* 20:16 (open). "The dashboard's impact is dependent on its use by the individuals for whom it was designed. Yet, few studies measure dashboard use." "Only one study examined dashboard use and indicated that few individuals (28%) used the dashboard at least once." "Dashboards are only as accurate as the data they use"; users may "ignore the dashboard to avoid receiving negative feedback"; sustainment fails when a dashboard "is poorly integrated with end users' workflow processes."
- Pauwels et al. (2009), "Dashboards as a Service," *J. Service Research* 12(2), 175–189 (read). (p. 180) "Currently, we lack empirical knowledge about dashboards' success in delivering these overlapping benefits." Implementation failures: departmental metric politics, metric proliferation, periodicity mismatch, user resistance, lack of support.
- Schwendimann et al. (2017), *IEEE TLT* 10(1), 30–41: 346 papers → 55; "most studies are exploratory and proof-of-concept".

## Facet B — Does it work, and what makes it trusted

All fetches 5 October 2026. PubMed/PMC intermittently captcha'd; Emerald, Wiley, ACM DL, ScienceDirect, Lancet blocked.

### 1. Healthcare
- **Dowding et al. 2015** (abstract page read): "One hundred and twenty two full text papers were retrieved of which 11 were included in the review." "There was evidence that in contexts where dashboards were easily accessible to clinicians (such as in the form of a screen saver) their use was associated with improved care processes and patient outcomes." "further high quality detailed research studies need to be conducted."
- **Khairat et al. 2018**, *JMIR Human Factors* 5(2):e22 (PMC full text). 17 articles. "Visualization dashboard solutions decrease time spent on data gathering, difficulty of data gathering process, cognitive load, time to task completion, errors, and improve situation awareness, compliance with evidence-based safety guidelines, usability, and navigation." "Due to the relatively few studies conducted in this area, there is plenty of room…" "Most studies tested a single solution in a single implementation setting."
- **Buttigieg, Pace & Rathert 2017** — could not read any text. Do not quote.
- **Coiera et al. 2025**, "Clinical and economic impact of digital dashboards on hospital inpatient care: a systematic review," *JAMIA Open* 8(4):ooaf078 (PMC full text). 5,755 records → 70 studies: 61 quasi-experimental, 6 RCTs, 3 observational; risk of bias "highly variable", most "Serious" or "Moderate". Mortality (16 studies): "Five reported a decrease, whilst the majority (n = 15) found no significant change." Length of stay (31 studies): "28 reporting a reduction, an increase in five, and ten reporting no change." Harms (16 studies): "Increases were observed in six, decreases in four, and no change in 11." Conclusion: "Hospital dashboards do appear associated with either no change or a reduction in mortality, reduced costs, reduced LOS, and improved patient and caregiver satisfaction with care. Association with harms was equivocal." "Actual impacts … will likely be dependent on multiple local factors such as workflow integration."

### 2. Education
- **Verbert et al. 2014**, *Personal and Ubiquitous Computing* 18:1499–1514 (abstract): dashboards "promote awareness, reflection, and sense-making, and … enable learners to define goals and track progress toward these goals."
- **Bodily & Verbert 2017**, *IEEE TLT* 10(4):405–418 (abstract): 945 → 93 articles; "experiments to determine the effect of these systems on student behavior, achievement, and skills are needed to add to the small existing body of evidence".
- **Jivet et al. 2017** (EC-TEL, abstract): "We find evidence that current designs foster competition between learners rather than knowledge mastery, offering misguided frames of reference for comparison."
- **Jivet et al. 2018**, "License to evaluate," LAK '18 (preprint read in full): "very few dashboard evaluations take into account the educational concepts that were used as a theoretical foundation for their design." "only one paper directly evaluated improvement in self-regulated learning although 13 papers listed supporting self-regulated learning as one of their goals." "comparison with peers … was not perceived positively by all learners." Of 26 evaluated dashboards, 14 evaluated on usability/satisfaction.
- **Kaliisa et al. 2024**, "Have Learning Analytics Dashboards Lived Up to the Hype?" LAK '24 (author page): 38 studies. Achievement: "Most studies reported negligible or small effects". "There is no evidence to support the conclusion that LADs have lived up the promise of improving academic achievement." Participation: medium–large effects in several studies.

### 3. Government
- **Matheus, Janssen & Maheshwari**, *Government Information Quarterly* 37(3):101284 (2020; online 2018) (abstract + summary): "dashboards can improve transparency and accountability, however, realizing these benefits was cumbersome … Challenges include insufficient data quality, lack of understanding of data, poor analysis, wrong interpretation, confusion about the outcomes, and imposing a pre-defined view." "These challenges can easily result in misconceptions, wrong decision-making, creating a blurred picture resulting in less transparency and accountability, and ultimately in even less trust in the government." "Dashboards need to be complemented by mechanisms supporting citizens' engagement, data interpretation, governance and institutional arrangements." Body: "wrong information is worse than having no information". Two Rio de Janeiro cases.
- **Chokki et al. 2022**, *Digital Government: Research and Practice* 3(3) (read via Academia): Namur budget dashboard vs portal visualisations; control n=50, treatment n=58; higher usability (76.85 vs 66.3), higher stated willingness to engage (4.43 vs 4.02). Weak design.
- **Behn 2007 / 2008** on CitiStat/PerformanceStat (read): "It is impossible to prove that CitiStat, by itself, produced all (or even most) of the improvements that Baltimore achieved." PerformanceStat is "an ongoing series of regular, frequent, periodic, integrated meetings…" "it has to focus on them at meeting, after meeting, after meeting."
- **Gerrish 2016**, *Public Administration Review* 76(1):48–66 (abstract): "Using 2,188 effects from 49 studies, the analysis finds that performance management has a small average effect. However, the effect is substantially larger when indicators of best practices in high-quality studies are included."
- **Bevan & Hood 2006**, *Public Administration* 84(3):517–538 (abstract): "a system of governance of public services that combined targets with an element of terror."
- **Public use:** Barbazza et al. 2022 (33 national COVID dashboard teams; medRxiv read): "Above all, the dashboards targeted the general public, though no dashboard team described having direct or formal contact with the public in the early development stage." "monitoring user analytics was not pursued for the common reason: 'there wasn't time for deep analysis of user behaviour'." "Transparency on methods and admitting mistakes... were perceived as enablers." Sense about Science, *What Counts?* (2022): "Engagement with the PHE data dashboard and ONS websites was surprisingly high (35% and 15% of respondents)."

### 5. The 2020 public dashboards

### 6. Trust frameworks
- **UK Code of Practice for Statistics**, edition 2.0 (2018) p. 12 and edition 3.0 (Autumn 2025) p. 30. Pillars: Trustworthiness — "Confidence in the people and organisations that produce statistics and data" (3.0: "Building confidence in…"); Quality — "Data and methods that produce assured statistics"; Value — "Statistics that support society's needs for information". (2.0 p. 17) "Trustworthiness is a product of the people, systems and processes within organisations." (3.0 p. 3) "Official statistics serve the public good when, as public assets, they provide insight that allows them to be used widely for informing understanding and shaping action." - **O'Neill, 2002 Reith Lectures** (BBC transcripts read). Lecture 3: "Perverse incentives are real incentives." "Intelligent accountability, I suspect, requires more attention to good governance and fewer fantasies about total control." "Real accountability provides substantive and knowledgeable independent judgement." - OSR blog 28 June 2021: "you can't force someone to trust you. You can only show through the way you behave...that you are honest, reliable, and competent." - **van der Bles et al. 2020**, *PNAS* 117(14):7672–7683 (abstract): five experiments, n=5,780 incl. preregistered replication and BBC field study: "only a small decrease in trust in numbers and trustworthiness of the source, and mostly for verbal" uncertainty — numeric ranges barely dent trust; vague verbal hedges do.

## Facet C — The critical and theoretical scholarship

All fetched 2026-10-05.

### 1a. Kitchin, Lauriault & McArdle 2015 — load-bearing
- *Regional Studies, Regional Science* 2(1): 6–28 (CC-BY). Maynooth accepted manuscript read.
- Abstract: "we examine such initiatives arguing that they advance a narrowly conceived but powerful realist epistemology – the city as visualised facts – that is reshaping how managers and citizens come to know and govern cities… they are also underpinned by a naive instrumental rationality, are open to manipulation by vested interests, and suffer from often unacknowledged methodological and technical issues."
- "Indicators are recurrent quantified measures that can be tracked over time to provide information about stasis and change with respect to a particular phenomenon." (p. 7)
- "data are never raw, but always already cooked" (p. 15, citing Bowker 2005).
- "they do not simply act as a camera reflecting the world as it is, but rather act as an engine shaping the world in diverse ways" (p. 17). **Orchestrator spot-check: confirmed (p. 18 in the manuscript's pagination).**
- Uses: "instrumentally… conceptually… tactically (e.g., for delaying a strategy, substitute for action, deflect criticism), symbolically (e.g., to provide reassurance or place promotion), and politically (e.g., ammunition to support a particular position)" (p. 17).
- "Hard facts trump other kinds of knowing, and undermine and displace other scientific forms of urban knowledge that are less systematic and continuous" (p. 18).
- Conclusion: "we advocate for indicator and dashboard projects that openly recognize their situatedness as the product of a complex data assemblage; … that signal their positionality and politics; that fully document their lineage, data provenance, metadata, and levels of error and uncertainty; that recognize that they do not reflect the world as it actually is, but actively frame and produce the world." (pp. 23–24) **Orchestrator spot-check: confirmed.**

### 1b. Kitchin & McArdle 2017 — "Six key issues" (preprint read)
Realist epistemology; dashboards "are not objective, neutral mirrors of cities" but "actively frame and do work in the world"; "All datasets contain instrument and human error and bias"; six issues: epistemology, scope and access, veracity and validity, usability and literacy, use and utility, ethics. "The data and tools in dashboards can be used to treat places and populations within them differentially in ways that can be discriminatory."

### 1c. Kitchin, Maalsen & McArdle 2016, *Geoforum* 77: 93–101 (preprint read)
"far from being neutral, objective, apolitical communication tools, dashboards are inherently active and ideological"; when Dublin City Council baulked at showing negative indicators, the developers' reply: "If people know you have the data, but you don't show it, it looks like you're trying to dupe them."

### 2. Mattern, "Mission Control: A History of the Urban Dashboard," *Places Journal*, March 2015 (read at source)
- "the term dashboard, first used in 1846, originally referred to the board or leather apron on the front of a vehicle that kept horse hooves and wheels from splashing mud into the interior." ## Facet D — The statistics of aggregation

All fetches 2026-10-05.

### 1. MAUP
- **Openshaw 1984, CATMOG 38** (full scan read, Oslo mirror). "There are no rules for areal aggregation, no standards, and no international conventions to guide the spatial aggregation process. Quite simply, the areal units (zonal objects) used in many geographical studies are arbitrary, modifiable, and subject to the whims and fancies of whoever is doing, or did, the aggregating." (pp. 2–3) Scale problem: "the variation in results that can often be obtained when data for one set of areal units are progressively aggregated into fewer and larger units for analysis" (p. 8). Zoning: "Any variation in results due to the use of alternative units of analysis when the number of units is held constant is termed the aggregation problem." (p. 8) "the MAUP is today one of the most important unresolved problems left in spatial analysis." (p. 5) Link: "The ecological fallacy problem occurs because areal studies cannot distinguish between spatial associations created by the aggregation of data and real associations possessed by the individual data prior to spatial aggregation." (p. 14) "The simplest solution to the MAUP is to pretend it does not exist" (p. 31). Iowa: 99 counties, % Republican vote 1968 × % population over 60; "The value of the correlation at the 99 county level is 0.34"; for six-zone aggregations "the range of possible correlations is between -.99 and +.99" (Table 11, p. 22).
- NCRM/ReStore MAUP page (read): "the observed aggregated values will vary according to how we draw our area boundaries"; worked 16-area unemployment example; gerrymandering as MAUP in practice.
- Lee, Rogers & Soifer 2025, *Political Analysis* 33: 412–424 (open, read): results "change in wildly unpredictable ways".
- Andresen 2021 (encyclopedia entry, read): correlations –0.99 to 0.99 by zoning alone; regression estimates can vary ~nine-fold by unit size; remedy = sensitivity analysis across units.

### 2. Ecological fallacy
- **Robinson 1950**, *ASR* 15(3): 351–357 (JSTOR scan read; OCR digit corrections noted). Race × illiteracy, 1930, nine divisions: ecological r = .946; individual r = .203. Foreign birth × illiteracy: individual .118, ecological –.619 (sign reversal). "The relation between ecological and individual correlations … provides a definite answer as to whether ecological correlations can validly be used as substitutes for individual correlations. They cannot." (p. 356)
- Leyland & Groenewegen 2020 (open textbook, read): ecological fallacy — "the danger of attributing these macro relationships to relations at the individual level"; atomistic fallacy — "the analysis is carried out at the micro (individual) level, but inference is made at the macro (group) level".
- **Subramanian, Jones, Kaddour & Krieger 2009**, *IJE* 38(2): 342–360 (abstract + Harvard summary): "perils are posed by not only ecological fallacy but also individualistic fallacy." "Multilevel thinking, grounded in historical and spatiotemporal context, is thus a necessity, not an option." Harvard summary: "States with legalized segregation had higher illiteracy rates among both whites and blacks, but the effect was stronger on blacks." The area is not merely noise around individuals; context is real.

### 3. Simpson's paradox
- **Simpson 1951**, *JRSS B* 13(2): 238–241 (read): "The dangers of amalgamating 2 × 2 tables are well known" (p. 240). "The treatment can hardly be rejected as valueless to the race when it is beneficial when applied to males and to females." (p. 241) Which table to believe depends on what the third variable means.
- **Julious & Mullee 1994**, *BMJ* 309: 1480–1481 (read): kidney stones — open surgery 78% (273/350) vs percutaneous 83% (289/350) overall; <2 cm: 93% (81/87) vs 83%/87% (234/270 — arithmetic says 86.7%; check BMJ print); ≥2 cm: 73% (192/263) vs 69% (55/80). "The main reason why the success rate reversed is because the probability of having open surgery or percutaneous nephrolithotomy varied according to the diameter of the stones."
- **Bickel, Hammel & O'Connell 1975**, *Science* 187: 398–404 (read): "About 44 percent of the males and about 35 percent of the females were admitted." "The proportion of women applicants tends to be high in departments that are hard to get into and low in those that are easy to get into." "If the data are properly pooled … there is a small but statistically significant bias in favor of women." No lawsuit (Reinhart 2016).

### 4. Aggregation bias in indices
- **OECD/JRC 2008 Handbook** (JRC copy read): "composite indicators can send misleading policy messages if they are poorly constructed or misinterpreted." (p. 13) "To have an objective comparison across small and large countries, scaling of variables by an appropriate size measure, e.g. population, income, trade volume, and populated land area, etc. is required." (p. 23) Ranking: "not affected by outliers … Country performance in absolute terms however cannot be evaluated as information on levels is lost." (p. 28) z-scores: "Indicators with extreme values thus have a greater effect on the composite indicator." (p. 28) Min-max: "extreme values/or outliers could distort the transformed indicator." (p. 28) Compensability: "A deficit in one dimension can thus be offset (compensated) by a surplus in another." "In a linear aggregation, the compensability is constant, while with geometric aggregations compensability is lower." "countries with low scores prefer a linear rather than a geometric aggregation." (p. 33) Weights: "weights express trade-offs between indicators … This implies an inconsistency between how weights are conceived (usually measuring the importance of the associated variable) and the actual meaning when geometric or linear aggregations are used." (p. 33)
- **CWTS Leiden Ranking, "Responsible use"** (read): size-dependent vs size-independent indicators; "it is best not to pay attention to small performance differences"; "Focusing on the ranks of universities can be misleading because universities with quite similar values for a certain indicator may have very different ranks."
- Greco et al. 2019 (abstract); Mazziotta & Pareto 2013 (read): compensatory (arithmetic mean) vs non-compensatory (geometric) approaches.
- Agent's synthesis (marked as such): per-capita/rates remove size but make small denominators volatile; logs tame skew but compress the top; ranks are robust to outliers but discard magnitude; sums are transparent but let the large and the extreme dominate.
