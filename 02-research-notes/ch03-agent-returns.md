1.1 Cleveland, W.S. & McGill, R. (1984). "Graphical Perception: Theory, Experimentation, and Application to the Development of Graphical Methods." JASA 79(387): 531–544. DOI 10.1080/01621459.1984.10478080
URL read: https://math.pku.edu.cn/teachers/xirb/Courses/biostatistics/Biostatistics2016/GraphicalPerception_Jasa1984.pdf (course-hosted copy of the JASA PDF; JSTOR, T&F and two US university copies were blocked/401/cover-page-only)
How read: AT SOURCE (full PDF, read in three slices).
Exact quotations:
- Abstract opening: "The subject of graphical methods for data analysis and for data presentation needs a scientific foundation…Our approach is based on graphical perception—the visual decoding of information encoded on graphs—and it includes both theory and experimentation to test the theory."
- Framing of the ordering as hypothesis: "In this section we hypothesize an ordering of the 10 elementary perceptual tasks on the basis of the accuracy with which people can extract quantitative information by using them." … "The following are the 10 elementary tasks in Figure 1, ordered from most to least accurate:" followed by the list as printed: 1. Position along a common scale; 2. Positions along nonaligned scales; 3. Length, direction, angle; 4. Area; 5. Volume, curvature; 6. Shading, color saturation. (Note: 10 tasks grouped into 6 ranks; ties within a rank are deliberate — the authors could not separate length/direction/angle, or volume/curvature, or shading/saturation.)
- Core principle: "A graphical form that involves elementary perceptual tasks that lead to more accurate judgments than another graphical form (with the same quantitative information) will result in better organization and increase the chances of a correct perception of patterns and behavior."
- Experiment 1 (position–length): "In one experiment 55 subjects were shown the five types of graphs depicted in Figure 4." Stimuli were divided (stacked) and grouped bar charts; five judgment types (three position, two length); 51 usable subjects; 50 judgments each. Instructions: subjects were to make "a quick visual judgment and not try to make precise measurements, either mentally or with a physical object such as a pencil or your finger."
- Experiment 2 (position–angle): 54 subjects (51 usable), pie charts v bar charts, "ten sets of five numbers", 80 judgments.
- Results: "average errors for length judgments are 40%-250% larger than those for position judgments"; length-vs-position accuracy differed "by factors varying from 1.4 to 2.5"; position judgments were "1.96 times as accurate as angle judgments"; "Seventy-eight percent of the large errors occurred for the length judgments."
- What accuracy is NOT: "We agree with Ehrenberg (1975) that if this were the only goal, tables would be better." The theory "does not provide a complete prescription for how to make a graph" and should be applied "with judgment"; the framework is "a set of plausible statements" requiring revision.
- Recommendations: "radical surgery on these popular graphs is needed" — replace pie charts with dot charts, divided bar charts with grouped dot charts, shaded statistical maps with framed-rectangle charts; plot curve differences directly rather than asking the eye to read the vertical gap between curves. Closing: "If progress is to be made in graphics, we must be prepared to set aside old procedures when better ones are developed, just as is done in other areas of science."
What it establishes: an ordering of encodings by accuracy of RATIO/percentage judgments between two marked values, with position-on-common-scale best, length worse, angle worse again, area/volume/colour worst; the authors themselves call it a hypothesis, tested for only the top three ranks.
Strength: two lab experiments, ~51 usable subjects each (Bell Labs–era volunteers), peer-reviewed in JASA; the single most replicated result in the field (see 1.3, 1.4, 1.6). The lower ranks (area, volume, shading) were NOT tested in this paper.
Role: LOAD-BEARING.

1.2 Cleveland & McGill (1985). "Graphical Perception and Graphical Methods for Analyzing Scientific Data." Science 229(4716): 828–833.
URL read: http://webspace.ship.edu/pgmarr/Geo441/Readings/Cleveland%20and%20McGill%201985%20-%20Graphical%20Perception%20and%20Graphical%20Methods%20for%20Analyzing%20Scientific%20Data.pdf
How read: AT SOURCE (course-hosted copy).
Exact quotations: the ordering printed here as ten separate ranks: "Position along a common scale; Position on identical but nonaligned scales; Length; Angle; Slope (with angle not too close to 0, π/2, or π radians); Area; Volume; Density; Color saturation; Color hue." Caveat: "The ordering should be thought of as a tentative working hypothesis, based on current information, that can be expected to evolve." … "Aspects of the ordering are partly conjectural in that we have no controlled experimentation to support them." On divided bars: "comparing the three values of item B, or the values of item C" requires length judgments rather than position judgments; dot charts remove this. Results restated: "Position and length judgments are the most accurate; angle and slope judgments are nearly equally accurate in experiment 3 and less accurate than length judgments."

Strength: review/restatement by the original authors in Science; peer-reviewed.
Role: SUPPORTING (useful for the "what it does not claim" paragraph).

1.3 Heer, J. & Bostock, M. (2010). "Crowdsourcing Graphical Perception: Using Mechanical Turk to Assess Visualization Design." CHI 2010.
URL read: https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2010-MTurk-CHI.pdf
How read: AT SOURCE.
Exact quotations:
- Abstract: "Understanding perception is critical to effective visualization design. With its low cost and scalability, crowdsourcing presents an attractive option for evaluating the large design space of visualizations; however, it first requires validation."
- Replication: 50 subjects per chart type, 70 trials each, $0.05 per judgment; subjects estimated what percentage the smaller marked value was of the larger. "A total of 186 different Turkers participated in our experiments."
- Match with the original: results preserved "the rough shape and ranking of judgment types by accuracy (T1-5)". "The ranking of types by accuracy is consistent between the two experiments." "Types 1 and 2 are closer in the crowdsourced study; this may be a result of a smaller display mitigating the effect of distance." Summary: "Our new results match previous work, are consistent with theoretical predictions [21], and suggest that crowdsourcing is viable for testing graphical perception."
- New area results: "The results confirm our hypothesis that, on average, the accuracy of rectangular area judgments matches that of circular area judgments" but "Somewhat surprisingly, comparisons of rectangles with aspect ratio 1 exhibited the worst performance." Hence "viewers actually benefit from the inability of a squarified treemap algorithm to perfectly optimize the rectangles to 1:1 aspect ratios."
- Design numbers: "gridlines be separated by at least 8 pixels apart and increasing chart heights beyond 80 pixels provides little accuracy benefit on a 0-100 scale." Cost: "a cost savings factor of 6".
- Conclusion: "crowdsourced perception experiments are viable and contribute new insights for visualization design."
What it establishes: an independent replication, 26 years later, on a different population and medium, of the top of the hierarchy (position > length > angle); extends to area (rectangles ≈ circles; squares worst).
Strength: 186 participants, peer-reviewed CHI; the replication is of T1–T5 (position/length types) and angle, not of the whole hierarchy. The fetched text did not contain a Stevens'-law discussion.
Role: LOAD-BEARING (the replication).

1.4 Talbot, J., Setlur, V. & Anand, A. (2014). "Four Experiments on the Perception of Bar Charts." IEEE TVCG 20(12).
URL read: http://vis.cs.ucdavis.edu/vis2014papers/TVCG/papers/2152_20tvcg12-talbot-2346320.pdf
How read: AT SOURCE.
Exact quotations / figures: four Mechanical Turk experiments, 50 participants each (46 retained in Exp 1). Separating two bars "increases error by approximately 0.70 [0.46, 0.99] percentage points"; unaligned (stacked) comparisons add "1.16 [0.80, 1.61] percentage points"; distractors in stacked charts add "0.50 [0.14, 0.91]". "Separation makes comparison of short bars particularly difficult." In divided bars, adjacent segments showed a large negative bias ("7.38 [6.65, 8.23] percentage points") which the authors attribute to subjects making "part-of-whole comparisons" rather than height ratios; introducing gaps "substantially attenuated" the bias. Responses cluster: error "minimums near 0%, 50%, and 100%"; 50% was answered "nearly 5 times as often as expected"; responses are mainly "multiples of 10, followed by multiples of 5". Closing: "even for simple visualizations, such as bar charts, we still do not have a complete understanding of what impacts chart perception."
What it establishes: WHY the Cleveland types differ — distance, alignment, distractors and part-of-whole confusion; it confirms the T1–T5 ordering while showing effects are small in percentage-point terms.
Strength: 4 × 50 crowdsourced participants, peer-reviewed TVCG, Bayesian CIs reported.
Role: SUPPORTING (also feeds the stacked-bar point in §5).

1.5 Kosara, R. & Skau, D. (2016). "Judgment Error in Pie Chart Variations." EuroVis 2016 short paper.
URL read: https://media.eagereyes.org/papers/2016/Kosara-EuroVis-2016.pdf (author copy, redirect from kosara.net)
How read: AT SOURCE.
Exact quotations/figures: "Pie charts and their variants are prevalent in business settings and many other uses, even if they are not popular with the academic community." 108 MTurk participants (107 analysed); blue slice values 3%–97%, random rotation. Mean log error (±95% CI): baseline pie 1.151±0.098; exploded 1.236±0.101; larger slice 1.338±0.096; square pie 1.487±0.097; ellipse pie 1.570±0.097. "None of our designs changed the central angle, yet they all led to considerable error. This further undermines the importance of angle as the key visual cue." On C&M: "Cleveland and McGill show that pie charts are less accurately readable than bar charts."
Role: LOAD-BEARING for "angle may not be how pies are read".

1.6 Skau, D. & Kosara, R. (2016). "Arcs, Angles, or Areas: Individual Data Encodings in Pie and Donut Charts." Computer Graphics Forum 35(3) (EuroVis).
URL read: https://media.eagereyes.org/papers/2016/Skau-EuroVis-2016.pdf
How read: AT SOURCE.
Exact quotations/figures: 102 MTurk participants (92 retained). Log absolute error: baseline pie 1.032±0.138; baseline donut 1.000±0.137; arc-only 1.294±0.128; angle-only pie 1.967±0.167; angle-only donut 2.279±0.157; area-only 1.306±0.125. Angle was "the least important visual cue for both charts"; "angle cannot be the only way we read a pie or donut chart." Donut showed "slightly lower log error than the baseline pie chart".
What it establishes: when angle is isolated people do worst; arc and area alone do nearly as well as a full pie; donuts are not worse than pies.
Strength: ~92 crowdsourced participants, peer-reviewed; two consistent studies from the same lab, plus 1.7.
Role: LOAD-BEARING.

1.7 Kosara, R. (2019). "Evidence for Area as the Primary Visual Cue in Pie Charts." IEEE VIS short paper.
URL read: https://media.eagereyes.org/papers/2019/Kosara-VISShort-2019.pdf
How read: AT SOURCE.
Quotations: 80 Prolific participants (43 women, 37 men); 3D parallel-projected pies at 90°, 60°, 30°, 15° so angle and arc distort while area is constant; participants matched a 2D reference by dragging a handle. "Area emerges as the most likely cue used to read pie charts." Caveat: "the possibility of pie chart users re-projecting the chart" cannot be excluded.
Role: SUPPORTING.

1.8 Davis, R., Pu, X., Ding, Y., Hall, B.D., Bonilla, K., Feng, M., Kay, M. & Harrison, L. (2022). "The Risks of Ranking: Revisiting Graphical Perception to Model Individual Differences." IEEE TVCG.
URL read: https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2022-perception-individual-differences.pdf (author/lab copy)
How read: AT SOURCE. (Not asked for — a thread I pulled.)
Quotations/figures: replication of Cleveland & McGill with Bayesian multilevel modelling; 200 MTurk recruits, 118 completed 1,200 trials, 109 retained; 130,800 judgments. "some people show patterns of accuracy that credibly deviate from the canonical rankings." The canonical ranking (Bar > Pie > Stacked Bar > Bubble) held for ~22% of participants; "around 20-25% of people consistently perform better with another chart type" than bar; "less than 40% of people are expected to share the canonical ranking of Bar best and Pie as second-best." "expected differences in performance across pie, bubble, and stacked-bar charts (1-1.5pp) is smaller than the expected differences between people (1.5-3pp)."
What it establishes: the population-level ranking survives, but the gaps between pie/bubble/stacked-bar are smaller than person-to-person variation; the authors argue against treating the ranking as a design rule.
Strength: large trial count, peer-reviewed; one study.
Role: SUPPORTING (the modern "what the hierarchy does not settle").

PLAIN STATEMENT FOR THE AUTHOR (what the hierarchy settles / doesn't):
- Settles: for estimating the ratio between two values, position on a common scale beats length, which beats angle; this has been replicated (Heer & Bostock 2010; Talbot 2014; Davis 2022). The effect is real but modest in absolute terms (a few percentage points of error).
- Does not settle: anything about memory, understanding, trust, preference or speed of getting the gist; the lower ranks (area, volume, colour) were never tested in the 1984 paper and were called "partly conjectural" by the authors in 1985; the mechanism by which pies are read is not angle (Kosara & Skau); and individual differences are larger than the pie/bubble/stacked-bar gaps (Davis 2022). Cleveland & McGill themselves wrote that if accuracy were the only goal "tables would be better".

2.1 Healey, C.G. & Enns, J.T. (2012). "Attention and Visual Memory in Visualization and Computer Graphics." IEEE TVCG 18(7): 1170–1188.
URL read: https://www.csc2.ncsu.edu/faculty/healey/download/tvcg.12a.pdf (author copy)
How read: AT SOURCE (≈107k chars; read 0–103k).
Exact quotations:
- "A fundamental goal of visualization is to produce images of data that support visual analysis, exploration, and discovery of novel insights."
- "Typically, tasks that can be performed on large multi-element displays in less than 200-250 milliseconds (msec) are considered preattentive."
- Conjunctions: "When a target is defined by the joint presence of two or more visual properties, it often cannot be found preattentively." "Figs. 1e and 1f show an example of these more difficult conjunction searches. The red circle target is made up of two features: red and circular."
- FIT: "Each feature map registers activity for a specific visual feature. When the visual system first sees an image, all the features are encoded in parallel into their respective maps."
- Change blindness: "an interruption in what is being seen—a blink, an eye saccade, or a blank screen—renders us 'blind' to significant changes." Inattentional blindness: viewers "can completely fail to perceive visually salient objects or activities".
- Design guidance: "The visual features assigned to different data attributes must take advantage of the strengths of our visual system, must be well suited to the viewer's analysis needs, and must not produce visual interference effects." "Feature hierarchies suggest that the most important data attributes should be displayed with the most salient visual features, to avoid situations where secondary data values mask the information the viewer wants to see."
- Feature list (Figure 2 as fetched): hue, intensity, orientation, size, curvature, direction of motion, binocular disparity, flicker (plus texture density).

3.1 Franconeri, S.L., Padilla, L.M., Shah, P., Zacks, J.M. & Hullman, J. (2021). "The Science of Visual Data Communication: What Works." Psychological Science in the Public Interest 22(3): 110–161. DOI 10.1177/15291006211051956. Open access.

Publisher summary page: https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/visual-data-communication.html
How read: AT SOURCE for roughly the first two-thirds (through "How to Design an Understandable Visualization → Use familiar designs"); the sections on uncertainty/risk and the closing summary were NOT reached because of the fetch-tool size cap, despite five attempts via three hosts. For the ending I rely on the APS publisher page (SECONDARY). Please have someone open the PDF directly for pp. ~140–161.

Abstract (verbatim, complete): "Effectively designed data visualizations allow viewers to use their powerful visual systems to understand patterns in data across science, education, health, and public policy. But ineffectively designed visualizations can cause confusion, misunderstanding, or even distrust—especially among viewers with low graphical literacy. We review research-backed guidelines for creating effective and intuitive visualizations oriented toward communicating data to students, coworkers, and the general public. We describe how the visual system can quickly extract broad statistics from a display, whereas poorly designed displays can lead to misperceptions and illusions. Extracting global statistics is fast, but comparing between subsets of values is slow. Effective graphics avoid taxing working memory, guide attention, and respect familiar conventions. Data visualizations can play a critical role in teaching and communication, provided that designers tailor those visualizations to their audience."
Verbatim quotations by section:
- Scope: "This review focuses on how to effectively design visualizations that communicate data to students and the general public." "We restrict our scope to quantitative visualizations, omitting discussion of qualitative visualizations of text data, diagrams, and processes." "We focus on research and prescriptions that are most relevant for communication to nonspecialist audiences."
- Why visualise: "Visualizations allow powerful processing of an entire two-dimensional rectangle of information at once, in stark contrast to the limitation of reading handfuls of symbolic numbers per second." Fig. 1: "Four sets of 11 numbers have identical statistics but dramatically different patterns, as revealed by the scatterplots below each column." "…the two-dimensional processing power of your visual system, the largest single processing system of the brain."
- Channel ranking: "The leftmost column of Figure 2 presents five of the channels that can depict metric data to the human visual system. This list is ordered by the typical precision with which a viewer can verbally state the ratios between the two values shown." "…the 1:7 ratio can be relatively precisely extracted for position, but that task is a bit tougher for area, and far more difficult for intensity, at the bottom of the list." "Because position is the clear winner for precision, visualization designers often prioritize the vertical and horizontal dimensions of two-dimensional space." Critical caveat: "The ranking of precision for visual channels depicted in the first column of Figure 2 is based on a measure of precision in a particular task: the average error in verbal reports of the ratio between two depicted values." And on generalising across tasks: "the existing pattern of results is currently too complex to generalize to novel combinations."
- Axis manipulation: "The second value appears to be roughly double the first value. Look more closely at the y-axis: The second value is only about 1% bigger than the first." Fig. 3: "Deceptive axis manipulations across a line graph (top left) and a bar graph (top right)." "Ratings in a zero-baseline condition averaged around 1.5, whereas ratings in a deceptive-baseline condition averaged 2.8."
- Which channel is read (pictograms): values "encoded by one-dimensional length (the height of each person), producing a 1:2 ratio" but "your estimate of the depicted values is determined instead by the area taken up by each person, leading to something closer to a 1:4 ratio"; "viewer judgments are more strongly determined by two-dimensional area".
- Line-graph illusion: "the two curves are identical (y = x³), but the darker line is translated vertically upward by a constant of 1,000"; "it is difficult to see that the vertical distance between the two lines is the same across their entire horizontal span"; "This illusion makes it difficult to visually estimate differences between lines, especially lines with steep slopes."
- Combining values (areas/stacked): "the eye is tempted to translate them into the aspect ratio and the area of each rectangle".
- Categorical perception / rainbow: "continuous values can be warped by categorical perception"; "The seven discrete colors that we see in a rainbow, which are not present in the rainbow itself"; the visual system "systematically bins continuous wavelengths into one of several perceptual categories, exaggerating metric differences"; "These additional hues create new color-category boundaries that can dramatically exaggerate the differences"; a blue-to-red scale "creates a salient categorical color boundary at the color transition point".
- Colour-vision: "Color-vision impairments are estimated to affect 4% of the global population, or roughly 300 million people." "protanopia, or red–green color blindness, is the most common". "The simplest way to make visualizations accessible to viewers with color blindness is to avoid using hue as the only encoding channel." (NB: the 4% global figure differs from the ~8%-of-men figure in Birch 2012, §4.5; the two are not inconsistent — one is all-population global, the other is men of European descent — but the book should pick one and say which.)
- Distinguishing groups: "Plotting the groups as categorically different colors is often the first choice because the visual system processes color differences more efficiently than shape differences across the two-dimensional visual plane." "…differentiating those classes is easier if the encoded colors are farther apart in a perceptual color space." "easier to differentiate red from blue than from orange-red". Shapes: "prioritize the difference between open (circle, square, triangle) and closed (×, +, *) shapes". "redundant encoding does not substantially improve visual processing efficiency". Semantic colours: "easier for viewers to answer questions about a chart that labels those values with yellow and red, compared with a standard palette".
- Statistics are fast: "The encoding properties in the first column of Figure 2 are properties that we process in parallel across two-dimensional images." "Faster than an eyeblink, we can pull statistics from data encoded as positions, one-dimensional lengths, two-dimensional areas, angles, or intensities."
- Comparison is slow: "Comparisons include local comparisons between elements, groups of elements, or global trends." "Each of those verbal sentences describes a single visual comparison that must be extracted serially, as part of a sequence." "comparisons are already highly capacity limited".
- Grouping/guiding: "A visualization designed to guide viewers to make the 'right' visual comparisons can lead those viewers to make meaningful insights." "The same classic grouping cues studied in the perception literature can control which values are selected and compared." "Visualization designers will often deliberately control visual salience to bring the viewer's eye straight to the critical comparison." "One technique is to add salient color highlighting a single group of items to ensure that viewers process that comparison first."
- Persuasion: "An analysis of distortions and biased annotations in news media visualizations showed that rhetorical biases are pervasive in data graphics." "People's perceptions of a visualization's message, and their ability to recall it, are particularly influenced by the visualization's title." Viewers "made unwarranted claims about similar correlational data, and they did so more often when the visualizations aggregated the data into fewer groups".
- Working memory / legends: "Interpreting the graphs… requires individuals to map the symbols and colors in the graphs to their referents in the legends below. This task is highly demanding of limited working memory resources." "One study found that people answered questions about data both faster and more accurately when the data were directly labeled in graphs compared with when there was a legend." "instead of legends, use direct labels whenever possible". "When visualizations are well designed, they can help viewers overcome working memory limits by offloading information storage and some types of processing to a page or screen." Older adults comparing health plans with a visually encoded table "made better and, in some cases, faster decisions".
- Animation: "Our capacity for tracking the motion of objects that move in arbitrary directions is highly limited, to as few as one or two objects at a time." "If a viewer's capacity is overwhelmed during an animation, they may not retain the motion information before the animation is over." "the transient information effect, a 'loss of learning due to information disappearing before the learner has time to adequately process it'". "students' descriptions of processes were no more accurate with animations than with labeled static diagrams". "Allowing a viewer to control an animation manually may prepare them to focus their limited capacity on the right subsets of information."
- Conventions/schemas: "Understanding a visualization can depend on a graph schema: a knowledge structure that includes default expectations, rules, and associations." "Reading a bar graph requires a schema that allows the viewer to do what you do when viewing a bar graph: to focus instantly on the positions of the bar tops, because they signal the numbers depicted by the bars." "Larger values tend to be mapped to higher vertical positions, perhaps because of the natural metaphor of stacking more objects in higher piles." "When these mappings are violated, viewers can become confused." "Even relatively common visualizations such as scatterplots are misunderstood by 37% of adults in the United States." "Expert communities have developed idiosyncratic visualizations that serve important data-analysis or -communication purposes, but they require learning new graph schemas." "Schemas can bias judgments when a new instance deviates from the schema."
- Stated gaps (as read): channel-ranking generality ("too complex to generalize to novel combinations"); the dissociation between position's precision for single values and intensity for aggregate judgments "is not well understood"; task taxonomies need underlying perceptual models; literacy measurement understudied.
Publisher summary of the un-reached end (SECONDARY, APS page): guidelines fall under "1) proper mapping of visualized values to concepts, 2) conveying uncertainty and risk, and 3) communicating clearly"; "When communicating risk to audiences who may have a lower ability to work with numbers and mathematics, rely on absolute instead of relative rates"; companion commentary by Jonathan Schwabish, "The Practice of Visual Data Communication: What Works" (PSPI 22(3): 97–109) with "five practical principles" — the SAGE page for it was access-restricted so I could not quote the five.
What it establishes: the chapter spine — three organising claims (perceptual accuracy; perceptual efficiency: statistics fast, comparisons slow; cognitive accessibility: working memory, attention, conventions).
Strength: narrative review by five leading researchers in APS's flagship review journal, peer-reviewed, open access; it is a synthesis, not new data.
Role: LOAD-BEARING.

5.1 Correll, M., Bertini, E. & Franconeri, S. (2020). "Truncating the Y-Axis: Threat or Menace?" CHI 2020.
URLs read: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.02035 and https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1907.02035
How read: AT SOURCE (author preprint).
Quotations/figures: "Bar charts with y-axes that don't begin at zero can visually exaggerate effect sizes." Three Prolific experiments, within-subjects: Exp 1 N=40 (bar v line × value/trend framing × truncation 0/25/50%): "Increased y-axis truncation results in increased perceived severity (F(2,76) = 89, p < 0.0001)"; bar v line not different (p = 0.50). Exp 2 N=32 (plain bar v broken axis v gradient): "There was no significant difference between perceived severity among visualization designs (F(2,60) = 3.1, p = 0.05)." Exp 3 N=25 (plus value estimation): "Perceived severity was significantly different across levels of truncation (F(1,20) = 11, p = 0.003)"; trend-estimation error unaffected (p = 0.96). Discussion: "Different designs might provide more visual indications that this exaggeration is occurring, but did not substantially alter the reported impact of the exaggeration." "Merely indicating that truncation has occurred, even in a prominent and unambiguous way, may not be sufficient to 'de-bias' viewers of truncated charts." "The accurate estimation of values does not seem to counteract the visual magnification of difference." "We reject the unequivocal dichotomy of 'honest' and 'dishonest' charts". Recommendation: designers should "consider the scale of the meaningful effect sizes and variation they intend to communicate, regardless of the visual encoding." "There is no obvious way for designers to relinquish the responsibility of considering effect size in their charts."
Answer to the brief's question: NO — a break marker or gradient did not remove the exaggeration; people could still read the numbers correctly yet still felt the difference was bigger.
Strength: three small (25–40) within-subject experiments, peer-reviewed CHI, consistent direction; converges with 5.2.

Verbatim opening (as quoted at https://sql-troubles.blogspot.com/2020/07/data-management-numeracy-quotes.html, SECONDARY): "Numeracy has two facets - reading and writing, or extracting numerical information and presenting it. The skills of data presentation may at first seem ad hoc and judgemental, a matter of style rather than of technology, but certain aspects can be formalized into explicit rules, the equivalent of elementary syntax."
The six rules as summarised at https://habr.com/en/articles/54032 (SECONDARY; paraphrase, not quotation): (1) round to two significant (effective) figures; (2) include row/column averages; (3) put figures to be compared in columns — "the eye finds it much easier to scan down a column than across a row"; (4) order rows/columns by size; (5) layout/spacing to guide the eye; (6) graphs for qualitative shape, tables for quantities. Kozak et al. 2011 (Australian Journal of Crop Science, http://www.cropj.com/kozak_5_13_2011_1876_1881.pdf, AT SOURCE, peer-reviewed) confirms rule 1's wording: Ehrenberg suggests "using two significant or effective digits for reporting numerical values", "effective digits" meaning the digits that vary within the data; Kozak concludes "three effective digits" are "often acceptable" in biology. Cleveland & McGill 1984 (at source) cite Ehrenberg approvingly on tables for precise lookup.
Modern tests: the annotated bibliography at https://pages.graphics.cs.wisc.edu/Tables/designAndUses.html (UW-Madison, SECONDARY) says of Ehrenberg 1977: "asserted without experimental results". Wainer, H. (1997). "Improving Tabular Displays, With NAEP Tables as Examples and Inspirations." JEBS 22(1): 1–30 — ERIC abstract (https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ546737, ABSTRACT-ONLY): "Four guidelines that make tables more effective data displays are presented. The need for these guidelines and their application are illustrated with data from the National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP)." Wainer's 1995 ETS report on display methods (Wiley, blocked) is the one most likely to contain reader-testing. I found no controlled experiment testing Ehrenberg's rules with readers — state the rules as expert craft, not as evidence.
Evidence on significant figures people use/remember: none found at source beyond Talbot et al. 2014's observation that chart readers answer in "multiples of 10, followed by multiples of 5" — i.e., people themselves round to ~2 effective digits when estimating. That is the closest empirical hook.
Role: LOAD-BEARING (practice) but weakly evidenced.

WHAT SURPRISED ME
======================================================================
- Cleveland & McGill never tested the bottom of their own hierarchy and said so ("partly conjectural… no controlled experimentation"); the famous diagram is half experiment, half hypothesis.
- Pies are not read by angle. Three studies from one lab point to area (or arc), and angle-only stimuli are the worst of all encodings tested.
- A broken-axis marker does not help: people estimate the numbers correctly and still judge the difference as more severe (Correll); teaching people about truncation does not help either (Yang: 83.5% still show the effect).
- 3D bars cost almost nothing in the study everyone cites; the real culprit was the neighbouring bars (Zacks 1998).
- The dual-axis prohibition rests on no controlled study I could find; the one experiment cited for it is about a different chart.
- The sole experimental number for "how many colours" is seven (Healey 1996, for rapid search), and the commonly quoted "4% of the population" (Franconeri) and "8% of men" (Birch) are different denominators, not a disagreement.
- Ehrenberg's table rules, repeated for fifty years, appear never to have been tested with readers; the nearest empirical support is incidental (people round to multiples of 5 and 10 when reading charts).

--- 1.1 Borkin, Bylinskii, Kim, Bainbridge, Yeh, Borkin, Pfister & Oliva (2016 print; presented VIS 2015). "Beyond Memorability: Visualization Recognition and Recall." IEEE TVCG 22(1): 519–528.
URL read: http://vcg.seas.harvard.edu/files/pfister/files/infovis_submission251-camera.pdf (Harvard VCG author copy)
How read: at source (full PDF).
Exact quotations:
• Abstract: "In this paper we move beyond memorability and investigate how visualizations are recognized and recalled. For this study we labeled a dataset of 393 visualizations and analyzed the eye movements of 33 participants as well as thousands of participant-generated text descriptions of the visualizations. This allowed us to determine what components of a visualization attract people's attention, and what information is encoded into memory. Our findings quantitatively support many conventional qualitative design guidelines, including that (1) titles and supporting text should convey the message of a visualization, (2) if used appropriately, pictograms do not interfere with understanding and can improve recognition, and (3) redundancy helps effectively communicate the message. Importantly, we show that visualizations memorable "at-a-glance" are also capable of effectively conveying the message of the visualization. Thus, a memorable visualization is often also an effective one."
• Method: "A total of 33 participants (17 females, 16 males) participated in the experiment"; "SR Research EyeLink1000 desktop eye-tracking system with a chin-rest mount 22 inches from a 19 inch CRT monitor"; encoding phase 10 s per visualization, recognition 2 s, recall with blurred images and written descriptions.
• "People spend the most amount of time looking at the text in a visualization, and more specifically, the title."
• "When participants were shown visualizations with titles during encoding, the titles were fixated 59% of the time." "Titles were also more likely to be fixated (76% of the time) when present at the top of the visualization than when present at the bottom (fixated 63% of the time)."
• "the elements with the highest total fixation time during recognition are the titles (M = 274ms) followed by human recognizable objects (M = 246ms, p < 0.01)."
• "the elements that were refixated the most were the legend, table header row...and title." "the elements that were fixated the longest were the paragraph, legend, and header row."
• Recall: "46% of the time the participants described the contents, or rewording, of the title." "The average description quality of visualizations with titles is 1.90 as compared to 1.30 for visualizations without titles."
• Redundancy defined: "A visualization exhibits data redundancy if the data being presented is visually encoded in more than one way...or the use of channels such as color, size, or opacity to represent a value already exhibited in a visualization such as the x- or y-axis values. In contrast, a visualization exhibits message redundancy if the main conclusion or message of the visualization is explicitly presented to the viewer in multiple ways: the addition of explanatory annotations, labels, text, and pictures."
• "The average description quality is higher for visualizations that contain message redundancy (M = 1.99) than for visualizations that do not (M = 1.59)." "Of the visualizations in the top third of description quality ranks, 57% contain message redundancy and 34% contain data redundancy."
• Pictograms: "the average description quality for visualizations with pictograms (2.01) is statistically higher than for visualizations without pictograms (1.50, p< 0.001)." "Pictograms do not hinder the memory or understanding of a visualization."
• Discussion: "Titles and text are key elements in a visualization and help recall the message." "Pictograms can often serve as visual hooks into memory, allowing a visualization to be retrieved from memory more effectively."
• Limitations: blurred-image recall "has its limitations, including the potential to bias participants to more easily recall visual elements"; the authors say the correlational findings "cannot make any causality assumptions."
What it establishes: on static infographic-style visualizations, people look at text—above all the title—more than anything else; what they later write down is very often the title reworded; adding text that restates the message ("message redundancy") raises the quality of what is recalled.
Strength: 33 lab participants, 393 stimuli, high-grade eye tracker; peer-reviewed TVCG; correlational on naturally occurring stimuli (titles were not experimentally manipulated). Stimuli are infographics/news/scientific figures, not dashboards.
Role: LOAD-BEARING.

--- 1.2 Borkin, Vo, Bylinskii, Isola, Sunkavalli, Oliva & Pfister (2013). "What Makes a Visualization Memorable?" IEEE TVCG 19(12): 2306–2315.
URL read: http://olivalab.mit.edu/Papers/Borkin_etal_MemorableVisualization_TVCG2013.pdf (MIT author copy)
How read: at source.
Exact quotations:
• Abstract (full): "An ongoing debate in the Visualization community concerns the role that visualization types play in data understanding. In human cognition, understanding and memorability are intertwined. As a first step towards being able to ask questions about impact and effectiveness, here we ask: 'What makes a visualization memorable?' We ran the largest scale visualization study to date using 2,070 single-panel visualizations, categorized with visualization type (e.g., bar chart, line graph, etc.), collected from news media sites, government reports, scientific journals, and infographic sources. Each visualization was annotated with additional attributes, including ratings for data-ink ratios and visual densities. Using Amazon's Mechanical Turk, we collected memorability scores for hundreds of these visualizations, and discovered that observers are consistent in which visualizations they find memorable and forgettable. We find intuitive results (e.g., attributes like color and the inclusion of a human recognizable object enhance memorability) and less intuitive results (e.g., common graphs are less memorable than unique visualization types). Altogether our findings suggest that quantifying memorability is a general metric of the utility of information, an essential step towards determining how to design effective visualizations."
• Method: 261 MTurk participants; memory game, each image shown 1 s with 1.4 s gaps; ~87 responses per target image. Consistency: Spearman rank correlation across participant halves ≈ 0.81 for d′.
• "Visualizations containing pictograms have on average a higher memorability score (Mean (M)=1.93) than visualizations without pictograms (M = 1.14,t(297) = 13.67, p < 0.001)."
• "We also observed a statistically significant effect of the data-to-ink ratio attribute on memorability scores with a 'bad' (M = 1.81), i.e., low data-to-ink ratio, being higher than a 'good' rating (M = 1.23,t(208) = 6.92, p < 0.001)."
• "More surprisingly, we found that unique visualization types (pictoral, grid/matrix, trees and networks, and diagrams) had significantly higher memorability scores than common graphs (circles, area, points, bars, and lines)."
• "Thus not all chart junk is created equal: annotations and representations containing pictograms are across the board more memorable."
• The caveat, in the authors' own words: "Clearly, a more memorable visualization is not necessarily a more comprehensible one." "A few things to bear in mind: first of all, as previously stated, this memorability study examined the memorability of visualizations as if they were images and not memorability based on engagement and comprehension of the visualization." "We do not want just any part of the visualization to stick (e.g., chart junk), but rather we want the most important relevant aspects of the data or trend the author is trying to convey to stick." "Understanding what makes a visualization memorable is only the first step to understanding how to create effective data presentations."
What it establishes: image-level memorability (do you recognise you've seen this before, after a one-second glance) is a stable property; more colours, pictograms, low data-ink ratio and unusual chart forms make a picture more memorable. It says nothing about whether the data were understood. Bar and line charts—the dashboard's staple—are the least memorable as images.
Strength: large stimulus set, crowdsourced, replicable (dataset public, MASSVIS); peer-reviewed. Measures recognition of the image, not content.
Role: LOAD-BEARING for the "memorable ≠ comprehensible" distinction; SUPPORTING for ornament.

--- 1.3 Bateman, Mandryk, Gutwin, Genest, McDine & Brooks (2010). "Useful Junk? The Effects of Visual Embellishment on Comprehension and Memorability of Charts." CHI 2010.
URL read: https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/communication/Bateman2010.pdf (course copy hosted by Columbia; author PDF mirrors at hci.usask.ca were not reachable)
How read: at source (full PDF).
Exact quotations:
• "Twenty participants (9 male, 11 female), aged between 18 and 40 were recruited from a local university (7 graduate, 13 undergraduate)."
• "The charts were digitized and formatted to be displayable on a computer monitor as full-screen slides, yielding 14 charts, each with a Holmes version and a plain version."
• "After the reading and description task, participants were placed in one of two recall conditions (immediate recall or long-term recall), with ten participants in each." "The long-term participants scheduled their second part of the study for two to three weeks later."
• Accuracy (immediate description): no significant difference for subject (t19=0.84, p=.412), categories (t19=1.38, p=.185), trend (t19=0.23, p=.818). "There were no differences in the scores of chart descriptions for the subject of the chart... depending on whether or not visual embellishments were present."
• Long-term recall: "For participants in the long-term recall group, there were significant differences in recall scores for the subject of the chart (t9=2.56, p=.015), the categories in the chart (t9=5.03, p≈.000), the trend of the chart (t9=1.95, p=.042), and the value message (t9=2.41, p=.020)."
• Value message: participants identified a value message significantly more often in Holmes charts (t19=3.37, p=.003). "Participants saw value messages in the Holmes charts more often because the images do often convey values, and this was likely the intention of the designer."
• Eye tracking: "Participants spent 67% of their on-screen time looking at data or data and embellishments for Holmes charts as compared to 78% for Plain charts." About 13% of time on purely decorative embellishments.
• Preference: "Participants also found it easiest to remember the charts with visual embellishments...and to remember the details of these charts."
• Conclusion: "Although we are cautious about proposing specific design recommendations, it seems clear that there is more to be learned about the effects of different types of visual embellishment in charts. Our results question some of the premises of the minimalist approach to chart design, and raise issues for designers about how charts are designed and used in different publications and different contexts."
• Limitations the authors state: they used "the most extreme type of visual embellishment"; "further study is needed to consider other aspects of this practice before drawing strong conclusions; such as different tasks." The paper contains no statement about small-sample power.
What it establishes: with Nigel Holmes's heavily illustrated charts (each a single, simple data series), description accuracy was no worse, and after 2–3 weeks the ten long-term participants recalled subject/categories/trend better.
Strength: WEAK-TO-MODERATE. n=20 overall, n=10 in the key long-term condition; students; 14 very simple charts; Holmes charts are an extreme, metaphor-driven style. Note for the author: t(9)=1.95 gives a two-tailed p ≈ .08, so the reported p=.042 for "trend" is one-tailed—only subject and categories survive a two-tailed reading. Peer-reviewed (CHI best-paper nominee), heavily cited, and widely over-read.
Role: SUPPORTING (as the trigger of a debate, not as a settled result).

URL read: https://people.ischool.berkeley.edu/~hearst/papers/tvcg2022.pdf (author copy)
How read: at source.
Exact quotations:
• Abstract (full): "While visualizations are an effective way to represent insights about information, they rarely stand alone. When designing a visualization, text is often added to provide additional context and guidance for the reader. • Design guidelines (as summarised in the paper): annotate with multiple text elements rather than minimalist; match semantic content to the message; place text by semantic level (L1 axis labels, L2/L4 point annotations, L3 title); consider a text-only alternative.
• Limitations: univariate synthetic line charts; crowdsourced.

URL read: https://www.hidykong.com/papers/Kong-CHI18.pdf (author copy)
How read: at source.
Exact quotations:
• Abstract (full): "Slanted framing in news article titles induce bias and influence recall. While recent studies found that viewers focus extensively on titles when reading visualizations, the impact of titles on visualization interpretation remains underexplored. "the attitude-consistency of the title had no influence on the perceived level of bias (χ² = 3.896, df = 4, p = 0.42)."
• Recall paradox: "33% of the participants (N=34) stated that they did not remember any of the titles and 9% (N=9) stated that they did not remember one of the two titles"—yet many had "already written the content of the title as the main message." 3. DIRECT LABELLING AGAINST LEGENDS
=====================================================================
VERDICT: I found NO controlled study in the visualisation literature that directly pits direct labels against a legend and measures accuracy, speed or recall. "Label at the mark" is PRACTICE, resting on three indirect lines of evidence: (a) eye-tracking showing that legends are re-fixated and that legend-reading costs gaze transitions; (b) the split-attention/spatial-contiguity literature in educational psychology (a large meta-analytic effect for placing text next to its referent); (c) one TVCG experiment in which "legends converted to direct labels" was bundled into a "declutter" manipulation. Details:

--- 3.1 Huestegge & Philipp (2011). "Effects of spatial compatibility on integration processes in graph comprehension." Attention, Perception & Psychophysics 73: 1903–1915.
URL read: https://www.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020330/Methoden/Publikationen/Effects_of_spatial..._APP.pdf (author/institutional copy)
How read: at source.
Exact quotations: "A precondition for efficiently understanding and memorizing graphs is the integration of all relevant graph elements and their meaning. In the present study, we analyzed integration processes by manipulating the spatial compatibility between elements in the data region and the legend." Four experiments (n = 18, 16, 18, 12 students). "gaze transitions between data region and legend were more frequent in incompatible (M = 4.37) than in compatible (M = 3.63) graphs." "gaze transitions from legend to data region occurred more often...than gaze transitions from data region to legend". Result: "data–legend compatibility reduced the time needed to understand graphs, as well as the time needed to retrieve relevant graph information from memory." They describe Carpenter & Shah (1998) as modelling "an integration process [that] relates meanings to semantic referents defined by labels, legends, or titles" evidenced by "frequent gaze transitions between graph elements".
What it establishes: reading a legend costs ~3.5–4.5 round trips per simple graph, and misordering the legend relative to the lines adds measurable time. Direct labels remove the round trip; this study did not test that directly. (Follow-up: Riechelmann & Huestegge 2018, same journal, extends to multiple graphs—not fetched.)
Strength: small lab samples, peer-reviewed, eye tracking; indirect.
Role: SUPPORTING.

--- 3.2 Borkin et al. 2015 (see 1.1): "the elements that were refixated the most were the legend, table header row...and title"; Matzen et al. 2017 (see 4.4): "Users are also more likely to re-fixate text-based areas, such as the legend." Both show the legend is a place the eye keeps returning to—i.e. a lookup table, not a one-pass read.

--- 3.3 Ginns (2006). "Integrating information: A meta-analysis of the spatial contiguity and temporal contiguity effects." Learning and Instruction 16: 511–525.
URL read: https://www.academia.edu/1400902/Integrating_information_A_meta_analysis_of_the_spatial_contiguity_and_temporal_contiguity_effects
How read: at source (full text on Academia page).
Exact quotations: "Meta-analytic techniques were applied to 50 independent studies yielding performance data." Weighted mean effect d = 0.85 overall (2,375 students); spatial contiguity d = 0.72 (95% CI 0.61–0.82, 37 comparisons); larger for high element-interactivity material (d = 0.78) than low (d = 0.28). Conclusion: "for complex learning materials in particular, increasing either the spatial or temporal contiguity" improves learning.
What it establishes: putting words physically next to the thing they explain, rather than in a separate key or caption, has a large, replicated effect on comprehension in instructional materials. This is the strongest general evidence behind direct labelling, though it was not measured on charts with legends specifically.
Strength: meta-analysis of 50 studies; peer-reviewed; educational psychology domain.
Role: LOAD-BEARING for the principle, by analogy.

--- 3.4 Ajani, Lee, Xiong, Knaflic, Kemper & Franconeri (2022). "Declutter and Focus: Empirically Evaluating Design Guidelines for Effective Data Communication." IEEE TVCG 28(10).
URL read: https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10350268 (NSF public-access copy)
How read: at source.
In their "decluttered" condition the manipulation included: removed backgrounds, colours, borders, gridlines, tick marks; "converted legends to direct labels". So the legend→label change was tested, but confounded with everything else in "declutter". Results in §6.2 below.

--- 6.2 Ajani et al. (2022) "Declutter and Focus" (full citation at 3.4). At source.
Exact quotations: Abstract (full): "Data visualization design has a powerful effect on which patterns we see as salient and how quickly we see them. The visualization practitioner community prescribes two popular guidelines for creating clear and efficient visualizations: declutter and focus. The declutter guidelines suggest removing non-critical gridlines, excessive labeling of data values, and color variability to improve aesthetics and to maximize the emphasis on the data relative to the design itself. We evaluated how these recommendations impact recall of the depicted information across cluttered, decluttered, and decluttered+focused designs of six graph topics. Undergraduate students were asked to redraw previously seen visualizations, to recall their topics and main conclusions, and to rate the varied designs on aesthetics, clarity, professionalism, and trustworthiness. Decluttering designs led to higher ratings on professionalism, and adding focus to the design led to higher ratings on aesthetics and clarity. They also showed better memory for the highlighted pattern in the data, as reflected across redrawings of the original visualization and typed free-response conclusions, though we do not know whether these results would generalize beyond our memory-based tasks. The results largely empirically validate the intuitions of visualization designers and practitioners."
"Participants were 2.5-3x more likely to recall a focus-relevant conclusion for focused designs relative to the non-focused designs". "neither decluttering alone nor the addition of focusing showed strong evidence for improving trustworthiness of a graph". "Compared to the focus guideline, we found weaker but generally positive evidence for the benefits of the declutter guideline". What it establishes: this is the cleanest EVIDENCE (not claim) that a message headline + one highlight colour + a one-sentence annotation changes what people take away and remember; stripping ornament alone mostly changes how professional the chart looks.
Strength: small n (24), within-subjects, memory tasks; peer-reviewed TVCG; co-authored by a leading practitioner (Knaflic) and a perception lab (Franconeri).
Role: LOAD-BEARING.

--- 6.3 Kim, Setlur & Agrawala (2021). "Towards Understanding How Readers Integrate Charts and Captions: A Case Study with Line Charts." CHI 2021.

THREADS PULLED (not asked for)

1. Dashboard-specific eye tracking exists and is recent: Yang et al. 2025 "Dashboard Vision" (60 participants, 1,216 dashboards) — text gets ~43% of attention despite its small area, numbers are the single most-looked-at object class, subtitles outdraw titles, attention starts upper-left and at the title/filters. Plus Sultanum & Setlur 2026 preprint (65.6% of reading flows start at the title). These supersede the F-pattern folklore for this book.
2. Matzen et al. 2017 (Sandia) reanalysed Borkin's eye data: title among first three fixations in 87.8% of scanpaths; data overtakes title after ~1.5 s.
3. Ajani et al. 2022 "Declutter and Focus" is the missing experiment the chartjunk debate needed: decluttering alone → "professional"; adding a message headline + highlight + annotation → 2.5–3× recall of the intended conclusion. Trust unmoved by either.
4. Ginns 2006 meta-analysis (d = 0.72, 37 comparisons) is the real evidential root of "put the label by the mark"; the visualisation field has never run the direct legend-vs-label test.
5. Haroz 2015 + Skau 2015 + Borgo 2012 together reconcile Bateman: ornament that IS data is free or helpful; ornament that distorts the mark (rounded/triangular bars) or sits behind the data (background images) costs accuracy or time; memory may still go up.
6. Kong 2019's trust ordering: readers rate the data most credible, then the chart, then the title—yet recall the title's message. The least-trusted component carries the most.
7. Kim, Setlur & Agrawala 2021 (n = 2,168): captions can only redirect readers to features the chart already makes prominent. Text and salience must agree.

WHAT SURPRISED ME
=====================================================================
• The Bateman "useful junk" result that launched a decade of argument rests on ten people in the long-term condition, and one of its four significant long-term effects (trend, t(9)=1.95, p=.042) is only significant one-tailed. Few's critique, for all its tone, was right about the sample.
• The authorship error in the brief: the title papers are Kong, Liu & Karahalios, not Agrawala. Easy to propagate; worth a footnote-level check in the manuscript.
• Nobody has run the obvious experiment—legend versus direct label on an ordinary chart. The whole practice stands on legend re-fixation counts and a meta-analysis from instructional design. The book should say so.
• Readers trust the title least and remember it most (Kong 2019). In the same vein, Kong 2018's participants said they did not remember the title while writing it down as the chart's message.
• In Stokes 2023, the no-text and title-only charts ranked lowest—title alone is not enough; readers wanted the annotations at the data.
• Ajani 2022: neither decluttering nor focusing moved trustworthiness ratings at all. Clean does not read as honest; it reads as professional.
• Dashboard Vision: subtitles (the specific label under each tile) drew proportionally more attention than the dashboard's titles—the small words next to the thing beat the big words above everything.
• The ONS post, often cited as evidence for message titles, cites no study and reports no outcome; its only number is that bulletins take 9.5× longer to read than people spend on them.
• Tuch 2012: "prototypicality"—looking like what it is supposed to be—drives appeal as strongly as simplicity by 50 ms. For dashboards this argues for convention over novelty at first glance, a point the chartjunk literature never makes.

### 4.1 Tversky, Morrison & Bétrancourt 2002, "Animation: can it facilitate?", IJHCS 57(4):247–262
- URL read: https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs448b/papers/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf · AT SOURCE.
- Abstract verbatim: "Graphics have been used since ancient times to portray things that are inherently spatiovisual, like maps and building plans. Effective graphics conform to the Congruence Principle according to which the content and format of the graphic should correspond to the content and format of the concepts to be conveyed. - Figures: 36 participants (18 analysis, 18 presentation). "Overall, accuracy was quite low in this study, averaging 65%." "Small Multiples was significantly more accurate than Animation" (p < .001); traces intermediate. Time — presentation: animation 15.80 s, small multiples 25.30 s, traces 27.80 s; analysis: animation 83.10 s, small multiples 45.69 s, traces 55.01 s. Subjective (7-pt): animation enjoyment 4.3, excitement 4.3; small multiples enjoyment 3.7; traces 3.5. "Despite the subjects finding trend animation to be more fun and exciting, they only slightly preferred it to the static depictions." Recommendation: "An analyst should consider examining their data with a small multiples view, but then presenting it with animation."
- Strength: controlled lab experiment, n = 36, peer-reviewed; replicated in spirit on phones by Brehmer et al. 2019 (§1.10: small multiples faster). Role: LOAD-BEARING.

### 4.3 Heer & Robertson 2007, "Animated Transitions in Statistical Data Graphics", IEEE TVCG 13(6) (InfoVis 2007)
- URL read: https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2007-AnimatedTransitions-InfoVis.pdf · AT SOURCE.
- Abstract opens: "In this paper we investigate the effectiveness of animated transitions between common statistical data graphics such as bar charts, pie charts, and scatter plots." 24 subjects per experiment. Exp 1 (object tracking): animation beat static transitions in all conditions (p < .001); staged beat direct animation for zoom/filter (p = .026) and timestep scatterplot (p = .002). Exp 2 (value-change estimation): animation better for scatterplots and grouped bars (p < .001); static transitions produced more "unknown" answers, especially with axis rescaling. Conclusion: "Animation improved graphical perception at both syntactic (object tracking) and semantic (change estimation) levels of analysis," via "object constancy for changing objects". Design principles: congruence (maintain valid graphics, consistent mappings, semantic correspondence, avoid ambiguity) and apprehension (group similar transitions, minimise occlusion, maximise predictability, simple transitions, stage complex ones).
- Establishes: the one place animation reliably helps is a transition between two states of the same chart, where it preserves identity of marks. Strength: controlled, n = 24, peer-reviewed. Role: LOAD-BEARING.

### 1.1 Padilla, Kay & Hullman — "Uncertainty Visualization" (review chapter)
- Citation: Padilla L., Kay M., Hullman J. "Uncertainty Visualization." Chapter for Wiley (published in *Wiley StatsRef / Computational Statistics in Data Science*, 2021/2022). The copy read is the authors' circulated preprint, headed "Handbook of Computational Statistics and Data Science, Chapter X by Lace Padilla, Matthew Kay, Jessica Hullman — Circulation limited to personal use, see published chapter for wide circulation and citation."
- URL read: https://friendly.github.io/6135/papers/Uncertainty_Visualization_Padilla_Kay_Hullman_2020.pdf (course mirror of the author preprint; the Wiley abstract page is https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781118445112.stat08296)
- How read: at source (author preprint, full text).
- Exact quotations:
  - "Uncertainty is inherent to most data and can enter the analysis pipeline during the measurement, modeling, and forecasting phases."
  - Taxonomy — two families plus hybrids: "Graphical annotations that can be used to show properties of a distribution, such as the mean, confidence/credible intervals, and distributional moments." / "Mapping probability or confidence to a visual encoding channel...such as color, position, and transparency." / "Marks and encodings that both communicate uncertainty can be combined to create hybrid approaches."
  - Frequency framing: "Showing distributional information in a frequency format (e.g., 1 out of 10 rather than 10%) more naturally matches how people think about uncertainty." HOPs "are random draws from a distribution that are presented in an animated sequence."
  - Named errors: deterministic construal error — "When individuals attempt to substitute visual uncertainty information for deterministic information." Boundary/containment: "Ranges that are represented by boundaries lead people to believe that data inside and outside the boundary are categorically different." On the hurricane cone: "viewers believe areas inside the cone are categorically different from areas outside the cone." Within-the-bar bias (Newman & Scholl): "If the data point fell within the bar, the participants were more likely to believe that it came from the represented population. However, they believed that data points that were the same distance from the mean but located just outside the bar were from a different population."
  - Evidence summary: quantile dotplots "reduce the variance of probabilistic estimates compared to density plots"; HOPs "can outperform static error bars [10, 57], icon arrays [58], line ensembles [57], and violin plots [10]."
  - Intervals: "visualizations of intervals have widely documented issues (e.g., [3, 9, 21], such as provoking viewers to incorrectly think of distributional data as categorical."
  - Recommendation: "There is no single visualization technique we endorse, but there are some that should be critically considered before employing them." / "If a designer needs to show an interval, we also recommend displaying information that is more representative, such as a scatterplot, violin plot, gradient plot, ensemble plot, quantile dotplot, or HOP." / "There is no one-size-fits-all uncertainty visualization approach...visualization designers must think carefully about each of their design choices."
- Establishes: the field's own taxonomy (annotation vs encoding vs hybrid; frequency-based formats as a distinct class) and its own list of known misreadings (deterministic construal, boundary/containment, within-the-bar).
- Strength: authoritative review by the three most-cited authors in the sub-field; no new data of its own.
- Role: LOAD-BEARING (the organising frame for the whole chapter).

### 1.2 Hullman, Resnick & Adar 2015 — Hypothetical Outcome Plots (PLOS ONE)
- URL: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0142444 — at source, full text.
- Quotations:
  - Abstract: "Many visual depictions of probability distributions, such as error bars, are difficult for users to accurately interpret. We present and study an alternative representation, Hypothetical Outcome Plots (HOPs), that animates a finite set of individual draws."
  - "With HOPs, people made much more accurate judgments about plots of two and three quantities. Accuracy was similar with all three representations for most questions about distributions of a single quantity."
  - "For all three two variable plots, subjects who used HOPs had much lower MAE than those used violin plots and error bars" (reported p < 0.001).
  - Three-variable: "Subjects who used HOPs had lower MAE than those who used violin plots and error bars" (p < 0.001).
  - Conclusion: "HOPs lead to more accurate estimates of properties even of very simple multivariate distributions consisting of just two and three variables, both when the variables are independent or correlated."
  - Setup: "288 subjects" (Mechanical Turk); nine trials (four univariate, four bivariate asking P(B > A), one trivariate); stimuli from "5000 draws from a normal distribution."
  - Caveat in the paper: HOPs did worse for estimating the mean of high-variance single distributions.
- Establishes: when the question is "which of two things is more likely to be bigger," animated draws beat error bars and violins; for single-quantity questions, no advantage.
- Strength: strong (large pre-registered-style crowd experiment, open data), but MTurk sample and a single stimulus family.
- Role: LOAD-BEARING.

### 1.3 Kay, Kola, Hullman & Munson 2016 — "When (ish) is My Bus?" (CHI)
- URL: https://users.eecs.northwestern.edu/~jhullman/busUncertaintyVis.pdf (author copy; mjskay.com copy was rate-limited) — at source.
- Quotations:
  - "Users often rely on realtime predictions in everyday contexts like riding the bus, but may not grasp that such predictions are subject to uncertainty."
  - "We present several candidate visualizations of uncertainty for realtime transit predictions in a mobile context, and we propose a novel discrete representation of continuous outcomes designed for small screens, quantile dotplots."
  - "In a controlled experiment we find that quantile dotplots reduce the variance of probabilistic estimates by ~1.15 times compared to density plots and facilitate more confident estimation by end-users."
  - Definition: "we use the quantile function (inverse CDF) of the distribution to generate 'draws' from evenly-spaced quantiles. We plot the quantile 'draws' using a Wilkinsonian dotplot, yielding what we call a quantile dotplot: a consistent discrete representation of a probability distribution."
  - Rationale: "By using quantiles we facilitate interval estimation from frequencies: e.g., knowing there are 50 dots here, if we are willing to miss our bus 3/50 times, we can count 3 dots from the left to get a one-sided 94% (1 − 3/50) prediction interval."
  - Setup: "After removing 9 participants for incomplete data, we had 320 participants in the bus-timeline and 221 participants in the route-timeline layouts." Conditions: "density, stripeplot-50, dotplot-20, and dotplot-100".
  - Results: "Dotplot-20 has the lowest estimated variance (SD of ~11 percentage points), being about 1.15 times more precise than density plots." / "By contrast, dotplot-100 has similar variance to density, consistent with people estimating area instead of counting dots, perhaps because there are more dots than they may be willing to count." / "Participants expressed higher confidence in their estimates on average in the dotplot-20 condition (mean = 81/100, 95% CI: [77, 83])."
- Establishes: a countable number of dots (about 20) is what helps; 100 dots collapses back to area-estimation. The gain is modest (1.15x) but real, and confidence rises.
- Strength: good (n≈540, controlled); single domain.
- Role: LOAD-BEARING for "frequency formats on small screens."

### 1.4 Correll & Gleicher 2014 — "Error Bars Considered Harmful" (IEEE TVCG)
- URL: https://graphics.cs.wisc.edu/Papers/2014/CG14/Preprint.pdf — at source (author preprint).
- Quotations:
  - Abstract (full): "When making an inference or comparison with uncertain, noisy, or incomplete data, measurement error and confidence intervals can be as important for judgment as the actual mean values of different groups. These often misunderstood statistical quantities are frequently represented by bar charts with error bars. This paper investigates drawbacks with this standard encoding, and considers a set of alternatives designed to more effectively communicate the implications of mean and error data to a general audience, drawing from lessons learned from the use of visual statistics in the information visualization community. We present a series of crowd-sourced experiments that confirm that the encoding of mean and error significantly changes how viewers make decisions about uncertain data. Careful consideration of design tradeoffs in the visual presentation of data results in human reasoning that is more consistently aligned with statistical inferences. We suggest the use of gradient plots (which use transparency to encode uncertainty) and violin plots (which use width) as better alternatives for inferential tasks than bar charts with error bars."
  - Within-the-bar bias: "A bar in a bar chart creates a region of false certainty" — "values visually within the bar are perceived as likelier data points than values outside of the bar."
  - One-sample experiment (96 participants, 8 per cell): "participants in the bar chart condition considered red dots below the mean (and so within the visual area of the bar) significantly more likely than those above the bar."
  - Confidence: participants were "significantly more confident with the alternate encoding types which provided more detail about the probability distribution (gradient: M = 5.12, violin: M = 5.06) than with the bar charts".
  - Two-sample: bar charts caused participants to "predict outcomes that were significantly larger than with other encodings"; elevated confidence with bars was described as "unjustified" even for non-significant differences.
- Establishes: the bar + whisker idiom systematically distorts; gradient and violin encodings align lay judgments better with the statistics.
- Strength: good (several crowd experiments); the original coinage of within-the-bar bias is Newman & Scholl 2012 (not fetched; cited in 1.1).
- Role: LOAD-BEARING.

### 1.5 Hofman, Goldstein & Hullman 2020 — inferential vs outcome uncertainty (CHI; Best Paper)
- URL: http://jakehofman.com/pdfs/visualizing-inferential-uncertainty.pdf (author copy, full text); abstract also at https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/how-visualizing-inferential-uncertainty-can-mislead-readers-about-treatment-effects-in-scientific-results/ — at source.
- Quotations:
  - Abstract: "Scientists presenting experimental results often choose to display either inferential uncertainty (e.g., uncertainty in the estimate of a population mean) or outcome uncertainty (e.g., variation of outcomes around that mean). How does this choice impact readers' beliefs about the size of treatment effects? We investigate this question in two experiments comparing 95% confidence intervals (means and standard errors) to 95% prediction intervals (means and standard deviations). The first experiment finds that participants are willing to pay more for and overestimate the effect of a treatment when shown confidence intervals relative to prediction intervals. The second experiment evaluates how alternative visualizations compare to standard visualizations for different effect sizes. We find that axis rescaling reduces error, but not as well as prediction intervals or animated hypothetical outcome plots (HOPs), and that depicting inferential uncertainty causes participants to underestimate variability in individual outcomes."
  - Exp 1 (1,743 after attention check): "Participants were willing to pay substantially more on average (80 Ice Dollars compared to 50)" with CIs vs PIs; with extra caption text "72 versus 54 Ice Dollars." Probability of superiority: "Participants who saw 95% CIs thought that they would win 86% of the time...whereas those who were shown 95% PIs reported an average probability of superiority of 67%." True value 57%. Cohen's d = 1.29 (matching-text condition).
  - Exp 2 (1,830): willingness to pay for the small effect — 95% CI 88, rescaled CI 70, 95% PI 52, HOPs 49 Ice Dollars. "distributions for the 95% PIs and HOPs conditions have an average implied SD of approximately 17.5 Ice Dollars, which is much closer to the true value of 15.3 Ice Dollars than the average implied SD of approximately 11 Ice Dollars for the 95% CI conditions."
  - Warning: "Because scientists often use CIs and publish small effects, many scientific results are likely exaggerated in the minds of some readers."
- Establishes: a standard error bar (CI/SE) makes readers think the *effect on individuals* is much bigger than it is; showing outcome spread (PI, HOPs) fixes most of this.
- Strength: strong (two large experiments, large effect sizes).
- Role: LOAD-BEARING — directly relevant to any dashboard that puts a CI around an average.

### 1.6 Padilla, Ruginski & Creem-Regehr 2017 — hurricane cone vs ensemble (Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications)
- URL: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41235-017-0076-1 — at source (open access).
- Quotations:
  - "Ensemble and summary displays are two widely used methods to represent visual-spatial uncertainty; however, there is disagreement about which is the most effective technique to communicate uncertainty to the general public."
  - Prior misreading (citing Ruginski et al. 2016): "participants viewing any of the summary displays compared to the ensemble display were significantly more likely to self-report that the display depicted the hurricane growing in size over time." The cone "only depicts a distribution of potential hurricane paths and no information about the size," yet viewers associate "the salient increasing diameter of the cone boundaries" with physical growth.
  - Exp 1: cone viewers "believed that locations at the center of the hurricane that were at a later point in time would receive more damage than at an earlier time point"; "a greater effect of distance from the center of the storm on judgments with the ensemble display compared to the cone."
  - Exps 2–3: "when participants use ensemble displays to make point-based judgments, they may overweight individual ensemble members in their decision-making process" (an oil rig lying on a drawn track was judged more at risk).
  - "decisions about visualization methods should be informed by the viewer's task."
- Establishes: a hard boundary (the cone) is read as containment and as size; spaghetti ensembles remove that but create a new error (a drawn line is read as a prediction for the place it crosses).
- Strength: good; lab/online with non-experts; three experiments.
- Role: LOAD-BEARING for the "boundaries are read as categories" point.

### 1.7 Belia, Fidler, Williams & Cumming 2005 — researchers misread error bars (Psychological Methods)
- URL: http://www.edmeasurement.net/5245/Belia-2005-CIs-SEs.pdf (course copy of the published PDF) — at source text.
- Quotations:
  - "Little is known about researchers' understanding of confidence intervals (CIs) and standard error (SE) bars."
  - 473 respondents (authors in psychology, behavioural neuroscience, medicine).
  - CI task: "Only 22% of respondents set the means so the p value was between .025 and .10"; "Their mean response corresponds to p ≈ .009."
  - SE task: "SE task respondents were generally too lax" — "Their mean response corresponds to p ≈ .109."
  - "Overall, 31.5% (99/314) used the incorrect rule that error bars just touch when means are just statistically significantly different (p ≈ .05)."
  - Repeated measures: "Only 11% of RM respondents gave any such recognition" of the design problem; "For Psy, 3/51 (6%) recognized the problem; for BN, 8/47 (17%)."
  - Conclusion: "We conclude that very many researchers whose articles have appeared in leading journals in psychology, behavioral neuroscience, and medicine have fundamental and severe misconceptions about how CIs and SE bars can justifiably be used."
- Establishes: the people who *make* error bars cannot read them reliably — so a lay audience will not.
- Strength: strong for its claim (large expert sample); older.
- Role: LOAD-BEARING (rhetorically decisive).

### 1.8 Verbal probability terms

- Role: LOAD-BEARING for any dashboard that labels confidence with words.

### 1.9 Spiegelhalter 2017 — "Risk and Uncertainty Communication" (Annual Review of Statistics and Its Application 4)

  - "This review briefly examines the vast range of techniques used to communicate risk assessments arising from statistical analysis."
  - Numbers: "Use absolute risks (but also provide relative risks when dealing with potential catastrophic events)." / "When appropriate, express chance as a proportion, a frequency, or a percentage—it is crucial to be clear about the reference class." / "To avoid framing bias, provide percentages or frequencies both with and without the outcome." / "Keep the denominator fixed when making comparisons with frequencies, and use an incremental risk format." / "Be explicit about the time interval."
  - General: "Be clear about objectives." / "Segment audience into target groups and identify their needs, beliefs, and skills." / "Develop, test, and evaluate material with target groups." / "Build trust by being trustworthy." / "Use plain language and limit information to only what is necessary." / "Have the humility to admit uncertainty."

### 1.10 Markant, Rogha, Karduni, Wesslen & Dou 2023 — "When do data visualizations persuade?" (CHI; arXiv 2302.03776)
- URL: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.03776 (and abstract page https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03776) — at source, full text.
- Quotations:
  - Abstract: "strong prior attitudes are associated with smaller belief changes when presented with data that contradicts existing views, and that visual uncertainty representations may amplify this effect." Also: participants' statistical beliefs shifted but "their attitudes remained unchanged, highlighting the need for further research on whether data visualizations can drive longer-term changes in views and behavior."
  - Conditions: "Line" (scatterplot + correlation line, no uncertainty); "HOP" — "animated draws from the 95% confidence interval for the population correlation"; "Ensemble" — "static representation of the same random draws from the 95% confidence interval."
  - N = 331 after exclusions (Line 108, Ensemble 113, HOPs 110). Topics: COVID-19 vaccination and labour-union membership datasets. Beliefs elicited with a "Line+Cone" interface before and after; change = |r_post − r_prior| and |CI_post − CI_prior|.
  - Result: the best model showed "credible interactions between prior distance, attitude strength, and visualization condition"; "the effect of global attitude was more pronounced in visualization conditions with uncertainty representations (HOP and Ensemble) compared to visualizations with only the scatterplot and correlation line (Line condition)"; "uncertainty representations had no effect when the data conflicted with prior beliefs but the viewer did not have a strong preexisting attitude".
  - Their interpretation: "people with strong attitudes are more likely to attend to the uncertainty representation (e.g., examining the distribution of alternative correlation lines in the Ensemble condition) because they are motivated to find support for their existing belief about the correlation, leading them to assign less weight to the most likely correlation when updating their beliefs." Weak-attitude viewers "may have focused more on the correlation line representing the most likely relationship while being less motivated to inspect the uncertainty representation."
  - Their own caveat: "it should be noted that alternative models achieved comparable performance" and the condition difference "warrants further investigation to evaluate its robustness."
- Establishes: showing uncertainty is not neutral — for a reader who already disagrees, the spread of plausible lines is raw material for discounting the central estimate. The authors themselves rate this finding as tentative.
- Strength: moderate (one study, n=331, model-dependent interaction, authors flag fragility).
- Role: SUPPORTING — important counterweight; must be quoted with its caveat.

### 2.1 Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995 — frequency formats (Psychological Review)
- URL: https://pages.ucsd.edu/~scoulson/203/GG_How_1995.pdf (course copy of published PDF) — full text.
- Quotations: "Bayesian algorithms are computationally simpler in frequency formats than in the probability formats used in previous research." - Strength: foundational, heavily replicated (and contested on mechanism, not on the effect).
- Role: SUPPORTING (the theory behind "X in 1000").

### 2.2 Gigerenzer, Gaissmaier, Kurz-Milcke, Schwartz & Woloshin 2007 — "Helping Doctors and Patients Make Sense of Health Statistics" (Psychological Science in the Public Interest 8(2))
- URL: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2008.00033.x — at source (open access full text).
- Quotations: "Many doctors, patients, journalists, and politicians alike do not understand what health statistics mean or draw wrong conclusions without noticing." Mammography: with conditional probabilities only 21% of 160 gynaecologists gave the right answer ("about 1 out of 10 women with positive mammograms actually has breast cancer"); with natural frequencies 87%. Relative-risk framing: "Early detection with mammography reduces the risk of dying from breast cancer by 25%" corresponds to about "1 in 1,000 fewer deaths," yet most respondents estimated hundreds per thousand. The paper defines "minimal statistical literacy" (no certainty, only acceptable risk; know outcome, time frame and absolute size; screening has harms as well as benefits; tests give false positives and negatives).
- Strength: strong, canonical.
- Role: LOAD-BEARING for relative vs absolute and natural frequencies.

### 2.3 The "1 in X" effect — Pighin, Savadori, Barilli, Cremonesi, Ferrari & Bonnefon 2011 (Medical Decision Making 31(5):721–729)
- URL: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0272989X11403490 — at source, abstract (full abstract verbatim).
- Abstract: "Among numerical formats available to express probability, ratios are extensively used in risk communication, perhaps because of the health professional's intuitive sense of their clarity and simplicity. Moreover, health professionals, in the attempt to make the data more meaningful, tend to prefer proportions with a numerator of 1 and shifting denominators (e.g., 1 in 200) rather than equivalent rates of disease per unit of population exposed to the threat (e.g., 5 in 1000). However, in a series of 7 experiments, it is shown that individual subjective assessments of the same probability presented through proportions rather than rates vary significantly. A 1-in-X format (e.g., 1 in 200) is subjectively perceived as bigger and more alarming than an N-in-XN format (e.g., 5 in 1000). The 1-in-X effect generalizes to different populations, probabilities, and medical conditions. Furthermore, the effect is not attenuated by a communicative intervention (verbal analogy), but it disappears with an icon array visual aid."
- Strength: good (7 experiments); abstract-level detail only.
- Role: LOAD-BEARING for "keep the denominator fixed" — note the icon array cured it.

### 2.4 Relative vs absolute risk — Akl et al. 2011 Cochrane review "Using alternative statistical formats for presenting risks and risk reductions"

### 2.5 Icon arrays
(a) Garcia-Retamero & Cokely 2013, Current Directions in Psychological Science 22(5) — URL https://digitalcommons.mtu.edu/michigantech-p/12874/ — ABSTRACT-ONLY (institutional repository). Abstract verbatim: "Informed decision making requires that people understand health risks. Unfortunately, many people are not risk literate and are biased by common risk communication practices. These studies show that appropriately designed visual aids are often highly effective, transparent, and ethically desirable tools for improving decision making, changing attitudes, and reducing risky behavior. Theoretical mechanisms, open questions, and emerging applications are discussed."
(b) Garcia-Retamero & Cokely 2017, "Designing Visual Aids That Promote Risk Literacy: A Systematic Review of Health Research and Evidence-Based Design Heuristics," Human Factors 59(4) — URL https://www.riskliteracy.org/files/Systematic%20Review.pdf (author site) — full text.   - 36 publications, 27,885 participants, 60 countries; "Eighty-eight percent of the studies showed that static visual aids tend to be beneficial."
  - On the Galesic et al. 2009 result: "Among this group of innumerate yet moderately graph-literate people, accuracy increased from less than 20% to nearly 80% when visual aids were used." / "Depicting the risks using icon arrays can effectively reduce or eliminate denominator neglect, especially in people with low numeracy and/or high graph literacy."

(c) Galesic, Garcia-Retamero & Gigerenzer 2009, Health Psychology 28(2):210–216 — NOT reached at source (APA PsycNet robots-blocked; PubMed and Europe PMC captcha/blocked; MPG PuRe 403). Reported here only SECONDARY via (b). Do not cite its figures except as "as summarised by Garcia-Retamero & Cokely 2017."
(d) Okan, Garcia-Retamero, Cokely & Maldonado 2012, J. ### 2.6 Fagerlin, Zikmund-Fisher & Ubel 2011 — "Helping patients decide: ten steps to better risk communication" (JNCI 103(19):1436–1443)
- URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3218625 — at source (PMC full text).
- The ten steps (headings verbatim) with one key sentence each:
  1. "Use plain language to make written and verbal materials more understandable." — "Plain language generally refers to materials that are written in a simplified manner so that people of low literacy (eighth grade or lower level of education) can read them."
  2. "Present data using absolute risks." — "Research has consistently shown...that changes in risk appear larger when presented using relative risk than when using an absolute risk."
  3. "Using pictographs to communicate risk and benefit information." — "Pictographs are more quickly and better comprehended than other graphical formats and can help to prevent patients from being biased by other factors."
  4. "Consider the impact of presenting statistical information using frequencies rather than percentages." — "We recommend...that communications about cancer risks and treatment benefits be presented using frequency formats."
  5. "Highlight the incremental risks associated with treatment distinctly from baseline risks." — "One way to prevent this problem is to separate baseline and incremental risk information."
  6. "Be aware that the order of presenting risks and benefits can alter risk perceptions." — "When the risks of tamoxifen were presented after the benefits, participants perceived the risks as more worrisome and more common."
  7. "When there are numerous risks and benefits, conclude with summary tables." — "Include a summary table at the end of patient materials that concisely reminds patients of the relevant attributes and the risk and benefit statistics."
  8. "Recognize that comparative risk information is persuasive not just informative." — "Comparative risk information is a powerful influence on risk perceptions and can bias decisions."
  9. "Consider that less information may be more effective." — "Simpler presentations...resulted in improved understanding and greater sensitivity to the magnitude of risk reduction."
  10. "Repeatedly reinforce the time interval over which a risk occurs." — "We urge cancer risk communicators to always describe the relevant time intervals clearly."
- Strength: expert consensus commentary citing experiments; health context.
- Role: LOAD-BEARING (a ready-made checklist that transfers to any dashboard showing rates).

### 3.3 Börner, Maltese, Balliet & Heimlich 2016 — "Investigating aspects of data visualization literacy using 20 information visualizations and 273 science museum visitors" (Information Visualization 15(3); note: the journal is *Information Visualization*, not *Public Understanding of Science* as the brief said)
- URL: https://cns.iu.edu/docs/publications/2015-borner-investigating.pdf (author/institutional copy) — full text.
- Quotations:

All at source.
- Conformance (Understanding → conformance page): Level A — "the web page satisfies all the Level A success criteria, or a conforming alternate version is provided." Level AA — "the web page satisfies all the Level A and Level AA success criteria, or a Level AA conforming alternate version is provided." Level AAA — "the web page satisfies all the Level A, Level AA and Level AAA success criteria, or a Level AAA conforming alternate version is provided." - SC 1.1.1 Non-text Content (Level A): "All non-text content that is presented to the user has a text alternative that serves the equivalent purpose, except for the situations listed below." Exceptions — Controls, Input: "If non-text content is a control or accepts user input, then it has a name that describes its purpose." Time-Based Media: "If non-text content is time-based media, then text alternatives at least provide descriptive identification of the non-text content." Decoration, Formatting, Invisible: "If non-text content is pure decoration, is used only for visual formatting, or is not presented to users, then it is implemented in a way that it can be ignored by assistive technology."
  - Bearing on charts: a chart is non-text content; "equivalent purpose" is the operative phrase — a one-line alt is not equivalent for a chart carrying data.
- SC 1.3.1 Info and Relationships (Level A): "Information, structure, and relationships conveyed through presentation can be programmatically determined or are available in text." Understanding example: "A bus schedule consists of a table with the bus stops listed vertically in the first column and the different buses listed horizontally across the first row... The bus stop and bus cells are identified as headers for their corresponding row or column so that assistive technology can programmatically determine which bus and which bus stop are associated with the time in each cell."
  - Bearing: a data table behind a chart needs real header cells; a dashboard's grouping of tiles should be structural, not just visual.
- SC 1.4.1 Use of Color (Level A): "Color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information, indicating an action, prompting a response, or distinguishing a visual element." Note: "This success criterion addresses color perception specifically. Other forms of perception are covered in Guideline 1.3 including programmatic access to color and other visual presentation coding."
  - Bearing: a legend that distinguishes series only by colour fails this. (The Understanding page's Intent does not mention charts by name.)
- SC 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum) (Level AA): "The visual presentation of text and images of text has a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1, except for the following:" Large Text: "Large-scale text and images of large-scale text have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1"; Incidental: "Text or images of text that are part of an inactive user interface component, that are pure decoration, that are not visible to anyone, or that are part of a picture that contains significant other visual content, have no contrast requirement."; Logotypes: "Text that is part of a logo or brand name has no contrast requirement."
- SC 1.4.4 Resize Text (Level AA): "Except for captions and images of text, text can be resized without assistive technology up to 200 percent without loss of content or functionality."
- SC 1.4.10 Reflow (Level AA): "Content can be presented without loss of information or functionality, and without requiring scrolling in two dimensions for:" — "Vertical scrolling content at a width equivalent to 320 CSS pixels"; "Horizontal scrolling content at a height equivalent to 256 CSS pixels" — "Except for parts of the content which require two-dimensional layout for usage or meaning." Note 2 examples of excepted content: "Images required for understanding (such as maps and diagrams), video, games, presentations, data tables (not individual cells), and interfaces where it is necessary to keep toolbars in view while manipulating content."
  - Bearing: maps, diagrams and data tables are the named exceptions; a dashboard *as a whole* is not.
- SC 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast (Level AA): "The visual presentation of the following have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against adjacent color(s):" User Interface Components: "Visual information required to identify user interface components and states, except for inactive components or where the appearance of the component is determined by the user agent and not modified by the author"; Graphical Objects: "Parts of graphics required to understand the content, except when a particular presentation of graphics is essential to the information being conveyed." Understanding page on charts: "The graphical objects are the slices of the pie (chart)." / "The graphical objects are the lines in the graph, including the background lines for the values, and the colored lines with shapes." / "The lines should have 3:1 contrast against their background, but as there is little overlap with other lines they do not need to contrast with each other or the graduated lines." / "A graph uses a light background and ensures that the colors for each line have a 3:1 contrast ratio against the background."
  - Bearing: this is the one SC whose official explanatory text speaks about charts directly.
- SC 2.1.1 Keyboard (Level A): "All functionality of the content is operable through a keyboard interface without requiring specific timings for individual keystrokes, except where the underlying function requires input that depends on the path of the user's movement and not just the endpoints." Note 1: "This exception relates to the underlying function, not the input technique. For example, if using handwriting to enter text, the input technique (handwriting) requires path-dependent input but the underlying function (text input) does not." Note 2: "This does not forbid and should not discourage providing mouse input or other input methods in addition to keyboard operation."
  - Bearing: hover-only tooltips on a chart deliver information no keyboard user can reach.
- SC 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum) (Level AA): "The size of the target for pointer inputs is at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels" except — Spacing: "Undersized targets are positioned so that if a 24 CSS pixel diameter circle is centered on the bounding box of each, the circles do not intersect"; Equivalent: "The function can be achieved through a different control on the same page that meets this criterion"; Inline: "The target is in a sentence or its size is otherwise constrained by the line-height of non-target text"; User Agent Control: "The size of the target is determined by the user agent and is not modified by the author"; Essential: "A particular presentation of the target is essential or is legally required for the information being conveyed". If there are many pins close together, the spacing between pins will often be below 24 CSS pixels. It is essential to show the pins at the correct map location, therefore the Essential exception applies."
- SC 3.1.5 Reading Level — Level AAA: "When text requires reading ability more advanced than the lower secondary education level after removal of proper names and titles, supplemental content, or a version that does not require reading ability more advanced than the lower secondary education level, is available." Definition: "The two or three year period of education that begins after completion of six years of school and ends nine years after the beginning of primary education." (UNESCO ISCED.)

- Classification: HARD LINE at A/AA; 3.1.5 is AAA and functionally guidance.

### 4.2 WAI-ARIA 1.2 — W3C Recommendation 06 June 2023 — SPEC
- URL: https://www.w3.org/TR/wai-aria-1.2/ — at source.
- Abstract: "Accessibility of web content requires semantic information about widgets, structures, and behaviors, in order to allow assistive technologies to convey appropriate information to persons with disabilities. This specification provides an ontology of roles, states, and properties that define accessible user interface elements and can be used to improve the accessibility and interoperability of web content and applications."
- role img: "A container for a collection of elements that form an image." (Further descriptive sentences about captions/multiple files were not surfaced by the reader; the first sentence is the definition.) role figure (Document Structure role): "A perceivable section of content that typically contains a graphical document, images, code snippets, or example text. The parts of a figure MAY be user-navigable."
- Is there a chart role? No. No role named "chart" or "graph" exists in ARIA 1.2.
- Related: WAI-ARIA Graphics Module 1.0, W3C Recommendation 02 October 2018, https://www.w3.org/TR/graphics-aria-1.0/ — graphics-document: "A type of document in which the visual appearance or layout of content conveys meaning."; graphics-object: "A section of a graphics-document that represents a distinct object or sub-component with semantic meaning. A graphical object may itself have nested sub-components."; graphics-symbol: "A graphical object used to convey a simple meaning or category, where the meaning is more important than the particular visual appearance." And: "In general, authors should use the graphics-document role for structured graphics such as charts, maps, diagrams, technical drawing, blue prints and instructional graphics."
- The abandoned chart-roles proposal: W3C wiki "SVG Accessibility/ARIA roles for charts," https://www.w3.org/wiki/SVG_Accessibility/ARIA_roles_for_charts — "a proposed system of roles and properties for using ARIA to annotate charts, graphs, and maps"; last edited 11 September 2015; proposals such as graphics-datachart/graphics-dataunit/graphics-axis were never standardised.
- Classification: ARIA is normative for *how* to express semantics, but not a conformance target for a dashboard; it offers img/figure/graphics-document and nothing chart-specific. Bearing on charts: an SVG chart should be role="img" (or graphics-document) with an accessible name, or it is invisible to a screen reader.

### 4.4 COGA — "Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities" — W3C Working Group Note 29 April 2021 — GUIDANCE
- URL: https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/ — at source.
- Own status statements: "This document was published by the Cognitive and Learning Disabilities Accessibility Task Force (Coga TF)...as a Working Group Note." / "Publication as a Working Group Note does not imply endorsement by the W3C Membership." / "This is a draft document and may be updated, replaced or obsoleted by other documents at any time." / "The objectives and patterns presented here provide supplemental guidance beyond the requirements of WCAG." / "Following this guidance is not required for conformance to WCAG."
- Eight objectives: Help Users Understand What Things are and How to Use Them; Help Users Find What They Need; Use Clear and Understandable Content; Help Users Avoid Mistakes and Know How to Correct Them; Help Users Focus; Ensure Processes Do Not Rely on Memory; Provide Help and Support; Support Adaptation and Personalization.
- Objective 3 patterns (verbatim titles): "Use Clear Words"; "Use a Simple Tense and Voice"; "Avoid Double Negatives or Nested Clauses"; "Use Literal Language"; "Keep Text Succinct"; "Use Clear, Unambiguous Formatting and Punctuation"; "Include Symbols and Letters Necessary to Decipher the Words"; "Provide Summary of Long Documents and Media"; "Separate Each Instruction"; "Use White Spacing"; "Ensure Foreground Content is not Obscured by Background"; "Explain Implied Content"; "Provide Alternatives for Numerical Concepts".
- Objective 6 patterns: "Provide a Login that Does Not Rely on Memory or Other Cognitive Skills"; "Allow the User a Simple, Single Step, Login"; "Provide a Login Alternative with Less Words"; "Let Users Avoid Navigating Voice Menus"; "Do Not Rely on Users Calculations or Memorizing Information".
- Structure pattern 4.3.3 "Use a Clear and Understandable Page Structure": "Make sure it has a clear structure and hierarchy so that it is easy to understand," using "visual indicators to help people understand: structure and relative importance of the page content."
- Dashboard-relevant: "Provide Alternatives for Numerical Concepts" and "Do Not Rely on Users Calculations or Memorizing Information" are the two patterns that speak directly to a numbers-heavy screen (their body text was not surfaced by the reader; the author should open these two sections).
- Classification: GUIDANCE, by its own words.

### 4.7 UK legal position — who is bound
(a) Public Sector Bodies (Websites and Mobile Applications) (No. 2) Accessibility Regulations 2018, SI 2018/952. URLs: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2018/952/regulation/4 and /regulation/6 — at source. Reg 6: "Subject to regulation 7, public sector bodies must comply with the accessibility requirement." Reg 4(2) exclusion: "non-governmental organisations, unless they provide services that—(i) are essential to the public; or (ii) specifically address the needs of, or are meant for, persons with disabilities". (The interpretation clause defining the accessibility requirement as perceivable/operable/understandable/robust and the presumption-of-conformity clause were not fetched verbatim.)
(b) GOV.UK guidance "Understanding accessibility requirements for public sector bodies," https://www.gov.uk/guidance/accessibility-requirements-for-public-sector-websites-and-apps — at source: public sector bodies must "meet the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 AA accessibility standard" and publish an accessibility statement. "The following organisations are exempt from the accessibility regulations: non-government organisations like charities - unless they are mostly financed by public funding, provide services that are essential to the public or aimed at disabled people". "The accessibility regulations build on your existing obligations to people who have a disability under the Equality Act 2010" and "you're still legally required to make reasonable adjustments for disabled people when they're needed". "The Government Digital Service (GDS) monitors public sector bodies' compliance on behalf of the Minister for the Cabinet Office." Enforcement: EHRC (GB) and ECNI (NI). GOV.UK Service Manual (https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/helping-people-to-use-your-service/understanding-wcag): "Services must achieve WCAG 2.2 level AA as part of meeting government accessibility requirements."
(c) Equality Act 2010 s.20, https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/section/20 — at source. First requirement: "where a provision, criterion or practice of A's puts a disabled person at a substantial disadvantage in relation to a relevant matter in comparison with persons who are not disabled, to take such steps as it is reasonable to have to take to avoid the disadvantage." Third requirement (auxiliary aids): "where a disabled person would, but for the provision of an auxiliary aid, be put at a substantial disadvantage in relation to a relevant matter in comparison with persons who are not disabled, to take such steps as it is reasonable to have to take to provide the auxiliary aid." s.20(6): where the first or third requirement relates to information, the steps include ensuring "the information is provided in an accessible format".
(d) Explanatory Notes to Schedule 2 (services), https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/schedule/2/notes — at source: "As the duty is owed to disabled persons generally, it is an anticipatory duty which means service providers and people exercising public functions must anticipate the needs of disabled people and make appropriate reasonable adjustments."
(e) EHRC Code of Practice Ch. 7 and EHRC summary guidance were 403 from this environment; the legislation.gov.uk texts above are the primary sources anyway.
- Conclusion for a UK non-public-body organisation: WCAG is not a statute for it; the 2018 Regulations do not reach it unless it is an NGO providing services "essential to the public" or for disabled people (or is mostly publicly funded); but the Equality Act s.20/s.29 anticipatory duty to make reasonable adjustments — including providing information "in an accessible format" — applies to it as a service provider, and WCAG 2.2 AA is the standard a court or the EHRC would take as the measure of "reasonable." So: WCAG 2.2 AA = hard line for public bodies by regulation; de facto yardstick for everyone else via the Equality Act; everything else in Part 4 = guidance.

### 4.8 UK government chart rules adopted as voluntary standard
(a) Government Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: charts," https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-charts/ — at source. "This guidance is for people in government who need to design charts." Published 19 May 2022 (Type: Guidance). It ties itself to law: "all content published on public sector websites must meet the level A and AA success criteria in the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2." Rules going beyond WCAG: alt text may be "a table of the data presented in the chart" or "a text description of the message the chart is presenting"; "Avoiding using legends unless necessary. This is because legends use colour to match labels to data."; label lines directly or "put it at the end of the line on the right hand side of the chart"; "Aim for a maximum for four lines"; "Aim for a maximum of four categories within each stack"; clustered bars "we advise a limit of four bars per cluster"; pie charts only if "there are five categories or fewer" and "there is a dominant category"; "Dual axis charts" discouraged because "they can be easily misinterpreted"; "a sans serif font as some people find serif fonts hard to read"; avoid "shaded backgrounds," "unnecessary borders," "patterns, textures and shadows," "3D shapes"; "All axes, series, sectors and bars used in charts should be clearly labelled."; "Text should always be horizontal"; "Right align values on y-axis"; "It is best practice to provide the data displayed in each chart as an accessible data download."
(b) Analysis Function, "Accessible charts: a checklist of the basics," https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/charts-a-checklist — published 2 May 2023, "For: Anyone creating charts." Items marked (E) are "essential." Selected verbatim: "Avoid shaded backgrounds, unnecessary borders, patterns, textures, shadows, 3D shapes"; "Use a sans serif font"; "Make sure all text is horizontal"; titles/sources/footnotes "should be in the body text of the document or webpage" (E); "All charts should have a formal statistical title that states what the data is" (E); "All non-text content must have a text alternative" (E); bars: "it should always start at zero" (E); "When legends are used, users have to match labels using colour alone" (E — avoid legends); lines: "Try to place labels on the chart area or at the end of the lines" (E); "Line charts with more than four lines can get too cluttered"; pies: "Legends and keys rely on matching labels using colour alone" (E); small multiples: "All the numerical axes need to have the same scale to avoid misinterpretation."
(c) Analysis Function, "Data visualisation: colours," https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-colours-in-charts/ — graphical elements 3:1 against adjacent colours; text 4.5:1; categorical palette in order: Dark blue #12436D, Turquoise #28A197, Dark pink #801650, Orange #F46A25, Dark grey #3D3D3D, Light purple #A285D1; limit to four colours for basic visualisations; palette tested for colour-vision deficiency and greyscale; label directly rather than rely on a legend ("dual coding" is the author's term for this — the page's wording is direct labelling plus matching legend order to the visual order).
(d) ONS Service Manual — https://service-manual.ons.gov.uk/data-visualisation/guidance/chart-text and .../colours/using-colours-in-charts — at source. "All charts must have a title that is: front-loaded, in the active voice, in sentence case, as concise as possible, 15 words or fewer"; "The chart title should describe the main trend you want the chart to show to the user."; alt text format "A [chart type] showing that [chart title]." and "A chart alt text sentence should be between 100 and 200 characters and always end with a full stop."; "Always use five colours or fewer in your data visualisations to help users perceive the differences between colours"; "Never use more than nine colours"; "Any text where the colour contrast ratio is below 4.5:1 should only be used for large text"; "use the same colours for the same categories across all your charts".
- Classification: VOLUNTARY STANDARD for a non-public body; binding on government analysts as internal policy. These are the only UK documents that convert WCAG's abstract criteria into chart rules (no legends, ≤4 lines, start bars at zero, data download, horizontal text, titles that state the message).

## PART 5 — UNCERTAINTY ON MAPS: HELP OR HINDER?
---------------------------------------------------------------
### 5.1 MacEachren, Roth, O'Brien, Li, Swingley & Gahegan 2012 — "Visual Semiotics & Uncertainty Visualization: An Empirical Study" (IEEE TVCG 18(12))
- URL: https://geography.wisc.edu/cartography/projects/publications/MacEachrenEtAl_2012_TVCG.pdf (co-author institutional copy) — full text.
- Quotations: "two linked empirical studies focused on uncertainty visualization" examining "visual variables and iconicity of representation" to develop "initial guidelines for representing uncertainty." Variables: "fuzziness, location, value, arrangement, size, transparency, hue, saturation, orientation, shape" (+ colour properties). "Seventy-two (n=72) participants completed timed suitability ranking tasks"; "Thirty participants completed the assessment of aggregate uncertainty tasks"; participants were GIScience students and professionals — "purposefully sampled," not lay. Results: "Three symbol sets (fuzziness, location, and value) received a mean intuitiveness ranking over '5.0'"; "Three symbol sets (arrangement, size, and transparency) received a mean intuitiveness ranking between '4.0' and '5.0'"; "The remaining symbol sets (saturation, hue, orientation, and shape) had mean, median, and modal intuitiveness rankings below '4.0' and were therefore deemed as unacceptable." Iconic symbols "received a slightly higher mean intuitiveness ranking overall ('5.13') than their abstract counterparts ('4.98')" but "require slightly longer to rate" and work only "if users understand both the aspect of uncertainty being signified and the metaphor."
- Establishes: blur (fuzziness), lightness (value) and positional jitter are read as "uncertain" almost without instruction; hue, shape and orientation are not — so a hatched or washed-out choropleth is a defensible choice, a differently-coloured one is not. Caveat: experts, point symbols.
- Role: LOAD-BEARING for the design rule; SUPPORTING for "lay readers" (sample was not lay).

### 5.2 Kinkeldey, MacEachren & Schiewe 2014 — "How to Assess Visual Communication of Uncertainty? A Systematic Review of Geospatial Uncertainty Visualisation User Studies" (Cartographic Journal 51(4))
- URL: https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/how-to-assess-visual-communication-of-uncertainty-a-systematic-re/ — ABSTRACT-ONLY (publisher page 403). "For decades, uncertainty visualisation has attracted attention in disciplines such as cartography and geographic visualisation, scientific visualisation and information visualisation." Most work develops new methods; empirical evaluation is thin. "We highlight the importance of user tasks for successful solutions" and "recommend moving towards task-centered typologies to support systematic evaluation in the field of uncertainty visualisation." No study count surfaced.
### 5.3 Kinkeldey, MacEachren, Riveiro & Schiewe 2017 — "Evaluating the effect of visually represented geodata uncertainty on decision-making: systematic review, lessons learned, and recommendations" (Cartography and Geographic Information Science 44(1))
- Not reached at source (T&F 403; DiVA robots). SECONDARY only, via Penn State GEOG 486 course page https://courses.ems.psu.edu/geog486/node/907: "Most of the studies they analyzed suggested that the visualization of uncertainty does have an effect on task performance with maps and similar spatial displays"; "Uncertainty visualization seems at least not to significantly increase task-completion times"; Hope & Hunter 2007 (cited there): "participants perceive uncertain data as risky, which can induce irrational decision-making via loss-aversion"; the page's verdict: "whether uncertainty visualization is useful...is context dependent and still thoroughly up for debate."
- Honest answer to the thread: the geospatial literature finds that showing uncertainty usually changes decisions and rarely slows people, that it can help accuracy, and that it can also trigger risk-aversion; it has not shown a clean benefit for lay choropleth readers, and the field's own reviews say the answer depends on task. Combined with Padilla 2017 (cone/ensemble), the safest design claim is: use fuzziness/value/texture, never a hard boundary and never hue; expect the display to shift behaviour, not just understanding.
- Strength: moderate/weak at source; flagged.
- Role: SUPPORTING.

## THREADS PULLED (not asked for)
---------------------------------------------------------------
1. **The journal correction**: Börner et al. 2016 is in *Information Visualization* (SAGE), not *Public Understanding of Science*. The paper also explicitly did *not* test correct reading, only familiarity and naming — so it cannot be cited for "X% could not read a bar chart."
2. **The 49% vs 21% numeracy gap**: the famous "half of UK adults" is Skills for Life 2011 (England, Entry Level 3 or below = 49.1%); PIAAC 2023 puts England at 21% at Level 1 or below (OECD 25%) and *improving* since 2012. The two use different thresholds; a careful book should give both and say which is which. The OSR page also states there is "no comprehensive public assessment" of chart-reading ability in the UK — a genuine evidence gap worth naming.
3. **Icon arrays cure the 1-in-X effect** (Pighin 2011) — the same device that fixes denominator neglect fixes "1 in 200 feels bigger than 5 in 1000."
4. **A number in brackets after the word is what works; a glossary elsewhere does not** (Wintle 2019) — a concrete, cheap dashboard rule.
5. **COGA's two numbers patterns** ("Provide Alternatives for Numerical Concepts"; "Do Not Rely on Users Calculations or Memorizing Information") are the closest thing W3C has to a cognitive-accessibility rule for dashboards; worth opening in full.
6. **WCAG's own chart text lives under 1.4.11**, not 1.1.1 or 1.4.1 — the pie-slice and line-graph examples are the official chart guidance.
7. **The dead ends at W3C** — the 2015 chart-roles wiki and the Infographics CG (closed 2023) — show that a chart-specific accessibility standard was attempted and abandoned; useful for the "why is there no chart standard?" question.
8. **Padilla's ensemble caveat**: spaghetti plots stop the cone misreading but create a new one (a line through your town = a forecast for your town). Any switch from interval to ensemble/HOP trades one error for another.

## WHAT SURPRISED ME
- How thin the *lay-reader* evidence on map uncertainty is: the two cartographic reviews conclude "it depends on task," and the best-known semiotics study used GIS experts. The strongest lay-reader map evidence is actually Padilla's hurricane work.
- Kay's quantile dotplot gain is only ~1.15x in precision — the paper is cited as if it were transformative; the real finding is that 20 countable dots beat 100 uncountable ones.
- WCAG's plain-language criterion (3.1.5) is AAA, which W3C itself says should not be mandated site-wide; "plain language" on a UK dashboard is therefore guidance everywhere except by voluntary house style. The hard lines that actually bite on charts are 1.1.1, 1.3.1, 1.4.1, 1.4.11 and 2.1.1.
- Markant et al.: the field's favourite remedy (HOPs/ensembles) may make a sceptical reader *less* persuadable — and the authors say so with unusual candour about model fragility.
- Hofman et al.'s effect size (d = 1.29 for willingness to pay; 86% vs 67% vs truth 57%) — a standard error bar roughly doubles the perceived advantage of a treatment.
- The OSR's admission that the UK has no public measurement of chart-reading ability at all.
