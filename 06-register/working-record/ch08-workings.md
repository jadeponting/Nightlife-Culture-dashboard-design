# Working record · Chapter 8 — Automation without admin

*How this chapter was made. These sections stood in the chapter at draft 2 and were moved here, word for word, on 14 October 2026 when the book was finished. They are the record of the method: what the chapter expected against what it found, what it did to the chapters before it, the gaps it met, where it met the design system and the Decision Book, and the loose ends it flagged. Nothing here is a rule; the rules are in the register, the open choices in the decision log, the probes in `03-tests/PROBES.md`.*

## Before you start

This is the eighth chapter of a research book written for Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio led by Carly Heath, about how dashboards work and why. The book exists to support the Vibe Map — a planned public, non-commercial, interactive account of the people, activity, value and support systems that sustain a place's nightlife culture, built first for the West of England. You do not need to have read Chapters 1 to 7 to read this one. Five things from them recur: the working definition — *a dashboard is a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader*; Chapter 4's rule that every figure carries its provenance and its revision status, and its warning that a platform's data describes the platform's users; Chapter 6's finding that business closures "are not final until the third year" and that a modelled figure's history is revised; Chapter 7's finding that a seasonally adjusted series changes when the next month arrives and cannot exist under three years; and version 1's founding case, the observatory — a maintained container over unmaintained contents, with update labels reading "almost 7 years ago". Where this chapter refers to Carly, it means the studio's founder; where it refers to the Vibe Map, it is illustrating, not specifying. The studio's rulebook for design decisions is the Decision Book; its visual rules are in a design system (Style Guide v1.5).

Two conventions, explained fully in Chapter 0. Every rule carries a **kind** — *Standard*, *Evidence*, *Reading*, *Practice* or *Nightlife Culture* — and a **tier**: *Fixed*, *Adaptable* or *Decorative*; "Fixed as a stance" marks a rule the studio adopts whose source is a reading or a practice, and "Fixed as a procedure" fixes the steps and not the parameter. Flags in square brackets mark loose ends: `[Further research]`, `[Prototype flag]`, `[Validate with users]`, `[Revisit → Ch n]`, `[Revisit at end]`, `[Rich reference]` and `[Counterbalance → Ch n]`. A dagger (†) marks a source read at the authors' abstract or summary only; "secondary" marks a quotation reached through another named document; "(read once)" marks a quotation returned on a single reading and not confirmed by a second. The *symmetry test* (Chapter 0) means that every standard this chapter applies to others' systems it applies, in section 11, to the studio's own; the *failure register* is the brief's list, in Carly's words, of the ways a dashboard fails — among them "if it breaks or isn't reliable" and "if people don't trust it". Three terms of the trade are used throughout and defined where they first appear: an *API* (an application programming interface — the published way one piece of software asks another for data), a *rate limit* (how often it may ask), and a *tile* (one square of a map, served separately so that only the squares in view are fetched; a dashboard's panels are called *panels* here, never tiles). Four more are defined where they first appear and gathered here: a *heartbeat* is a signal a scheduled job sends to say it ran, and the service that listens for it; the *bus factor*, also called the *truck factor*, is the number of people who could leave before a project stopped; an *SLO* is a service level objective, a stated target for a measured property of a service, and an *SLA* the agreement about what happens if it is missed; a *page*, in the reliability engineers' vocabulary quoted in section 7, is an alert sent to a person's pager, not a web page. Sizes in KB and MB are decimal; KiB and MiB, used by one source, are binary (1 KiB = 1,024 bytes) and are quoted as written. Where a source is called "the fetch tool's reader", it means the text-only reader this book's research used to open pages.

A note on this chapter's sources. The research agents again had no web search; but this chapter's sources are mostly specifications, official guidance and platforms' own documentation, which live at known addresses, and the record is fuller than Chapters 6 and 7 could manage. Every quotation given was confirmed present on its page by a second fetch, spot-checked by the author (twelve sources; one reshaped sentence caught and corrected), or is marked as weaker than that; an independent verifier then re-traced about four hundred strings on some 125 pages and its corrections are in this draft, with the sources it confirmed marked "verified" in the list at the end. Developer documentation carries dates rarely and changes often: every limit, price and quota in this chapter is given as read on 6 October 2026, and several of the sentences the research was briefed to find had already been rewritten or removed by the time it looked. That drift is recorded in section 8 as evidence.

## 11 · The studio's own positions, tested

The chapter has so far argued from its sources. This section turns the same sources on the studio's own standing positions — the ones in version 1 of this book, in the Decision Book, in the design system and in the house framework — and records, for each, whether the evidence strengthens it, qualifies it, counterbalances it, or leaves it a choice. Chapter 0 calls this the symmetry test, and it is done here before the truths are written so that the truths are not the positions restated.

**"No admin."** The studio's phrase. Section 7 and 8 support the architecture it points to and narrow what it can mean: nobody tends the refresh; someone reads the ticket, re-points the job when an interface moves and decides what happens when a source closes. The UK's own dashboard guidance says in terms that running one is "not an appropriate project for a single person" (section 3). A small studio that intends to run one should write down whose job the ticket is and who is second. *Qualified.*

**"Staleness degrades visibly."** Version 1's Chapter 8 held that a stale series should look stale on the face. The protocol (`stale-if-error`), the cloud provider's anti-pattern list ("Invalidating or emptying local state as a result of a failed refresh") and the workbook ("Stale data is almost always better than incorrect data") agree that the last good copy is kept and shown with its age. Where the chapter adds to the position: the age shown must be the data's, not the page's (section 4); and "visibly degrades" must stop short of "silently disappears". The honest amputation of a series that has closed — which version 1 also allowed — must meet the retirement procedure of section 3: notice, an archive of what was shown, and the series' last state kept reachable. *Strengthened, with two conditions.*

**"Freshness is first-screen furniture."** Chapter 1's audit put the date on the face. Section 4 says the single date is not enough: a reader needs the period the data describes, the date it was published, the date it was last revised and the declared frequency, because a date without a frequency does not say whether the figure is late. The first of these is official — "Always make it clear which dates or period your dashboard is relevant to" — and the other three are the studio's extension of it. No design system reached (GOV.UK, Carbon, PatternFly) says a dashboard should show its last refresh; the practice is found in one product's documentation (the stamp "in the bottom left corner of the report") and a 1994 heuristic ("The design should always keep users informed about what is going on"). The four-part statement is the studio's, supported by the catalogue standards and the official rule on the period, and thin on display precedent; the chapter holds it. *Strengthened and extended; display precedent thin.*

**"Living series", and the house framework's "maintained … or extractive".** The framework quoted in Chapter 1 — confidential; cited, not reproduced — holds that a map not "actively maintained and ethically governed… risks becoming outdated or extractive as soon as it is published". Sections 5 and 6 add the supply side: the open licence "does not guarantee the continued supply of the Information", and the streaming and social platforms' interfaces have each been narrowed within five years. A living series is a promise the studio makes on top of supplies that promise nothing. The position stands; the chapter's addition is that "maintained" has a service record behind it (section 12), with a named role, a retirement plan and a successor. *Strengthened; made concrete.*

**The observatory.** Version 1's Chapters 1 and 8 made a long-running civic data observatory the founding failure: a maintained container over unmaintained contents. Section 7's standards cut both ways. If the observatory is judged by them — does it show the data's date and frequency, does it keep the last good copy, does its alarm go to a role — then so is the studio's own plan, and the plan must meet the sixty-day disabling, the notification to one person and the dropped job on the free scheduler before it is entitled to the comparison. *Counterbalanced: the standard applies to us first.*

**The ambition to read ticketing and streaming interfaces.** The Decision Book and the prototype's own questions assume these can be called. Section 6 reports what the terms permit, and the chapter stops there: it is a `[Prototype flag]` and a Chapter 10 question whether to proceed, and the terms should be open on the table when it is decided. The chapter's one methodological contribution is the distinction between a dependency on *supply* (the public interfaces: permitted, unguaranteed) and a dependency on *terms* (the platforms: guaranteed nothing, and addressed clause by clause). *A choice, now with its evidence attached.*

**"A map is the face."** The design system makes the map the recognisable surface of the Vibe Map. Section 10 does not dispute it; it prices it. A vector map costs over two-fifths of the script budget of the phone that three in ten online adults in DE households rely on, before tiles; its supply has four endings; its boundaries have a vintage and a size; and the standards require a non-map twin. The face can be a map; the page cannot be only one. *Qualified.*

**"Built in the open, on free tools."** The studio's repository and hosting assume the free tiers. Section 8 lists their edges on the day read: 25 MiB per asset; 1 GB per site; a paused state at the credit cap; schedules dropped under load and disabled after sixty quiet days; and no availability commitment that could be established. The free tier is a design constraint with a date on it, not a neutral default. *Qualified.*

- `[Further research]` **Single-pass:** marked "(read once)" where used; after the verifier's pass these are few — chiefly the GDS failure list, the Ticketmaster paging rule and definition fragments, the cron(8) sentences' dating, and the single-pass negatives in the design systems. **Secondary:** TechCrunch for Twitter 2023; MDN for `Retry-After`, `ETag` and `Cache-Control`; SPDX for the OGL text (the National Archives page still loops); Wikipedia for the maintenance-share and bus-factor summaries; Bundlephobia for library weights (version unmatched to releases). **Abstract only (†):** Avelino et al. 2016.
- `[Further research]` **Measured evidence that stale dashboards cost trust.** None found. The nearest are Fogg's 2002 credibility guideline ("People assign more credibility to sites that show they have been recently updated or reviewed") and Nielsen's 1996 "web gardener"; the Open Data Barometer's "very limited value" is a judgement. The chapter's rule that staleness must be visible rests on the protocol, the reliability doctrine and the studio's position, not on a user study. `[Validate with users]`: does a reader of the three user groups read "data to [date]" at all, and what do they do when it is old?
- `[Further research]` **A measured study of UK open-data staleness or withdrawal.** None reachable. The instruments that once measured it — the Global Open Data Index, the Open Data Barometer — have themselves stopped. Portal Watch may hold it.
- `[Further research]` **What a map costs a phone in bytes.** No published per-view measurement; suppliers bill by load or request. `[Prototype flag]`: open the prototype on the baseline device and network and count — tiles, boundaries, renderer, figures — and put the number in line 6 of the service record.
- `[Prototype flag]` **Whether to call the platform interfaces at all**, on their terms, for this purpose. Section 6 reports the terms; Chapter 10 and the prototype decide. The decision is made with the pages open and recorded in line 2.
- `[Prototype flag]` **The degraded mode, seen.** Break the job on purpose on launch day and look at the page on the baseline phone. Record the date in line 5.
- `[Prototype flag]` **The sixty-day disabling.** If the prototype's refresh runs on the free scheduler in a public repository, the watch in line 4 must be shown to catch the schedule switching off. Test it by waiting, or by disabling it.
- `[Validate with users]` **"Last updated" against the four-part statement.** Does a reader prefer the single date, and does the four-part statement (period, published, revised, frequency) read as fussy or as trustworthy? The chapter holds the latter and has no user evidence for it.
- `[Validate with users]` **Vector or raster, as experienced.** Whether the slowest quarter of the three user groups' phones draw the vector map at all, and whether the raster version "recognises their place" in the failure register's sense, is an afternoon's test and not a literature question.
- `[Revisit at end]` **Five records.** The provenance set (Ch 4), recipe card (Ch 5), claim record (Ch 6), comparison record (Ch 7) and service record (Ch 8) may be one document with five parts; the service record's line 2 overlaps the provenance set's source lines. Chapter 0 should say.
- `[Revisit → Ch 10]` **Platform terms and consent.** Spotify's III.13 and Ticketmaster's user-action clause are read here as technology; they also bear on whose behaviour a derived measure describes and who agreed. Chapter 10 should pick them up.
- `[Revisit → Ch 9]` **A "vibe" measured from a live source.** If any measure of a vibe is live by nature, it accepts section 7's chain arithmetic and section 6's terms; Chapter 9 should know what that costs before proposing it.
- `[Rich reference]` Government Analysis Function, *Data visualisation: building and managing dashboards* (6 February 2026) — the one official UK text that covers a dashboard's whole life, from "a digital service" to the six-month retirement banner and the archived repository. Half an hour, and it is the frame for the service record.
- `[Rich reference]` Treynor, Dahlin, Rau & Beyer, "The Calculus of Service Availability" (*ACM Queue*, 2017) — short, open, and the source of the chain rule and "design for graceful degradation".
- `[Rich reference]` Willison, "Git scraping" (2020) and "The Baked Data architectural pattern" (2021) — the no-admin architecture in its originator's words, with its boundary ("at most a few times a day") stated by him.
- `[Rich reference]` RFC 9111 §4.2–4.3 with RFC 5861 — two short normative texts that give the chapter its whole vocabulary of fresh, stale, lifetime and "serve the old copy if the new one fails".
- `[Rich reference]` The Spotify sequence — the blog posts of 27 November 2024 and 15 April 2025, the February 2026 migration guide, Developer Policy III.13 — as a dated, primary case of an interface narrowing in three steps.
- `[Rich reference]` Alex Russell, "The Performance Inequality Gap, 2024" — the one source that turns devices and networks into a byte budget, with the working shown.
- `[Rich reference]` The OpenStreetMap Foundation's tile and Nominatim usage policies — two pages that say "free data, not free servers" in operator's language and speak directly to scheduled jobs.

## Design system (Style Guide v1.5) and Decision Book — conflict log

**Agrees.** The provenance code on every chart — "reference period, geography and boundary vintage" — is the geography half of this chapter's line 7 and the period half of line 1. DTA-05 and DTA-06 are the boundary rules as stated. BRF-15 ("Put version and date on the face of anything that will be superseded") and BRK-18 (name the version) are truth 1 in the house's own words, and the running foot already carries "v1.5 · date" for the document. DSH-13 (choose tooling against the requirements at the start) is the budget rule. DSH-15 (gaps render as gaps in every state) and DSH-03/DSH-18 (the default view answers unaided; explanation beside, not in a tooltip) are what this chapter needs to survive without script. The seven tests' "Phone" and "Screen-reader" lines are section 9 and the twin.

**Conflicts.** One direct. DSH-21 classes "empty and error states" as Decorative, "free within the rules above"; this chapter holds that the degraded state — the last good copy kept, its age in words, which series is missing and since when — is a Fixed procedure (AWS REL05-BP01; RFC 5861; the SRE workbook), and that a tile which blanks on a failed fetch is a defect and not a style. The rule register needs the state split: how the degraded tile *looks* is Decorative; *that* it shows the last good copy with its age is Fixed. Two tensions. The design system's recognisable face is a styled vector map, and this chapter prices that at over two-fifths of the script budget of the seventy-fifth-percentile phone before a tile arrives; whether the face is worth the budget is a creative decision, but the system needs a raster or static fallback state and a twin-table component before the decision can be made with its cost visible. And the design system has no component for the four-part freshness statement (period, published, revised, frequency) and no running-foot slot for the data's date as distinct from the document's; the chapter asks for both and leaves their form to the designers. **Logged for the design system:** a freshness component; a degraded-tile state; a map component with twin, buttons, attribution and supplier-logo slots; a stated byte budget and baseline device; a script-free rendering requirement. **Logged for the Decision Book:** a chapter on running a dashboard — freshness, dependencies and terms, refresh, watch and alarm, objective and degraded mode, budget, tile supply, retirement, owner — or an extension of Chapter 15 that carries them.
