# Working record · Chapter 4 — From data to evidence

*How this chapter was made. These sections stood in the chapter at draft 2 and were moved here, word for word, on 14 October 2026 when the book was finished. They are the record of the method: what the chapter expected against what it found, what it did to the chapters before it, the gaps it met, where it met the design system and the Decision Book, and the loose ends it flagged. Nothing here is a rule; the rules are in the register, the open choices in the decision log, the probes in `03-tests/PROBES.md`.*

## Before you start

This is the fourth chapter of a research book written for Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio led by Carly Heath, about how dashboards work and why. The book exists to support the Vibe Map — a planned public, non-commercial, interactive account of the people, activity, value and support systems that sustain a place's nightlife culture, built first for the West of England. You do not need to have read Chapters 1 to 3 to read this one. Four things from them recur: the working definition — *a dashboard is a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader*; Carly's failure register, the eight ways she says a dashboard fails (two of them bear on this chapter: the display that is "a fancy spreadsheet with some charts that tell you lots of big numbers, but not what actually happens", and the one that "breaks or isnt reliable"); Chapter 2's finding that there is no reader in general, and its sceptical reader who prefers a plain table; and Chapter 3's seven rules for showing data, which assume the number being shown is already fit to be shown. Chapter 1 named the "heartbeat" — the visible age of a display's data — and the "aggregation of the biggest", a total that speaks for its largest contributors; both return here. This chapter is about that assumption. Where it refers to Carly, it means the studio's founder; "the brief" is her written brief for this book; where it refers to the Vibe Map, it is illustrating, not specifying. The studio's own rulebook for design decisions is called the Decision Book, and its visual rules live in a design system (Style Guide v1.5); this chapter tests the Decision Book's chapter on data structure, description and disclosure against the standards it cites.

Two conventions, explained fully in Chapter 0. Every rule in this book carries a **kind** — *Standard* (an external rule nobody here wrote), *Evidence* (a named study), *Reading* (our interpretation, open to challenge), *Practice* (a practitioner's framework or craft knowledge, not tested as research) or *Nightlife Culture* (a decision or finding of the studio's own) — and a **tier**: *Fixed* is a hard line with a right answer; *Adaptable* is a stated default you may argue with and must log if you leave; *Decorative* is free. Flags in square brackets mark loose ends: `[Further research]`, `[Prototype flag]` (something the Vibe Map can settle with its own data), `[Validate with users]`, `[Revisit → Ch n]`, `[Revisit at end]`, `[Rich reference]` and `[Counterbalance → Ch n]`. A dagger (†) after a source means it was read at the authors' published abstract only. Where a quotation reached this chapter through a document other than the one it comes from, the text says "as quoted in" or "as reproduced by".

This chapter is heavier on standards than on experiments, because the ground it covers — what a number must carry before it may be called evidence — has been written down by statistics offices, regulators and standards bodies rather than discovered in laboratories. Where a standard is quoted it is quoted exactly, with its number, because the point of a standard is that it can be looked up. Three bodies recur and are introduced once here. The *Office for Statistics Regulation* (OSR) is the UK's statistics regulator, part of the UK Statistics Authority; it publishes the *Code of Practice for Statistics*, which is now in its third edition (published 3 November 2025, replacing the 2018 edition that most commentary still cites). The *Government Analysis Function* is the UK civil service's professional home for analysts and the owner of the guidance on tables, charts and spreadsheets that the Decision Book already adopts. *Eurostat* is the statistical office of the European Union, whose quality vocabulary is written into European law and whose online glossary is used here for definitions because it is public and plain.

## Looking back

Against Chapters 0 to 3, the archived version 1, and the studio's Decision Book.

- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's "aggregation of the biggest" with the arithmetic (a total is a weighted statement about its largest terms), the instruments (Lorenz, Gini, top-share, HHI) and an experimental mechanism (visible popularity increases concentration). **Strengthens** Chapter 1's suppression paragraph with the full vocabulary of what a blank can be and the two honest trades (suppress, perturb). **Opens a thread** for Chapter 1: the Code of Practice it cited is now edition 3.0, and its Standards for Public Use — "cite sources", no "undue certainty" — are a regulatory version of Chapter 1's argument that trust is earned by conduct. `[Revisit → Ch 1]`
- **Strengthens** Chapter 2's "the denominator is the persuasive unit" by showing it is also the *methodological* unit: a rate exists to compare unequal populations, and the reference class is a choice with a philosophical name. **Strengthens** Chapter 2's "release numbers with their data and caveats so an opposing coalition's analyst can check them": the provenance set and the comparability declaration are what "checkable" means. **Counterbalances** Chapter 2's finding that the sceptical reader prefers a plain table — a plain table of a skewed quantity is a table of its biggest rows; the plain table needs the median and the share too.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 3's "nothing crucial behind a click" in one direction and **counterbalances** it in another. The gap rule (never join across a missing point) is a Chapter 3 rule about words and marks that this chapter found in a standard; but twenty lines of provenance and comparability cannot all be on the face, and the chapter proposes that the fact of qualification is what must be visible. `[Counterbalance → Ch 3 §9]` **Strengthens** Chapter 3's rule that a word for uncertainty carries its number: a word for a nothing ("n/a", "—") carries its kind. **Opens a thread** for Chapter 3's precision rule: the Census 2021 "1" is not a 1.
- **Weakens** version 1's Chapter 8 in one respect and **strengthens** it in another. Its gap vocabulary — *never measured · not yet collected · suppressed for confidentiality · community-held · collection break* — mixed kinds of nothing with reasons for nothing, and this chapter's official mapping separates them; "collection break" is [b], a property of a series rather than a cell; "not yet collected" matches the ABS's "n.y.a." and the lagged series; "community-held" — consented non-publication — appears in no official vocabulary this chapter found, and is a Nightlife Culture kind for Chapter 10 to own. Version 1's rules that gaps render in every filter state, that nothing is interpolated across a break, and that suppression is enforced at query time with secondary suppression are all consistent with the standards found here and survive. `[Revisit → Ch 10]`
- **Strengthens** version 1's Chapter 3 (the map) on what a pin is: a pin has a grain (local unit, enterprise, room), and version 1's critique of a music-venue map — that when every pin is equal no pin is argued for — is, in this chapter's terms, a display that has not stated its unit of observation.
- **Chapter 0.** Two markings were proposed by Chapter 3 and both are used here, the second often, and this chapter's cold reader asked a fair question of the tier vocabulary — what "Fixed as a stance" or "Fixed as a procedure" obliges a reader to do. Chapter 0 should say: a rule Fixed *as a stance* is one the studio adopts and will not argue about on a given display, though its source is a reading or a practice rather than a standard or a finding; a rule Fixed *as a procedure* fixes the steps, not the parameter. The markings used: *Practice backed by analogous Evidence* (not needed often in a standards chapter) and *Evidence (adjacent domain)* (the midnight census; the missing-data literature; the outlier-reporting literature — all from medicine and psychology). A third marking earned its place in this chapter: *Secondary* — a quotation that reached the chapter through a named reproduction, used where the source could not be opened and the reproduction was independent or institutional. Chapter 0's legend should carry all three.
- **The Decision Book.** Its Chapter 8 (data structure, description and disclosure) and Chapter 9 (tables and provenance) were tested against the standards they cite. **Agrees:** ISO 8601 with descending order and offset; natural dates in display; boundary vintage with every geographic figure; no comparison across vintages without a stated lookup; published codes not names; published classifications with version; the suppression rules including secondary suppression and the ~60% floor; fixed categories to prevent differencing; re-identification assessed across the publication; "ship the full provenance set"; precision matching the data; and "A statistic without provenance is a claim, not a finding". **Conflicts:** two. The chart rule "Show gaps explicitly — dashed, hatched, or broken, with a note" (DAT-14) can be read as permitting a dashed join, which the Analysis Function's chart guidance explicitly rejects — "do not join the points either side of the missing data point, even if the line is dotted or dashed"; the rule should say *break*, never *dash across*. And the provenance set (DTA-19's list: source, reference period, geography and vintage, method, revision status, disclosure treatment, illustrative flag) omits the responsible party and the issued/modified dates that every standard includes; the illustrative flag is a sound addition the standards lack. **Silent on:** the unit of observation and the row count; the day-boundary rule; the denominator and the reference class; the funnel for small bases; the six kinds of nothing and the blank-cell rule (its suppression rules cover one kind); "No to NA"; zero as a value; free as a value; imputation flagging and the author of an estimate; the official sense of "reliable"; the comparability declaration beyond vintage; comparator-group method and the circularity caution; concentration measures beside totals; the big-data paradox and the ten characteristics; outlier rules, thresholds and with-and-without; positive deviance. All logged for Carly as candidate additions. `[Counterbalance → Decision Book Ch 8]`

## Design system (Style Guide v1.5) and Decision Book — conflict log

**Agrees.** The design system's provenance-on-every-chart rule and its status chips (COL-17: "Status and provenance chips carry a short code that states the tier in text") are the right furniture for truths 2 and 3: a code in text, never colour alone, is what the Analysis Function's square-bracket shorthand is. "Strong design must never make weak evidence look stronger than it is" is, in this chapter, the gap that is not joined, the [p] that is not dropped, and the platform figure that is not the city. The Decision Book's Chapter 8 — ISO 8601, boundary vintage, codes not names, disclosure before design, secondary suppression, the ~60% floor, the provenance set — is a correct reading of the GOV.UK, GSS and Analysis Function standards it cites, and its line that this layer "has almost no creative latitude, and that is a feature" is the right stance.

**Conflicts.** Two in the Decision Book, logged in Looking back: the gap rule's "dashed" and the provenance set's two omissions. One in the design system, and it is small: the status-chip vocabulary (as far as this chapter has seen it) carries the *tier* of a claim — Fixed, Adaptable, Decorative — and the *status* of a figure (provisional, estimated, suppressed), but not the *kind of nothing* in an empty cell. A chip set for the six (or seven) kinds would be the design system's part of truth 2. `[Counterbalance → design system]`

**Silent on.** Everything in sections 3, 4, 8, 9 and 10: the unit of observation; the day boundary; the denominator; concentration beside totals; the reading of platform feeds; outlier procedure. These are not visual rules and may not belong in a style guide; they belong in the data contract and in the Decision Book's data chapters, and this chapter's rules table is the candidate list.
