## Before you start

This is the seventh chapter of a research book written for Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio led by Carly Heath, about how dashboards work and why. The book exists to support the Vibe Map — a planned public, non-commercial, interactive account of the people, activity, value and support systems that sustain a place's nightlife culture, built first for the West of England. You do not need to have read Chapters 1 to 6 to read this one. Five things from them recur: the working definition — *a dashboard is a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader*; Chapter 4's rule that a comparison declares what it holds equal, in a ten-line declaration; Chapter 5's finding that a rank carries an interval and is "no longer a magic number"; Chapter 6's ladder of causal claims, on which a dashboard speaks from the first rung and "contributed to" is earned by a tested theory of change; and Chapter 6's control chart, whose limits the data sets and whose false-alarm rate is stated. Where this chapter refers to Carly, it means the studio's founder; where it refers to the Vibe Map, it is illustrating, not specifying. The studio's rulebook for design decisions is the Decision Book; its visual rules are in a design system (Style Guide v1.5).

Two conventions, explained fully in Chapter 0. Every rule carries a **kind** — *Standard*, *Evidence*, *Reading*, *Practice* or *Nightlife Culture* — and a **tier**: *Fixed*, *Adaptable* or *Decorative*; "Fixed as a stance" marks a rule the studio adopts whose source is a reading or a practice, and "Fixed as a procedure" fixes the steps and not the parameter. Flags in square brackets mark loose ends: `[Further research]`, `[Prototype flag]`, `[Validate with users]`, `[Revisit → Ch n]`, `[Revisit at end]`, `[Rich reference]` and `[Counterbalance → Ch n]`. A dagger (†) marks a source read at the authors' abstract or summary only; "secondary" marks a quotation reached through another named document; "reconstructed" marks an abstract rebuilt from an index; "(read once)" after a quotation means it was returned on a single reading and not confirmed by a second — the independent verifier later confirmed every such sentence in this chapter, and the mark is kept so the reader can see where the first draft was thinner. The *symmetry test* (Chapter 5) means that every standard this chapter applies to others' comparisons it applies, in section 11, to the studio's own; the *failure register* is the brief's list, in Carly's words, of the ways a dashboard fails — among them "if it looks the same every month" and "if it's just a fancy spreadsheet with some charts that tell you lots of big numbers, but not what actually happens". Some terms from earlier chapters recur without re-argument: the *badge rule* (Chapter 6: no "significant" badge stands alone as the reason for a highlight); the *forking paths* (Chapter 6: every filter is a fork, and a pattern found at the end of one is a multiple-comparisons result); the *holdout* (Chapter 6: a pattern is a finding only on data that did not produce it); the *price list* (Chapter 5: the rate at which an index trades one part for another); the *one-tailed search* (Chapter 5: an instrument built to find shortfall, or thriving, finds the small and the lucky); the *dynamic census* (version 1's proposal for a living series with a lead over official statistics, tested in Chapter 6); "the face" and "one tap" (Chapter 5: what is on the display itself, and what is one interaction away); the *three user groups* (the studio's named readers for testing). Abbreviations used in quotations: SHMI (the NHS Summary Hospital-level Mortality Indicator); SPC (statistical process control); QMI (an ONS quality and methodology information report); EPR (the External Panel Review of *Doing Business*); C3 (the Cultural and Creative Cities Monitor's index); SMS (the Maryland Scientific Methods Scale).

## 11 · The studio's own comparisons, tested

Version 1 of this book had a chapter on comparing places, and the studio's steers added positions of their own. The symmetry test requires that every standard this chapter has applied to others' comparisons be applied to these, in a list, before any is kept. The standards, from the sections above: a public measure changes what it measures, and a comparison is a public measure (section 3); a rank carries intervals too wide to separate most pairs (section 3); peers are chosen by a published method and not by the compared (section 4); like is compared with like by standardisation, and an observed-to-expected ratio is compared with the expectation (section 4); one place against itself over time needs a season, a length and a model named (section 6); before and after is Design 2 unless a comparison is added (section 7); a projection is a range with its assumptions, its track record and its switching value (section 9); and every comparison declares what it holds equal (section 1). One standard is this chapter's own addition, not drawn from a source: a verb that orders places — "beats", "ahead of", "behind" — is a ranking, whatever the display.

**"Identity is never ranked; conditions are compared through a gate."** Version 1 split a scene's measures into identity axes — "different, never better" — and condition axes — "better or worse, and policy-actionable" — and required the second to pass the design system's comparability gate. The first half holds, with one correction to version 1's examples. A nominal property cannot be ordered, and section 4's partial-order literature gives the method for saying "incomparable" when the properties conflict; but some of what version 1 listed as identity — "the shape of a scene's week", "who its audiences are" — can be put on an ordinal scale, and the studio's decision to treat them as identity is a decision, to be declared as one, not a fact about the measures. The second half needs this chapter's additions to the gate. A comparison of conditions across places is a comparison of rates across sizes, so Chapter 6's both-tails arithmetic applies and the gate must include the interval; it is a comparison of places that differ in more than the condition, so it must include standardisation or a declared decision not to standardise; and if the standardisation is against a common expectation, the ratios are compared with the expectation and not sorted against each other. The gate, as version 1 drew it, checked whether two indicators were the same indicator. It now also checks whether the two places are comparable on it, and whether the display orders them.

**"Default to the place-centred view: one place versus itself over time and versus peers it chose."** The first half is section 6 and 7's territory, and it is weaker than version 1 assumed. One place against itself over time is a before-and-after series — Design 2 — whenever a reader attaches a cause to a change in it; it cannot be seasonally adjusted until it is three years long; its latest smoothed point is its least reliable; its year-on-year rate moves for what happened a year ago; and if the place was chosen for attention because its number was extreme, regression to the mean is the first explanation. The place-centred view survives as a default, with those five conditions on its face. The second half fails the test as written. "Peers it chose" is the self-selected comparator, and the evidence this sprint could reach — from executive pay, at abstract, with its qualifications given in section 4 — is that the choice tends to flatter the chooser and that disclosure is the known check. Version 1's reason for the proposal — that the choice "reveals who each place believes its peers are — which is itself scene knowledge nobody currently collects" — is a good reason to *collect* the choice. It is not a reason to let it define the comparison. The amended position: the comparison set is produced by a published method with a stated distance and a fixed number — the ONS's twenty nearest neighbours, or the DfE's ten with their closeness labels, or the studio's own, documented to that standard — and any peers a place chooses for itself are shown beside that set, labelled as chosen, with the choice itself kept as data. That builds in the disclosure the executive-pay literature points to. Two things about the amended position should be said. The number of neighbours scales with the field — twenty of twenty-three districts is not a peer set, and the official methods were built for hundreds of authorities — so the number is "fixed and appropriate to the field", not a borrowed twenty. And the variables that define similarity are, in the end, chosen by someone; the studio choosing them with the scene is the compared choosing, one level up. The difference the method makes is that the choice is made once, published, and applied to every place alike before any comparison is drawn, rather than made afresh by each place for each comparison — which is the lever the pay literature found pulled. `[Prototype flag]`

**"No view survives cropping as a ranking."** Version 1's rule follows from section 3 and is kept, with a sharpening from the *Doing Business* successor: B-READY dropped the aggregate rank and kept quintile bands "from highest to lowest performers". Bands of position are an ordering, and a cropped display of places in five coloured tiers is a league table with fewer rows; the rule covers them. It does not cover categories of statistical separation — the funnel's alert and alarm, the ONS's three words — for the reason section 5 gave: those describe a place's distance from its own expectation and move with the place, not with the field. The symmetry test applies to them too, and the funnel and the three words are public measures like any other: a place shown "lower" will work to be shown "similar", and the funnel's limits become targets as surely as a rank did. Chapter 5's rule that the audit is designed with the measure applies to the funnel as it applied to the composite. It also meets the chart standard's "Always rank bars by value", and section 5 gave the reconciliation: a display may be sorted when the sorting is a reading aid and the finding is position against limits — the funnel, the distribution with the place marked, the peer set with its closeness labels. A sorted bar chart of places with no interval and no expectation is the thing the rule forbids, whatever its colour.

**"A borough with a scene beats a borough with a superclub."** Version 1 used this sentence to make a true point — that per-head intensity and total volume order places differently, which is Chapter 4's denominator rule — and made it with a rank verb. By this chapter's own standards the sentence fails: "beats" is an ordering, the two boroughs are being ranked on an intensity measure whose interval at borough scale is wide, and the claim that the hyperlocal view "genuinely reorders things" is a claim about a rank. The point survives in the form the method allows: the two denominators produce two different pictures, neither is a ranking, and the display shows both with what each holds equal. The symmetry test's purpose was to catch exactly this — a sentence the studio likes, in the form the chapter forbids to others.

**The comparability gate itself** (Style Guide §12: "Every chart comparing two or more places must state whether the indicator comparability gate has been cleared"). It is confirmed and extended. Chapter 4's ten-line declaration is the gate's content for the indicator; this chapter's comparison record, in section 12, is the gate's content for the comparison — who the peers are and who chose them, what was standardised, what the interval is, whether the display orders.

**Scenario claims — "x policy change gives y benefit at z risk".** The brief's phrase is the Green Book's object, and the Green Book's duties apply to the studio as they apply to a department: an explicit adjustment for optimism bias, by the studio's own record of projection against outturn if it has one and by the generic uplifts if it does not; a central estimate with a range; the switching value; an even number of scenarios; the label "projection"; and the words for likelihood carrying their numbers. The studio has no track record yet, which means its first projections carry a declared adjustment — the generic table for any cost or duration, a stated judgement for any benefit, because the table does not cover benefits — and say so; keeping the record, from the first projection, is the only way to earn a smaller one. `[Prototype flag]`

**The "dynamic census" and the leading claim** were tested in Chapter 6 and are not re-tested here; section 10 adds only that the producer of the oldest leading index hedges its own definition with "Historically" and "generally" and admits the real-time record was worse than the published one.

What survives the list: the identity/conditions split, with its examples corrected; the place-centred default, conditioned; the cropping rule, sharpened to cover bands of position and not categories of separation; the comparability gate, extended; and the scenario form, with the appraiser's duties attached. What does not survive as written: self-chosen peers as the comparison set, and the "beats" sentence. The amendments stand on the same evidence the chapter used against everyone else.

## Looking back

Against Chapters 0 to 6, the archived version 1, and the studio's Decision Book.

- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's truth that a published measure changes what it measures, with the strongest institutional case in the book: a global index whose rankings invited "external interference and conflicts of interest", were manipulated by staff, and were removed on the recommendation of the Bank's own review. Rankings are reactivity with a position attached.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 2's finding that the official reader has official methods, and names three that Chapter 2 did not: standardisation, the published peer set, and the appraiser's duties (optimism bias, switching values, ranges). **Opens a thread**: the official reader's own most famous uncertainty display was recommended for removal in 2024 and gone by 2026; one official forecaster now shows scenarios and the other still shows a fan.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 3's "show the shape" with the funnel, and its time-series rules with the end-point warning and the seasonal-adjustment caveats; **counterbalances** its small-multiples rule with the chart standard's "Always rank bars by value", which a small multiple of places obeys at its peril.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 4's comparability declaration and extends it: the declaration held the indicator equal; the comparison record holds the places, the chooser, the season and the design equal too. **Counterbalances** it: two places can carry an identical indicator and still not be comparable on it. Adds vintages and breaks beside revision status. `[Counterbalance → Ch 4]`
- **Strengthens** Chapter 5 as promised — the rank interval and the funnel are one discipline — and gives the sector exemplar's robustness figures exactly where Chapter 5 and version 1 had paraphrased them.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 6 throughout: the ladders are the rungs made operational; the placebo test is the holdout built into the design; the funnel is shrinkage seen from the other side; regression to the mean is the one-tailed search's commonest false positive; spillover and displacement are the ripples Chapter 6's displacement section needed a method for. **Counterbalances** Chapter 6's control chart: the history the chart draws its limits from is itself revised by seasonal adjustment when a new point arrives. `[Counterbalance → Ch 6]`
- **Strengthens** version 1's Chapter 5 in three positions (identity never ranked; no view survives cropping; the comparability gate) and **weakens** it in three: the place-centred default survives only with five conditions on its face; peers chosen by the place fail the symmetry test on the evidence (at abstract, from another field) of self-selected comparators; and "a borough with a scene beats a borough with a superclub" is a ranking sentence in a chapter against rankings. - **Opens a thread** for Chapter 0: four records now sit beneath a dashboard's numbers (provenance set, recipe card, claim record, comparison record); the legend should say whether they are one; and "Fixed as a procedure" has become the commonest tier in the book for rules whose steps are fixed and whose parameters are judgements.

The rules table above is the candidate for a Decision Book chapter on comparison. `[Counterbalance → Decision Book]`

## Design system (Style Guide v1.5) and Decision Book — conflict log

**Agrees.** Style Guide §12 — "Every chart comparing two or more places must state whether the indicator comparability gate has been cleared… A comparison without this note is not publishable" — is this chapter's first truth, and the comparison record is the gate's content extended from the indicator to the places, the chooser, the season and the design. "Strong design must never make weak evidence look stronger than it is" is, here, the sorted bar chart of places with no interval, the smoothed line reaching its last point, the single projected figure, and the before/after marker with no rivals listed. "Every big number carries a source line" extends to every year-on-year figure carrying its base month. The Decision Book's interval rule (Ch 6), vintage rule (Ch 8), arrow rule (Ch 10), small-base filter rule (Ch 15) and "does the causal chain hold" (Ch 16) all agree with this chapter and are extended by it.

**Conflicts.** None direct. Two tensions. The Decision Book draws on the Analysis Function's chart standard, which sorts bars by value; this chapter's ranking rule forbids a sorted display of places unless the sort is visibly a reading aid and the finding is position against limits — the design system needs a form for position against limits, or a rule for when a sorted bar is and is not a league table. And the design system's status chips mark a claim's firmness without a place for the comparison's terms — who chose the peers, what was held equal, which rung — which the comparison record carries and which a reader needs before a comparison's firmness means anything. `[Counterbalance → design system; Decision Book Ch 6]`
