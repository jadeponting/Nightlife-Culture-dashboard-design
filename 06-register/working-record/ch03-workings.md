# Working record · Chapter 3 — The canonical truths of showing data

*How this chapter was made. These sections stood in the chapter at draft 2 and were moved here, word for word, on 14 October 2026 when the book was finished. They are the record of the method: what the chapter expected against what it found, what it did to the chapters before it, the gaps it met, where it met the design system and the Decision Book, and the loose ends it flagged. Nothing here is a rule; the rules are in the register, the open choices in the decision log, the probes in `03-tests/PROBES.md`.*

## Before you start

This is the third chapter of a research book written for Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio led by Carly Heath, about how dashboards work and why. The book exists to support the Vibe Map — a planned public, non-commercial, interactive account of the people, activity, value and support systems that sustain a place's nightlife culture, built first for the West of England. You do not need to have read Chapters 1 and 2 to read this one. Two things from them recur: the working definition — *a dashboard is a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader* — and Chapter 2's finding that there is no reader in general. Where this chapter refers to Carly, it means the studio's founder; "the brief" is her written brief for this book; where it refers to the Vibe Map, it is illustrating, not specifying. The studio's own rulebook for design decisions is called the Decision Book, and its visual rules live in a design system (Style Guide v1.5); this chapter tests the Decision Book's chapter on charts against the evidence. The three user groups the Vibe Map will be tested with are policymakers and politicians, advocates and trade bodies, and funders and investors, with the people who make the night as co-developers.

Two conventions, explained fully in Chapter 0. Every rule in this book carries a **kind** — *Standard* (an external rule nobody here wrote), *Evidence* (a named study), *Reading* (our interpretation, open to challenge), *Practice* (a practitioner's framework or craft knowledge, not tested as research) or *Nightlife Culture* (a decision or finding of the studio's own) — and a **tier**: *Fixed* is a hard line with a right answer; *Adaptable* is a stated default you may argue with and must log if you leave; *Decorative* is free. Flags in square brackets mark loose ends: `[Further research]`, `[Prototype flag]` (something the Vibe Map can settle with its own data), `[Validate with users]`, `[Revisit → Ch n]`, `[Revisit at end]`, `[Rich reference]` and `[Counterbalance → Ch n]`. A dagger (†) after a source means it was read at the authors' published abstract only.

Two statistical conventions, since this chapter reports experiments. *d* is the difference between two groups measured in standard deviations, where 0.2 is conventionally small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. *n* is how many people took part. Where a finding comes from a few dozen students in one laboratory, the chapter says so; where it comes from thousands of people across countries, it says that too, and the difference matters more than the headline.

## Open questions and flags

- `[Further research]` **Legends against direct labels.** No controlled chart experiment exists. It is cheap to run and the field should have run it; so could the prototype. `[Prototype flag]`
- `[Further research]` **Dual axes.** No controlled study found; Isenberg et al. is about dual-scale charts. The prohibition is Practice.
- `[Further research]` **Does interacting improve understanding?** No study found shows that a reader who uses an interactive control understands more than one who does not; McKenna found comprehension flat and Boy found no extra exploration. The claim that interactivity is itself a good is untested here.
- `[Further research]` **Replicate Ajani et al.** The headline-plus-highlight result rests on 24 people and is the chapter's most-used experimental finding after the title studies; it needs a second sample.
- `[Further research]` **The big-number tile.** No study of whether a KPI number is read, trusted or misread against a chart or table cell. `[Prototype flag]`
- `[Further research]` **Franconeri et al. 2021, last third.** The uncertainty and summary sections were not reached by the fetch tool; the publisher's summary was used for the ending. Read pages ~140–161 directly.
- `[Further research]` **Abstract-only sources behind rules (†):** Birch 2012; Yang et al. 2021; Romano et al. 2020; Pighin et al. 2011; Vessey & Galletta 1991; Galesic & Garcia-Retamero 2011; Borgo et al. 2012; Inbar et al. 2007; Lindgaard et al. 2011; Jerez-Fernandez et al. 2014; Zhang & Schwarz 2012; Okan et al. 2012; Boy et al. 2016; Garcia-Retamero & Cokely 2013; Shah & Hoeffner 2002; Harrower & Brewer 2003; Kinkeldey et al. 2014.
- `[Further research]` **Sevi et al. 2020** — a reported null on log/linear scales and policy attitudes; not fetched; would qualify Romano's attitude findings (not its comprehension finding).
- `[Further research]` **Reda & Szafir 2021 "Rainbows Revisited"** — unreachable; would sharpen the task-dependence of the rainbow rule.
- `[Further research]` **Tufte at source.** The data-ink and chartjunk passages are quoted in their standard reproductions; check against the second edition before print.
- `[Further research]` **Galesic & Garcia-Retamero's item-level results** — which graph tasks the public failed most — need the full paper.
- `[Further research]` **COGA's two numerical patterns** ("Provide Alternatives for Numerical Concepts"; "Do Not Rely on Users Calculations or Memorizing Information") — open the body text for Chapter 13.
- `[Further research]` **Fernandes et al. 2018** (quantile dotplots improve *decisions*, not only estimates) — rate-limited; worth adding to section 10.
- `[Further research]` **Schwabish 2021**, the practitioner companion to Franconeri — access-restricted; its five principles would be a useful Practice counterpoint.
- `[Prototype flag]` **Measure the glance.** The Vibe Map can record time-to-first-interaction, what is touched, and on which device — the numbers this chapter had to borrow from newsrooms and journals.
- `[Prototype flag]` **Measure chart literacy.** The statistics regulator found no UK survey that captures the ability to read charts and tables; a short test modelled on the Visualization Literacy Assessment Test, run with the three user groups, would be a contribution in its own right.
- `[Validate with users]` **The sceptic's uncertainty.** Show the same finding with a bare line, an ensemble and a dotplot to readers who arrive opposed, and ask what it means.
- `[Validate with users]` **The designer's blind spot.** Before any default is fixed, have someone who did not choose it say what the first screen shows.
- `[Validate with users]` **Big number against sentence.** The same fact as a KPI tile and as a one-line sentence; which is remembered, which is trusted.
- `[Revisit → Ch 5]` Precision, rounding and effective digits belong with building a measure.
- `[Revisit → Ch 7]` Stock against flow; the truncated axis as a choice of effect size; small multiples for comparison across places.
- `[Revisit → Ch 11]` The first screen: fast-glance forms; upper-left; title then filters; defaults as advice; mobile first because touch screens have no hover.
- `[Revisit → Ch 12]` Recognition of place begins with a schema the reader already has.
- `[Revisit → Ch 13]` The rest of the W3C classification; COGA; the Analysis Function and ONS rules as adopted standards.
- `[Revisit → Ch 14]` Prototypicality against novelty; ornament that is the data; where the flyer's energy can live without touching the mark.
- `[Revisit at end]` **Does "canonical" survive?** Nineteen truths is still a long list; the closing chapter should ask which were cited by later chapters and demote the rest.
- `[Counterbalance → Ch 2 §5]` Markant: shown uncertainty may harden the strongly opposed.

## Looking back

Against Chapters 0, 1 and 2, the archived version 1, and the studio's Decision Book.

- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's truth 8 and Chapter 2's truth 12 (numeric uncertainty costs no trust) by supplying the *forms* — and **counterbalances** both with Markant: for the strongly opposed, shown uncertainty may be read as grounds for doubt. `[Counterbalance → Ch 2 §5]`
- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's truth 11 (rankings are screens, show intervals) with the caveat that a bare interval is itself misread; the interval must be shown as something countable.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's "scale by a denominator" rule and Chapter 2's "the denominator is the persuasive unit" with the stock/flow and 1-in-X findings: the choice of scale, baseline and denominator moves the reader whether or not it was meant to.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 2's finding that charts persuade through information, not aura: ornament behind the data costs time and buys memorability of the image, not of the finding; what gets the finding remembered is words and one highlight.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 2's "plain table for the hostile reader" with cognitive fit (tables for point values) and with the title-trust ordering: readers trust the data most and the title least while remembering the title. The plain table is the most-trusted component made the whole display.
- **Opens a thread** for Chapter 2's "name each reader": the schema a reader brings is part of what defines them; a form the officer reads fluently (a stacked area chart) is the one the public fails most.
- **Weakens** version 1's Chapter 1 (archived) in one detail: its "label at the mark" rule was tiered Fixed on a display audit, and this chapter finds it rests on no direct experiment and should carry that status. And a verification note: version 1 quoted Jachimowicz et al. for "individuals commonly perceive defaults as conveying an endorsement by the choice architect" as "read at source"; this chapter's research agent reported the sentence absent from the authors' PDF, and both the orchestrator's re-check and the independent verifier found it present in both the author PDF and the published text, citing McKenzie et al. 2006. The quotation stands; the episode is logged because a research agent's correction can itself be wrong, and the chapter's own first draft repeated the error before the verifier caught it.
- **Chapter 0.** The *Practice* kind earns its place again (Tufte; Ehrenberg; Few; the Analysis Function; Tse; the F-pattern), and a sub-kind is worth naming in the legend: *Practice backed by analogous Evidence* — a rule nobody has tested directly whose mechanism is understood and whose neighbours are well evidenced. "Label at the mark" is the type specimen.
- **The Decision Book.** Its Chapter 6 opens with the perceptual hierarchy as the finding "almost every chart rule" descends from, and this chapter tests it. **Agrees:** bars start at zero (and the Analysis Function checklist it cites already gives the better reason); label at the mark (with the evidential status now stated); never round beyond the data; alt text states the finding; show uncertainty on modelled values; four-series limits as voluntary Standard; creativity in layout not encoding. **Conflicts:** the reasons given for banning 3D ("volume is fifth") and constraining pies ("angle … two and three ranks down") rest on the conjectural half of the hierarchy — the rules survive on other grounds (3D adds nothing and obscures; pies fail on near-equal slices), and the Decision Book should cite those grounds instead. The rule "Use the highest encoding on the perceptual ranking" is Fixed there and should be Fixed only for precise comparison, Adaptable as a general form rule, given Davis et al. **Silent on:** the title's power to decide the reading; how much text a chart should carry; the truncated-axis finding that labels do not help; the choice of stock or flow; the forms of uncertainty display beyond "show it"; the first-glance evidence; interaction use rates; defaults as advice. These are logged for Carly as candidate additions to the Decision Book's data chapters. `[Counterbalance → Decision Book Ch 6]`

## Design system (Style Guide v1.5) — conflict log

**Agrees.** "Strong design must never make weak evidence look stronger than it is" is, in this chapter, the truncation finding, the stock/flow finding and the within-the-bar bias. The guide's provenance-on-every-chart rule and its evidence components are consistent with the two-part text alternative and the data-download rule. The guide's one-type-rule and status chips are not contradicted by anything here.

**Conflicts.** One, inherited from Chapter 2 and sharpened. The guide's joy-first register and its licence for expressive form sit against this chapter's first-glance evidence (appeal rewards simplicity and prototypicality), its ornament evidence (anything that touches the mark costs accuracy; anything behind the data costs time), and the Decision Book's own rule that in dashboards creativity lives in layout and hierarchy, not encodings. This is not a conflict in principle — the design system already says meaning never lives only in the decorative layer — but it is a conflict in practice every time a chart is styled, and the guide may want a line that says where on a dashboard the joy goes: words, hierarchy, register, imagery around the chart; never the mark, the axis or the colour that carries a value. `[Counterbalance → design system]`

**Silent on.** The title as the message-carrier (the guide governs headings, not chart titles); the amount and placement of text on a chart; the forms of uncertainty display; truncation; stock against flow; the public defaults for scale and risk; interaction use rates and the no-hover phone; defaults as advice. All candidates for the design system's dashboard section when it exists.
