# Working record · Chapter 11 — The first screen and the path through it

*How this chapter was made. These sections stood in the chapter at draft 2 and were moved here, word for word, on 14 October 2026 when the book was finished. They are the record of the method: what the chapter expected against what it found, what it did to the chapters before it, the gaps it met, where it met the design system and the Decision Book, and the loose ends it flagged. Nothing here is a rule; the rules are in the register, the open choices in the decision log, the probes in `03-tests/PROBES.md`.*

## Before you start

This is the eleventh chapter of a research book written for Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio led by Carly Heath, about how dashboards work and why. The book exists to support the Vibe Map — a planned public, non-commercial, interactive account of the people, activity, value and support systems that sustain a place's nightlife culture, built first for the West of England. You do not need to have read Chapters 1 to 10 to read this one, which opens Part 3, on the reading experience. "Version 1" means the book's first, archived edition, whose positions this chapter re-tests. Several things from the earlier chapters recur, among them: the working definition — *a dashboard is a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader*; Chapter 3's settled findings on the glance, on words, on defaults as advice, on how few readers touch anything and on "overview first", which this chapter cites and does not re-argue; the seven records the earlier chapters left beneath a dashboard's numbers — provenance set, recipe card, claim record, comparison record, service record, measure record, safeguards record — each of which asked how much of itself sits on the face `[Revisit → Ch 11]`; Chapter 8's rule that figures render before script and every map has a table twin; Chapter 9's question of how a distribution of felt answers is shown; and Chapter 10's three nothings — withheld, unmeasured, held — which must be told apart on a face without placing the held units. Where this chapter refers to Carly, it means the studio's founder; where it refers to the Vibe Map, it is illustrating, not specifying. The studio's rulebook for design decisions is the Decision Book; its visual rules are in a design system (Style Guide v1.5).

Two conventions, explained fully in Chapter 0. Every rule carries a **kind** — *Standard*, *Evidence*, *Reading*, *Practice* or *Nightlife Culture* — and a **tier**: *Fixed*, *Adaptable* or *Decorative*; "Fixed as a stance" marks a rule the studio adopts whose source is a reading or a practice, and "Fixed as a procedure" fixes the steps and not the parameter. This chapter uses *Standard (design system)* for the published guidance of a public design system — GOV.UK's, the ONS's, the NHS's, the civil service Analysis Function's — which is practice, binding on its own publishers, and backed by stated user research only where it says so. Flags in square brackets mark loose ends: `[Further research]`, `[Prototype flag]`, `[Validate with users]`, `[Revisit → Ch n]`, `[Revisit at end]`, `[Rich reference]` and `[Counterbalance → Ch n]`. A dagger (†) marks a source read at the authors' abstract or summary only; "secondary" marks a quotation reached through another named document; "(read once)" marks a quotation returned on a single reading and not confirmed by a second. The *symmetry test* (Chapter 0) means that every standard this chapter applies to others' displays it applies, in section 10, to the studio's own.

Because this chapter is about the surface a reader meets, it uses a handful of words in one sense each. The *sentence* is a view's headline message — what it says, to whom — written within the house's word limit; the *title* is the sentence as it sits above a chart. The *first screen* is what a reader sees on arrival before doing anything; its configured state is the *default view*, in Chapter 3's sense; a *landing pattern* is one of the six shapes a first screen takes, named in section 3. A *view* is a panel in one state; a *state* is the set of values of every selection that makes a view; a *query* is the request for a state; an *address* is the URL that encodes one; a *snapshot* is a dated, unchanging copy of the data and text behind a view; a *version* is a numbered edition of a dataset. The *face* of a panel is its visible surface, and the chapter distinguishes three distances from it — *on the face* (seen without acting, including by scrolling the page the panel is on), *one step away* (opened from the face by one action and shown beside it), and *on request* (a separate page reached by a link, such as the method page); Nielsen's phrase "progressive disclosure" appears only inside quotation marks, because Chapter 10 reserved "disclosure" for the statistical sense. The *authored account* is what the maker wrote — the sentence, the lines beside the charts, any text generated by rule — and *exploration* is what the reader does to make states; the word "story" appears only in quotations and in the names of the genres that use it. An *interaction* is anything a reader does that changes what is shown, and a *control* is the thing they do it with; *hover* is a pointer resting without a click; a *tooltip* is content shown on hover or on keyboard focus; *touch* is a pointer that cannot hover. An interaction's *purchase* is what it buys, version 1's word, kept; its *costs* are Lam's seven, named in section 6. *Generated text* is sentences produced by rule from data, which the ONS calls "semi-automated". An *empty state* is an input-first screen before input, and is never called a nothing; the *three nothings* are Chapter 10's. A *small screen* is a phone; "mobile" appears in quotations and figures. *Rhetoric* is Hullman and Diakopoulos's sense — how a display argues — and the house's "how this argues" page is its statement of its own. *Stigma* is Chapter 10's sense, for maps. The *brief* is Carly's written commission for this book. A few of the book's working words recur: the *poster rule* is the house's limit on a headline — five words where possible, ten at most; the *crop test* is version 1's rule that whatever is cut out of a view and shared must carry its provenance with it; the *walk* is Chapter 10's friendly-intruder test of a view's filters; a *provenance tier* and a *claim rung* are the statuses Chapters 4 and 6 give a figure's source and its causal strength; a layer is *lit* when shown at its own resolution and *held* when shown coarser or not at all by decision; the *floor* is Chapter 10's disclosure or precision threshold; the *two columns* are version 1's counted-beside-missed display, due in Chapter 12; the *three user groups* are the readers the studio tests with (Chapter 2); DSH and COL codes are rules in the Style Guide; PECR is the UK's electronic-communications privacy regulation (Chapter 10). Where "the fetch tool's reader" is mentioned, it means the text-only reader this book's research used to open pages.

A note on this chapter's sources. The research agents again had no web search. The empirical literature on dashboards and interaction is partly open (authors' copies, arXiv, open journals, the public design systems) and partly closed, and the closures this time fell on two of the most-cited negative results — the storytelling null of Boy, Détienne and Fekete (2015), whose repository sits behind a bot wall, and Song and Szafir's study of missing data — so that version 1's figures for the first could not be re-verified and are carried here only with a flag and through what other papers say of it, and the second is cited at its abstract. The fetch tool's reader invented four fluent quotations from a W3C specification on first pass, every one of which the presence check rejected; nothing from that page is quoted. The author spot-checked sixteen sources the same day (about ninety strings with controls; all held). An independent verifier then re-traced the chapter's load-bearing claims in about 125 pages — some six hundred strings and figures — and found seven wrong (among them a prediction gain attached to the wrong dataset, a map-reading percentage given a sentence that was not on the page, and a GOV.UK phrase read as a rule it is not), five cut in a way that changed their sense and three misattributed; all are corrected in this draft, and the sources it confirmed are marked "verified" in the list at the end. A cold reader with no project context then read the whole and found, among other things, that the chapter's own pass test measured recitation of a sentence while its own evidence said readers recite a title whether or not the chart supports it; the test now asks for the figure and a next step as well. Where the fetch tool's reader invented quotations, the page was a W3C specification on media queries, not the WCAG criteria quoted in section 6, which were each confirmed; it also reshaped two design-system sentences, caught the same way. Every quotation given was confirmed present on its page, or is marked as weaker than that.

## 10 · The studio's own positions, tested

The symmetry test: every standard this chapter applied to others' displays is applied to the studio's own positions — version 1's Chapters 1, 9 and 10, the Decision Book's Chapters 7 and 15, and the Style Guide's dashboard section — and each is left as *holds*, *holds with a change*, or *does not hold*.

*Write the sentence first; the visitor's question, not the institution's description.* **Holds, and gains its evidence**: titles first in the one reading-order study; titles over charts in recall; the public design systems' inverted pyramid. The second half — the reader's question over the maker's self-description — was a reading from version 1's audit and stays a reading; Chapter 2's finding that personal relevance turns a decision, and the search evidence that the data sought "was often deeply personal", are its nearest support.

*Five words where possible, ten at most.* **Holds as the house's rule**, beside the official limits it out-toughens: chart titles "15 words or fewer", headings "fewer than 60 characters", GOV.UK titles "65 characters or less". The ten-word ONS rule version 1 cited is gone from the ONS's pages; the house rule did not rest on it.

*The six landing patterns; message-then-input with an exemplar chosen as an endorsement.* **Holds as a reading, and the chapter says so plainly**: no study compares the six. What was found supports the pieces — message first (reading order; the ONS's "shows the main message without needing any interaction"); input beside content (search adopted when offered with content, not instead of it); the exemplar as anchor (Chapter 3); configuration-first as a toll (one or two in a hundred). The ONS's own area tool is still input-first, which is a choice the chapter records and does not endorse.

*Freshness and provenance as first-screen furniture.* **Holds with a change.** The NHS found review dates "away from the main content, closer to the footer" were not noticed; the health dashboard stamps "Up to and including" beside every chart; so the stamp belongs beside the figure. But the trust evidence says a source line is read as a signal that there is "no need for the user to check", and metadata made charts look "more thorough" without making their reading more accurate. The change: furniture on the face is a status in text that opens the record, not a credential; and the sentence comes before the furniture, because readers skip long text and read 28 per cent.

*The purchase table.* **Holds as a procedure; its rows are re-priced** in section 6: personalisation strongest; collection and adjacency now governed by Chapters 9 and 10; reweighting priced by the one public index's uptake; filter and drill priced by Chapter 10's query-time rules; prediction narrowed to unfamiliar data; linking and sharing added. The attribution version 1 gave the interaction taxonomy — that its authors call it descriptive and not prescriptive — **does not hold as worded**; they say validation "is a community project" and the taxonomy "may be incomplete", which serves the same purpose in their words.

*Nothing crucial behind click or hover; caveats visible by default, never in tooltips.* **Holds**, with its standard named (1.4.13 and its reasons), its practitioner source for touch, the design systems' own evidence on hidden content, and one qualification from the ONS's components: a tooltip that is keyboard-reachable and spoken is not crucial content behind hover, it is a redundant reading of content that is also on the face or in the table below; the rule is about what is *only* there.

*Every reachable state has a stable, citable address; shares obey the crop rules.* **Holds with a change, and the change is the chapter's largest.** An address is not enough: the national archive usually cannot hold an interactive page, links rot and content drifts, and the one public model that took citation seriously moved its citations to dated snapshots. The rule becomes three layers — a canonical address that encodes the selection; a dated, unchanging snapshot of data and text; a generated citation naming both — and the snag that two addresses can name one state is designed out by canonical ordering. The crop rule holds and gains the snapshot: a cropped share carries the snapshot's date.

*Prediction before reveal; one staged transition per reshape.* **Hold, narrowed.** The prediction gain is about four per hundred for a guess followed by an explanation on data of middling familiarity, smaller on unfamiliar data and nothing on familiar, and a bare guess bought nothing on unfamiliar data; the house's proposed home for it (a guess at a place's worth before the reveal) therefore needs the explanation step and a check on how familiar the figure is to the reader, and the guess is collectable only under Chapters 9 and 10. The transition rule rests on Chapter 3's evidence and is unchanged.

*The poster beside an open door; "the story is never a gate".* **Holds as a bet with three mechanisms** (section 4) and one unverified pillar: the storytelling null could not be re-read, and version 1's numbers for it are withdrawn from the chapter's text until someone with access confirms them `[Further research]`. The shape does not depend on them.

*The "how this argues" page.* **Holds, and gains precedents and a caution.** The vocabulary is the rhetoric paper's four layers and "provenance rhetoric"; the precedents are the ONS's quality-and-methodology notes "designed to give you information on the strength and limitations of the data", the ONS's own "About these articles" panel on its generated pages, and a newsroom whose method is "We build datasets from scratch, bulletproof our reporting, and show our work"; the caution is that the page is itself rhetoric, and that the one experiment on method notes found they changed perceived thoroughness and what was remembered, not accuracy. The page is written for the reader who checks, and its existence is a signal to the reader who does not.

*Per-place written sentences from templates in the house register.* **Holds, with its cost named.** The method is the ONS's 331 pages: topics ranked per place, phrases by rule, every page checked, labelled on the page, "not a labour-saving tool". Two rules join it: a generated sentence is labelled as generated, because readers' ratings move with what they are told about a text's origin, so what they are told must be true; and it knows the series breaks it speaks across, because the one famous error was a stock split the template did not know about.

*Reader contributions as a published layer.* **Holds only as Chapters 9 and 10 allow**: participatory, not co-produced, until what it changed is shown; collected on a basis chosen for publication; held or lit by the community's governance; its sample on the face.

*Scrollytelling as editorial output, priced as unevaluated.* **Holds, with the price updated**: one controlled test found recall equal to video and better than text per exposure, and worse than text per second; scroller against stepper made no difference; the practitioners say "up for debate, but it doesn't hide content". Editorial, still.

*The Style Guide's dashboard section and the Decision Book's Chapter 7 and Chapter 15.* The Decision Book's "name the purchase" (Chapter 7) is this chapter's procedure and gains Lam's cost side. Its Chapter 15 rules — nothing load-bearing in a tooltip; no filter combination may mislead unannotated — hold, the second now with Chapter 10's query-time machinery. The Style Guide's DSH rules were read against this chapter: DSH-15 and DSH-16 (gaps render as gaps; carry uncertainty through filtering) are section 8's; DSH-12 (observed testing with real users) is the two-readers and three-phones tests; DSH-21, which Chapter 8 found treats error states as decorative, meets this chapter's finding that a degraded state is a state with a data version and belongs in the face sheet. **Gaps**, logged in the conflict log: no rule on the first screen's sentence per place, on landing pattern, on citable states and snapshots, on the face sheet, on the small screen as the first version, on map breaks held across views, or on labelling generated text.

*The dashboard's own justification.* The chapter's first truth says a dashboard is a form to be justified, and the symmetry test applies it to the Vibe Map: the named reader, the cadence of updates, the need for combination and the resource to maintain it are Chapter 1's, Chapter 8's and Carly's to state, and the face sheet's first line is where the first of them is written for every view. **Recorded as a question the studio answers, not one the book answers for it.**

*Measuring the dashboard's own use.* Version 1 proposed it. **Holds, under Chapter 10**: "how your service is used, not about who uses it"; published, aggregate, dated; and read with the Bayesian study's caution that the people who interact are not the same people as those who do not.

## Open questions and flags

- `[Further research]` **The unreached.** Boy, Détienne & Fekete 2015 (full text; the null's numbers withdrawn until confirmed); Hullman et al. 2013 (sequence; n); Zhi, Ottley & Metoyer 2019 (full text; effect sizes); Song & Szafir 2019 (full text; the conditions and direction of findings); Lam 2008 (full text); Dimara & Perin 2020 (the definition; the review's size); Hullman, Adar & Shah 2011 (the caveats); Xiong et al. 2019 (n); Andreasson & Riveiro 2014 (n); Bartram, Patra & Stone 2017 (findings); Blascheck et al. 2019 and Boy et al. 2016 (discovery of interactivity); Seyser & Zeiller 2018; Stolper et al. 2016; Thudt et al. 2018; Kennedy et al.'s published text; Wilkinson 2005's own pages; the Kaspar et al. 2011 and Sun & Li originals; Ivanković et al. 2021 (158 public COVID dashboards); Kitchin et al. on city dashboards; the UK Data Service's citation guidance; any UK measurement of government link rot; the ONS Census maps' display rationale; the OECD's working paper on BLI responses; the Analysis Function's dashboard software page.
- `[Further research]` **Abstract-only (†) and single-pass:** marked throughout; Chapter 3's summary of McKenna et al. is corrected here (no stepper/scroller difference; comprehension flat).
- `[Further research]` **A head-to-head test of landing patterns for a data tool.** None exists. `[Prototype flag]`: the prototype can run two — message-then-input against input-first, on the same data, with the three user groups — and measure time to the sentence and whether the input is used.
- `[Further research]` **Tooltip discovery and use on touch devices.** No measured study reached; the argument rests on practitioners and a sister criterion. `[Prototype flag]`: instrument the prototype's own tooltips, aggregate, and report the share of sessions that opened one, by device class, under Chapter 10's analytics rule.
- `[Further research]` **Dot-density, hexagon and tile maps against choropleths**, and red as danger on a map, measured. None reached; Schiewe's dark-is-more is the nearest.
- `[Further research]` **Reach figures for public statistical interactives.** "large publishers are unwilling to share internal metrics"; no newer field figure found. `[Prototype flag]`: publish the prototype's own, aggregate and dated — the first of its kind if so.
- `[Prototype flag]` **The three nothings on one face**, with readers' confidence in the neighbouring figure measured for each.
- `[Prototype flag]` **A citation that survives a year.** Cite a prototype state in a document, change the data, and test whether the snapshot and citation still find what was cited; test the canonical-address rule by trying to make two addresses for one state.
- `[Prototype flag]` **Matched legends across filter states.** Hold the breaks across periods and peer sets, and test whether readers compare two views correctly; then re-break per view and test again.
- `[Validate with users]` **The sentence on a phone.** Whether a reader of each user group, on their own device, says the sentence back; and how much furniture (stamp, unit, floor, chips) sits between the top of the screen and the sentence before they skip.
- `[Validate with users]` **Generated sentences in the house voice.** Whether readers notice, mind, or rate them lower when labelled, as the meta-analysis predicts; and whether the poster rule survives generation.
- `[Validate with users]` **The small-area form.** Whether an inset, a tile map or a second view lets the scene's smallest districts be found, against Schiewe's 30–40 per cent.
- `[Validate with users]` **The "how this argues" page.** Who opens it; whether the sceptical reader finds what they came for; whether its existence changes anything for those who do not.
- `[Further research]` **One sentence or three.** The book names three user groups and the chapter writes one sentence per view; whether a first screen carries one sentence for all three, or three sentences the reader chooses between, is not settled by any evidence found. `[Validate with users]`: write both and test which each group says back.
- `[Further research]` **The returning reader.** The official condition for a dashboard is that "users are likely to revisit"; every test in this chapter is of a first visit. What a returning reader needs on the first screen — what changed since last time, in a sentence — is Chapter 8's freshness question read from the other side, and no study of it was found.
- `[Further research]` **What the regime costs the maker.** A face sheet per view, snapshots per release and two tests per first screen are not priced here, in a chapter that opens with the official warning that dashboards "require a lot of maintenance"; the Decision Book's candidate chapter should carry a minimum version `[Revisit → Ch 16]`.

- `[Revisit → Ch 14]` The editorial layer above the dashboard; what a creative treatment may do to the first screen's sentence, the map's breaks and the stamp; the embellishment evidence against this chapter's "text is not clutter".
- `[Revisit → Ch 16]` The face sheet as a build artefact; the purchase table's cut list; the retirement plan.

## Looking back

Against Chapters 0 to 10, the archived version 1, the Decision Book and the design system.

- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's "write the sentence first" with the reading-order study and the title-recall evidence, and its landing audit by naming what it is (a reading with single-pattern evidence beneath it); **counterbalances** its first-screen furniture: furniture is a signal and must open the record. `[Counterbalance → Ch 1]`
- **Strengthens** Chapter 2's finding that personal relevance turns a decision: the one control readers reach for is the one that finds them; and its official reader, who takes a view into a paper, now has a citable state to take.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 3 throughout — its interaction-reach, defaults and schema findings are this chapter's premises — and **corrects** its summary of McKenna et al. `[Counterbalance → Ch 3]` **Opens a thread** on text density: Chapter 3's words evidence meets Stokes et al.'s "not penalized for clutter".
- **Strengthens** Chapter 4's "a cell is marked, never blank" with the missing-data experiments (dropping the gap was most biased; explaining it beat blurring it), and its status codes as the right furniture.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 5's finding that a default weighting is itself the index with the one public index's uptake of reweighting; and its audit rule with the dashboard measuring its own use.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 6's "a templated claim is still a claim" with the generated-text method, its error case and its label.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 7's funnel and declared peers as display objects, and its "no ranking" with matched legends and quantile breaks as the mechanisms that keep a series of maps comparable without a league table.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 8 (figures before script and the table twin are now a national design system's component rules and a W3C equivalent route; the degraded state is a state with an aged data version; retirement is a lifecycle stage) and **counterbalances** its map pricing with the perceptual cost. `[Counterbalance → Ch 8]`

Logged, not designed.

## Design system (Style Guide v1.5) and Decision Book — conflict log

**Agrees.** The Decision Book's Chapter 7 rule — a departure names what it buys — is this chapter's interaction procedure, now with the seven costs on the other side of the ledger. Its Chapter 15 rules on tooltips and misleading filter combinations hold and are given their standards (1.4.13; Chapter 10's query-time machinery). The design system's text-coded status chips (COL-17) are the right form for the records' kinds on the face. DSH-12 (observed, task-based testing with real users) is the two-readers and three-phones test. DSH-15 and DSH-16 are section 8's rules for gaps and uncertainty through filtering.

**Conflicts.** One direct, carried from Chapter 8: DSH-21 treats error states as Decorative, and this chapter finds a degraded state is a state — a data version with an age — that belongs in the face sheet's line 8 and on the face with its stamp. Four gaps that will become conflicts if unaddressed. The Decision Book treats a view's address as a stable link; this chapter finds a link is not a citation and that a dated snapshot and a generated citation are needed, so the prototype's share button, as the Decision Book would have it, would produce citations that drift. The design system's map component has no rule that breaks are held constant across filter states, so two views of one series will be drawn on different legends and read as different. Neither document says what a generated sentence is labelled as, so a templated district sentence would read as written. And neither names the small screen as the first version, so the phone will receive what is left after the desktop is designed, which the corpus evidence says is the message's remainder. **Logged for the design system:** a snapshot banner (archive date; previous, next, live); a generated-citation block; a generated-text label; a small-area inset or tile-map pattern with the legend held; a "how this argues" link in the furniture; a status chip that opens its record; the degraded-state stamp. **Logged for the Decision Book:** a chapter on the surface — the sentence first and per place; the landing pattern as a declared endorsement; the small screen first; the authored account beside the open door with linking; the purchase table with costs; hover, pointer and target rules; one address per state; snapshot and citation; records' kinds on the face and followable; the three nothings; map rates, breaks and the stigma check; the two-readers and three-phones tests; own-use measured as how, not who; the retirement plan — or an extension of Chapters 7 and 15 that carries them.
