# Working record · Chapter 10 — Safety, ethics and consent

*How this chapter was made. These sections stood in the chapter at draft 2 and were moved here, word for word, on 14 October 2026 when the book was finished. They are the record of the method: what the chapter expected against what it found, what it did to the chapters before it, the gaps it met, where it met the design system and the Decision Book, and the loose ends it flagged. Nothing here is a rule; the rules are in the register, the open choices in the decision log, the probes in `03-tests/PROBES.md`.*

## Before you start

This is the tenth chapter of a research book written for Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio led by Carly Heath, about how dashboards work and why. The book exists to support the Vibe Map — a planned public, non-commercial, interactive account of the people, activity, value and support systems that sustain a place's nightlife culture, built first for the West of England. You do not need to have read Chapters 1 to 9 to read this one. Several things from the earlier chapters recur, among them: the working definition — *a dashboard is a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader*; Chapter 4's six kinds of nothing, one of which is a cell withheld for confidentiality and one of which — "community-held" — the book carried forward to this chapter because no official vocabulary has it; Chapter 6's rule that the identifying threshold is set by the scene's own knowability, above any national rule; Chapter 8's finding that every live source is a dependency on someone else's terms; and Chapter 9's measure record, whose last line — *what this changed* — outlives any consent that was given at the start, and whose floor of fifty answers pulled against the brief's rule that a dashboard fails "if its an aggregation of 'the biggest'". Where this chapter refers to Carly, it means the studio's founder; where it refers to the Vibe Map, it is illustrating, not specifying. The studio's rulebook for design decisions is the Decision Book; its visual rules are in a design system (Style Guide v1.5).

Two conventions, explained fully in Chapter 0. Every rule carries a **kind** — *Standard*, *Evidence*, *Reading*, *Practice* or *Nightlife Culture* — and a **tier**: *Fixed*, *Adaptable* or *Decorative*; "Fixed as a stance" marks a rule the studio adopts whose source is a reading or a practice, and "Fixed as a procedure" fixes the steps and not the parameter. This chapter adds two sub-kinds Chapter 9 proposed: *Standard (law)* for the text of a statute or regulation, and *Standard (regulator)* for a regulator's guidance, which is not law unless it is a statutory code and is said to be. Flags in square brackets mark loose ends: `[Further research]`, `[Prototype flag]`, `[Validate with users]`, `[Revisit → Ch n]`, `[Revisit at end]`, `[Rich reference]` and `[Counterbalance → Ch n]`. A dagger (†) marks a source read at the authors' abstract or summary only; "secondary" marks a quotation reached through another named document; "(read once)" marks a quotation returned on a single reading and not confirmed by a second. The *symmetry test* (Chapter 0) means that every standard this chapter applies to others' methods it applies, in section 18, to the studio's own.

Because this chapter is about law as well as method, it says plainly what it is and is not. It reports what statutes, regulators, standards bodies and studies say, with the wording and the date, and it does not give legal advice; nothing in it is a substitute for the studio's own counsel on its own facts. The law it reports moved under the chapter's feet: the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 was brought fully into force for data protection on 5 February 2026 and reorganised the research provisions the brief expected to find; the office of Information Commissioner was abolished and its functions transferred to an Information Commission on 30 September 2026, six days before this chapter's sources were read; and most of the regulator's guidance pages carry a notice that they are under review. The chapter therefore cites every statutory text as read on legislation.gov.uk on 6 October 2026, with its in-force date, and every regulator page with the date shown on it. Where the brief's expected map was out of date, the chapter says so rather than quietly correcting it, because the gap between what a careful reader expected and what the law now says is itself a finding about how fast this ground moves.

Because the chapter uses words that have one meaning in statute and another in ethics, and a third in the studio's own vocabulary, it fixes each to one sense. *Consent* is used only in its statutory sense — a "freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous indication of the data subject's wishes" — and only of a person; where the ethics literature speaks of what a community can give, the chapter uses the literature's own words, *agreement* and *consultation*, and section 11 says why the three are kept apart. *Disclosure* means statistical disclosure — learning who someone is (*identity disclosure*), learning something about someone already identified (*attribute disclosure*), or learning something about a group (*group disclosure*) — and never means telling people things; for that the chapter says *publish*, *state* or *tell*. A *release model* is the regulator's phrase for how far data goes: to the public, to defined groups, or nowhere outside the organisation. A cell is *withheld* when a disclosure rule removes it from a view; a layer is *held* when the studio and the people it describes decide that it exists, is kept and is not shown. There are two *floors*, and the chapter qualifies the word every time: the *disclosure floor* is the minimum count below which a cell is withheld, which Chapter 6 called the identifying threshold; the *precision floor* is Chapter 9's fifty answers, which is a rule about reliability and not about identification. A *row* is a microdata record; the word *record* is kept for the documents this book leaves beneath a dashboard. A *view* is a panel in one filter state; a *query* is the request that produces it. The *motivated intruder* is the regulator's legal test of whether information is identifiable; the *friendly intruder* is the Office for National Statistics' test procedure, a person asked to try. A *group* is people who share an attribute, including a group assembled by an algorithm; a *community* is people with relationships, a voice and some capacity to decide; a *scene* is the nightlife word for a community, used where the chapter speaks of the studio's own case — its positions, its rules, its illustration — and not in the general argument. *Harm* has the kinds Citron and Solove list and three objects — persons, groups and places. *Public* is qualified wherever it occurs — "in public" against "being public", "public release", "public interest", "the public good", "publicly available" — because the arguments in this chapter turn on which is meant. *Anonymous information* is a statutory status; *anonymisation* is the process that aims at it; *effective anonymisation* is the regulator's term for the measures that reach the legal threshold; *pseudonymised* data is personal data still. *Perturbation* is the general word for a deliberate small change to a published count; *noise* appears only inside quotations about differential privacy. *Moderation* is Grimmelmann's "governance mechanisms that structure participation in a community to facilitate cooperation and prevent abuse". The *brief* is Carly's written commission for this book; the *house framework* is the studio's own Vibe Setting Framework, unpublished and confidential, which this book cites and never reproduces. A few of the book's own working words recur: the *face* of a panel is its visible surface; a layer is *lit* when it is shown at its own resolution; the *money view* is the house's name for the panels that put a value on a scene; the *small room* is the brief's case of a place too small to pass a floor; the *table twin* is Chapter 8's rule that every map has a table saying the same thing; a *cell key* is the census method that makes the same cell perturb the same way in every table; a *legitimate interests assessment* (LIA) is the regulator's record for the three-part test; *RAS purposes* is the statute's name for research, archiving and statistics. Where "the fetch tool's reader" is mentioned, it means the text-only reader this book's research used to open pages.

A note on this chapter's sources. The research agents again had no web search. Statute was read at legislation.gov.uk, where every page carries the standing notice that revised legislation "may not be fully up to date", and the pages read reported themselves current to dates between 24 September and 5 October 2026; regulator guidance was read at ico.org.uk, ofcom.org.uk, gov.uk, ons.gov.uk and the UK Statistics Authority; the method and harm literatures were reached where they were open and marked † where they were not. The fetch tool's reader invented text on first pass in three places — a regulator's definition of community consultation, a six-part list in a data-trusts report, and page numbers for a law-review article — and all three were caught by the agents' second fetch and discarded. The author spot-checked seventeen sources the same day (about a hundred strings, controls included; every quoted string was found, four of them on a sibling page of the one first tried). An independent verifier then re-traced the chapter's load-bearing claims in about two hundred fetches — some six hundred strings, figures, dates and section numbers — and found five wrong (among them a 48-hour take-down limit inserted into the Online Safety Act on 29 June 2026 that the first draft missed), five misattributed and a dozen cut in a way that changed their sense; all are corrected in this draft, and the sources it confirmed are marked "verified" in the list at the end. A cold reader with no project context then read the whole, and its objections — above all that a held layer marked on a single room is itself a disclosure — changed section 14, the sixth truth and the record. Every quotation given was confirmed present on its page, or is marked as weaker than that.

## 18 · The studio's own positions, tested

The symmetry test: every standard this chapter applied to others' methods is now applied to the studio's own positions, as recorded in version 1 of this book, in the Decision Book's chapters on disclosure and uncertainty, and in the brief. Each is stated, tested, and left as *holds*, *holds with a change*, or *does not hold*.

*Threshold three for small cells.* Version 1 and the Decision Book withhold counts under three. The national policy lists "suppressing very small numbers (usually numbers less than three)" among its methods; its suggested minimum for online tools is five; the health service's standard the regulator cites is five; Chapter 6 set the floor by the scene's knowability, above any national rule. **Holds with a change:** three is the static-table value, and this chapter's finding is that a dashboard with filters is an online tool, for which the same office suggests five and a judgement "depending on the sensitivity of the table as a whole". The value is Adaptable; the procedure — a floor per view, set above the national rule where the scene can name the units, and stated — is Fixed. And the cost is now named: "suppression rules disproportionately impact small population groups", so every floor is paired with a banded or held form that keeps the small present.

*Secondary suppression.* **Holds**, and is restated for views: "If a cell requires protection in one table then it will require protection in all tables". The alternative the census uses — a consistent cell key, so that the same cell perturbs the same way in every view — is a legitimate substitute the Decision Book does not mention, and section 20 leaves the choice open.

*Fixed categories.* **Holds**, and gains its reason: "it is good practice, where possible, to have a set of standard classifications to use to release data", because differencing needs two cuts. A filter that lets the reader define a category, draw a boundary or choose a time window is the opposite of a fixed classification, and the rules now say that any such control is treated as a table builder and protected as one.

*Suppression at query time, with the reason.* Version 1 said a withheld cell says why. The census builder "will not provide details of which rule or rules have caused the areas to fail", and its design paper keeps parameters private because knowing them "may increase confidence in apparent attribute disclosures". **Holds with a change:** the *status* is always shown — a cell that is not shown is marked, never blank, and the kind of reason (disclosure floor; precision floor; held) is stated, because Chapter 4's six nothings require it — but the *rule and its parameters* need not be, and a dashboard may decide, as the national office does for its table builder (which says "because of the disclosure rules" and no more), that saying "fewer than five" tells an intruder which way to look. The reason is shown at the kind, not at the parameter, unless the parameter is itself published policy.

*The adversarial filter walk.* Version 1 proposed walking the filters as an adversary before release. **Holds, and is named**: it is intruder testing — "friendly intruders", "around a half day", "Each identification claim is given a percentage of confidence", and the standard that "a good result would be if there were a small number of correct claims with low confidence" — applied to an interactive view. Two changes: the walker is someone who knows the scene, because the motivated intruder is a regular; and the walk is not a substitute for the method, because the location-app case shows a system that passed casual inspection and failed systematic querying. The walk's claims, confidences and consequences are a line in the record.

*"Consent to be found."* Version 1 proposed that a scene consents to be findable. Section 11: a scene cannot consent; it can be consulted, govern and agree. Section 9: consent as a lawful basis fails where withdrawal cannot be fully actioned, and a published view cannot be withdrawn from those who read it; and data collected on consent cannot later take the statistics route (Article 8A(4)). **Does not hold as worded.** What the house meant survives in three parts with three names: individuals who contribute do so on a basis chosen for the fact of publication, told the withdrawal tiers; the scene is consulted and the consultation is recorded; and the layer that makes the scene findable is held or lit by a governance the scene recognises, with the decider named. The phrase to use is *agreed to be shown*, and the agreement is understood to add legitimacy and nothing to any person's rights.

*"The money view shows only consented scenes."* Same test, and two more. 84C(3) excludes from the research provisions processing "for the purposes of measures or decisions with respect to a particular data subject"; a money view built to move funding towards named places is, on its face, a view built for measures, and whether the subjects of those measures are "particular data subjects" is a question for counsel that the book records and does not answer. And section 15: the money view is the coordinate-for-speculation panel — "a leading indicator for housing price changes". **Does not hold as worded; holds as a procedure:** a money view is lit for a scene only after the scene's governance has agreed it, the harm assessment has considered the speculation reading, and the record says both; and the view is shown as Chapter 7 requires, against its own history, never as a ranking.

*Community-held as a kind of nothing.* **Holds with a change, and is now a status with precedents** (section 14): known, used, shown at a coarser resolution or not at all, decided by a named process, reason public, status visible for the category and never for the unit, tested by the walk, reviewed on a date. The change is that version 1 imagined the status on the room; the precedents and the chapter's own rules put it on the category, because a marked room is a disclosure. Chapter 4's carry-forward is discharged: the official vocabularies have no word for it, and conservation, heritage and environmental law have the thing.

*The floor of fifty against the small room.* Chapter 9 left this `[Revisit at end]`. This chapter separates the floors: fifty is a precision floor on a mean and has nothing to do with disclosure; the small room is lost to reliability, not to identification, and it is shown as *present, n answered, not scored*, or as *held*, never as absent — "potential risk should not necessarily be used as a rationale for avoiding local or small area data altogether". The tension is narrowed, not closed: the end of the book still decides whether a room under both floors is shown as unmeasured or held, and who decides which.

*The extraction finding.* Chapter 9 carried Chambers's "mix and balance of extraction and empowerment" as consequences evidence. This chapter gives it a frame — data justice's visibility pillar, CARE's collective benefit, responsible data's "unintended consequences" — and a line: the safeguards record's last, *what this changed*, which asks after launch who benefited from being made visible and who paid.

*Platform terms as transparency questions.* Chapter 8 read platform terms as the dependency's conditions. **Holds, and is widened**: data about identifiable accounts taken from a platform is data not obtained from the subject, owed Article 14's notice or a published reason, and "There is no automatic exception from the right to be informed just because the personal data is in the public domain".

*The studio's own analytics.* The house will want to know who reads the dashboard. PECR's statistics exception "is about how your service is used, not about who uses it"; segmenting readers by demographic "cannot rely on the statistical purposes exception"; wifi probe collection is outside it. **A new position, recorded:** the dashboard's own use is counted as aggregate use, without inferences about categories of reader, or it is counted on consent.

*The studio's own site as a regulated service.* If readers can place pins or write text that other readers see, the Online Safety Act's definition reads as applying, with no size exemption. **A new position, recorded:** contribution is a decision to run a regulated service — a named individual, a risk assessment within three months, a moderation function, a complaints route — at a small service's scale.

*Who is in the room.* The studio's history with a policing form that asked promoters to describe who would attend an event is Carly's knowledge, enters this chapter as frame, and is not researched here. It is the reason the chapter treats any panel that describes the people in a room — by ethnicity, faith, sexuality, health — as a panel whose inference is the controller's, on the regulator's intention test "regardless of how confident you are", and therefore as special-category processing needing a condition, a DPIA and the consultation of section 11; and it is the reason the chapter treats "what onlookers could (rightly or wrongly) infer" from a busy-venues map as a fairness question that the harm assessment must answer in writing.

*A nameable venue.* Most of a nightlife dashboard's small cells are businesses, and data-protection law protects natural persons; a sole trader or a venue named after its owner is a person in the statute's eyes, and a company is not. The statistics code protects "individual and business information" alike, and that is the standard the house adopts for its floors. What a studio owes a nameable business beyond confidentiality — in defamation, in commercial harm from a figure or a colour — was not researched for this chapter and is recorded for counsel `[Further research]`.

*The official reader as intruder.* Chapter 2 named the official reader as one the dashboard serves. This chapter adds that a licensing authority, a police force or a council can read the same panel as a list of premises to visit, and that nothing in the disclosure methods distinguishes that reader from any other. The house's history with a policing form (below) is why the harm assessment's "persons" and "groups" columns ask, for every panel, what a reader with power over the venues could do with it; and why a public-body partner that holds the held layer, the walk's claims or the assessment is asked, before it holds them, whether they can be requested from it under information law `[Further research]`.

*The book's own method.* This chapter's agents read public pages, and the frames of section 17 apply to them. The pages read were those of statutes, regulators, standards bodies, publishers and named organisations, and the content of no individual was fetched or quoted; the one case reached only through an encyclopaedia names a private person who was identified against her will in 2006, and the chapter records the case and does not repeat the identification. The chapter's rule for itself — quote the institution, not the person — is the one it asks of a dashboard.

*"A dashboard is not a research project."* The house has said the dashboard is a civic instrument and not a study. The statute defines research and statistics by method and output, not by actor; the regulator's indicative criteria are hypotheses, scrutiny, publication, ethics and committees; and the exclusions are substantial damage or distress, measures or decisions about particular persons, and — in the regulator's gloss — informing "the services you provide to them". Chapter 1 defined a dashboard as a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader. Whether that decision is a "measure or decision with respect to a particular data subject" is the question on which the dashboard's fit with the research and statistics provisions turns. **Recorded as open, for counsel**: the book cannot answer it and says so.

## Looking back

Against Chapters 0 to 9, the archived version 1, the Decision Book and the design system.

- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's trust failure — "it fails if people dont trust it" — with the regulator's and the statistical system's own tests for what a trusted release does, and **opens a thread** on its definition: a display that turns data into a decision is the sentence that brings a dashboard nearest to the law's "measures or decisions". `[Counterbalance → Ch 1]`
- **Strengthens** Chapter 2's sceptical reader with a new question — *who could find themselves in this?* — and its official reader with the DPIA, the assessment's consultation step and the published record they will expect. **Opens a thread**: the scene reader is now also a governing body, and section 12's door is Chapter 2's access finding from the other side.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 3's "show the shape" and its colour rules with the statistical system's stigma warnings ("Red traditionally spells danger"; large empty areas dominate), and **opens a thread** for a display object the book did not have: three nothings told apart on one face.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 4 throughout and **discharges** its carry-forward: "community-held" is a status with precedents in conservation, heritage and environmental law; and **counterbalances** its rule that a withheld cell says why — the national office's table builder states the kind and neither rule nor parameter, for a stated reason, and the chapter narrows "why" to the kind. `[Counterbalance → Ch 4]`
- **Strengthens** Chapter 5's split of recognition into legitimacy and validity with the Ada Lovelace definition of legitimacy as "the broad base of public support", and its audit rule as the practice that finds gaming.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 6's knowability rule with the regulator's own sentence ("one person or group knows a great deal about another person") and **counterbalances** its single threshold with a floor per view and a form that keeps the small present. `[Counterbalance → Ch 6]` **Strengthens** its warning about the precarious who leave no record: the small are the ones suppression removes, and not at random.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 7's rule against ranking with the measured harm of a graded map and the gentrification-indicator evidence; a comparison panel is a potential price signal.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 8: platform terms are transparency duties as well as dependency terms; the service record gains the office's "appropriate controls will have to be kept under review"; the site's own analytics have a rule; and the committed-fetch repository is why withdrawal is graded. **Opens a thread**: whether the walk and the audit are one scheduled job.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 9 in three places (the floor of fifty is a precision rule and the small room's loss is to reliability; who answered is where gating's cost is counted; "what this changed" outlives consent) and **retires** its phrase "consent at scene level" for consult, govern, agree. `[Counterbalance → Ch 9]` **Qualifies** its CARE citation with the owner's caution.

## Design system (Style Guide v1.5) and Decision Book — conflict log

**Agrees.** Suppression with secondary suppression (Decision Book Chapter 8) is this chapter's rule for static views. DSH-15 ("Gaps render as gaps in every filter state") and DSH-16 ("Carry uncertainty through filtering, or suppress below a base threshold") are the disclosure floor and the precision floor stated for every view; this chapter adds that the floor for an interactive view is higher than for a table. COL-17's text-coded status chips are the right furniture for *withheld*, *unmeasured* and *held*. BRF-04's "affected, influential, exposed" are the three names in the record's lines 7 and 8. The seven tests' "Dancefloor — retains cultural truth, avoids institutionalising the culture?" and "Partner — does the language make room for collaboration and recognise others' contribution?" are section 12 applied to a surface.

**Conflicts.** One direct. Version 1 and the Decision Book require a withheld cell to say *why*; the national office's practice, for a stated reason, is to say neither the rule nor the parameter, and this chapter narrows the house rule to the *kind* of reason — disclosure floor, precision floor, held — with the rule and parameter optional. The Decision Book's text should carry that narrowing, or state that the house publishes its parameters as policy and accepts the office's stated risk. Three gaps that will become conflicts if unaddressed. The Decision Book treats the identifying threshold as one number; an interactive dashboard needs one per view, higher than a table's, and a procedure for setting it against the scene's knowability. The Decision Book's disclosure chapter protects tables and has no rule for a filter as a query system — fixed classifications, a cap on combinations, a limit on overlapping views, a log, the walk — so the prototype would ship a table builder protected as a table. And neither the Decision Book nor the design system has the held layer as a status with a decider, a resolution, a reason and a review date, so a held room would be drawn as empty or not drawn. **Logged for the design system:** a held-status component, distinguishable from withheld and unmeasured, with its reason reachable; a three-nothings pattern for one face; a safeguards-record panel; a withdrawal notice at the point of contribution; a stigma check in the colour rules (no red or dark for low; no large empty areas dominating). **Logged for the Decision Book:** a chapter on safeguards — the record's fourteen lines as rules; the floor per view; the query-time controls; the walk; lawful basis before the first row; the DPIA's consultation step and publication; consult, govern, agree; the held status; withdrawal tiers; the three-column harm assessment; the Online Safety Act decision; the site's own analytics; the twelve-month question — or an extension of Chapters 8 and 15 that carries them.
