### P-002 · Chapter 1 · L200 · Why an outlier can be the point · Prototype flag

Put the facts together and the outlier changes character. The textbook definition — an observation "which deviates so much from other observations as to arouse suspicions that it was generated by a different mechanism" — is, read carefully, an invitation: *a different mechanism* is a different story, and a story is what a reader came for. John Tukey's founding attitude for exploratory data analysis was that "the greatest value of a picture is when it forces us to notice what we never expected to see." The discipline of anomaly detection exists because "anomalies in data translate to significant (and often critical) actionable information" — in fraud, in intrusion, in machine failure. And there is a method that makes the favourable outlier its object of study: *positive deviance*, which "enables communities to discover the wisdom they already have, and then to act on it." In its founding case, in Vietnamese villages in 1990, the families whose children were well-nourished on the same resources as everyone else turned out to be adding sweet-potato greens and paddy crabs to meals, feeding children "three to four times a day, rather than the customary two," and washing hands first; the practitioners report that malnutrition fell by 85% in the pilot villages — their own figure, uncontrolled, and to be quoted as such. The analytical move is the one Carly describes in words: the small venue that holds a scene together is not noise in the distribution of venues; it is a different mechanism, and the question is what it is doing right. `[Prototype flag]` A positive-deviance analysis on the Vibe Map's own data is one of the clearest early experiments available.

### P-003 · Chapter 1 · L222 · The idea that makes several readers possible · Validate with users

A dashboard that must serve a council officer, a promoter, a funder and a scene at once is attempting to be a boundary object, and the theory says two things about that. First, it does not require everyone to agree what the numbers mean; it requires a structure each can read in their own way — which is why Chapter 12's insistence that a scene recognises its place is not sentiment but a condition of the object working. Second, from Esther Turnhout's 2009 study of ecological indicators: the property is not designed in. Their effectiveness as boundary objects, "rather than a characteristic of the indicator itself, … is a social achievement, which depends on a common culture of shared values and preferences." The shared display is downstream of the shared community. You cannot build the common ground with the dashboard; you build the dashboard on common ground that already exists — the strongest argument in this book for the project's insistence on convening and co-design before screens. `[Validate with users]`

### P-009 · Chapter 2 · L59 · 1 · There is no reader in general · Validate with users

Two things follow from the table before any evidence is read, and a third has to be admitted. The first is that a single display cannot *persuade* all of these readers, because they are moved by different things; the most it can aim for is to be *legible* to all of them, and even that is an aim rather than a demonstrated possibility, because — as sections 4 and 6 show — the choice of what to count is already made in somebody's grammar. The second is that most of the readers are not the decision's final owner. The officer writes for the minister; the programme officer writes for the board; the viability consultant writes for the lender; the trade body writes for the window. Section 6 shows how reliably that stripping happens. The admission is that this is a chapter about readers in which no reader was interviewed: the table is built from what the sources say each reader is trained to accept, and the behavioural evidence in section 5 comes from householders, parents, patients and survey panels rather than from officers and promoters. ### P-012 · Chapter 2 · L373 · Is recognition a test? · Prototype flag Validate with users

So the answer the sources support is precise. Recognition is a legitimate test of *credibility* — whether the account is trustworthy to the people it describes — and not of *accuracy* — whether the numbers are right, which is tested by the methods of Chapters 4 to 7. And it is a test only when the recognising can change the thing recognised. A scene shown a finished dashboard and asked "do you see yourself?" is being offered a nod. The brief's failure condition is therefore a design *sequence*, not a design *feature*: the check happens before the release, and the route from "no" to a change is part of the dashboard. `[Validate with users]` `[Prototype flag]`

### P-013 · Chapter 2 · L427 · 12 · What this means for a nightlife dashboard · Prototype flag

### P-023 · Chapter 3 · L272 · The default is advice · Validate with users

The anchoring is measurable. Isaac Cho and colleagues trained 81 people on a multi-view analytics tool starting from either the map or the calendar; the view they started in dominated the first fifth of their session and shaped their estimates, and a high or low number shown first anchored their final answers (52.7 against 21.1 for the same question). Across five studies and 726 people, André Calero Valdez and colleagues found that the chart you saw a moment ago shifts how you judge the next one — a small, reliable priming effect. And Cindy Xiong, Lisa van Weelden and Franconeri documented the designer's blind spot: people told what to see in a chart rated that pattern as more salient and assumed naive viewers would see it too — "The inability to separate one's own knowledge and expertise from that of their audience can make visual data communication more difficult and less clear than presenters realize." The person who chose the default cannot see the chart as a newcomer does. `[Validate with users]`

### P-024 · Chapter 3 · L330 · Literacy · Prototype flag

For England, the official numeracy figure is the OECD's 2023 adult skills survey (PIAAC): 21% of adults at Level 1 or below, people who "struggle to complete mathematical tasks with distracting information or carry out multi-step mathematical processes" — better than the OECD average of 25%, and improving since 2012. The famous "half of UK adults have the numeracy of a primary-school leaver" is a different measurement with a different threshold: the 2011 Skills for Life survey found "49.1% were Entry level 3 or below" (the attainment expected at ages 9–11). Both are true; they should not be presented as the same scale. And the Office for Statistics Regulation's own review of statistical literacy is the one a dashboard builder should sit with: it found numeracy surveys, and "No evidence was found that aimed to capture the multidimensional nature of statistical literacy"; it calls for "more up-to-date, large-scale surveys". Nothing it found measured the public's ability to read a chart or a table. `[Prototype flag]` — the Vibe Map could.

### P-052 · Chapter 6 · L256 · The claim ladder, tested · Prototype flag

It fares well on one count, partly on a second, and needs three amendments. It passes Mayne: a strong claim with its challenges drawn beside it is a contribution story in display form. It partly meets Hill's temporality: a ticket bought twelve days before a journey precedes the journey, and that sequence is a rung-one fact the data can establish — but the anchor claim needs the event to have preceded the *decision* to travel, and a timestamp cannot show that; someone who had already decided to visit the city and then bought a ticket produces the same record. The first amendment is Pearl's. "The event was the visit's anchor" is a counterfactual — the visit would not have happened without the event — and the ticket's timestamp is observational. The claim ladder, as drawn, has a rung-one fact at the top labelled as a rung-three claim. The top line that fits the evidence is the fact (the ticket preceded the trip, for this share of visits, with this base); the anchor claim sits below it, marked as the contribution story it is. The second amendment is the Additionality Guide's, and it is the one the claim ladder most lacks: a deadweight line — the share of those visitors who would have travelled anyway. The claim ladder, as drawn, names the alternatives it refuses (the hotel choice, the restaurant spend, the second night) but not the one that matters most, which is that the trip was coming regardless; until that line exists the claim ladder does not meet Hill's fundamental question. The only way to estimate the deadweight share is to ask, and an answer to "would you have come anyway?" is a stated preference about a counterfactual — the weakest evidence section 3 discussed, and it should be marked as such on the display rather than dressed as a measurement. The third amendment is Hernán's. The claim ladder calls its lower rungs "associational claims", and that word makes them vaguer rather than safer. Each lower rung should state the causal question it cannot answer and why. With those three changes the form stands, and it stands on method rather than on the studio's conviction, which is what the symmetry test was for. `[Prototype flag]` The deadweight question can be asked of ticket-holders in the prototype, with its answer marked as stated preference.

### P-054 · Chapter 6 · L310 · When a pattern becomes a canonical truth · Validate with users

That is a description of what "canonical truth" means in operation, and this book adopts it as a working definition until the closing chapter revisits it. A pattern becomes a truth by two tests, and the second is social. The first is the holdout's: it held on data that did not produce it. The second is Glover's: those judged competent agreed. The two are not the same test, and a dashboard needs both. Two further things are not tests but the record that lets the tests be run: the date and view in which the pattern was noticed (so that the holdout is one), and the model's assumptions written down with what it is importantly wrong about (Chapter 4's assumptions log). Who counts as competent is the open question. Chapter 5 found, through Sen, that the scene has first claim on what its own measure means, and this chapter takes the position — a Nightlife Culture position, argued from that and from KDD's placing of "the customer's viewpoint" at step one — that the people a pattern describes are among those judged competent to say whether it is true. The seven truths this chapter states have themselves passed only the second test, by the agreement of the few people who have read them; the first awaits the prototype. `[Validate with users]` Which of the scene's people count as "those judged competent" for the Vibe Map's patterns is a question for the three user groups, not for this chapter.

### P-055 · Chapter 6 · L376 · What shrinkage does to the exceptional place · Prototype flag

Chapter 4 saw the cost of this through Gelman and Price's work on disease maps, and this chapter states it as a method rather than a worry. Shrinkage pulls every small place toward the average of places like it. The place that is exceptional for a real reason — the one street in the county with a scene — is pulled too, and the model cannot tell a true exception from a lucky swing until it has more data. The display that fits is therefore both numbers: the direct figure with its interval, and the shrunk figure with its weight, so that the reader can see how much was borrowed and from whom. A dashboard that shows only the shrunk figure has hidden the small place's own voice behind its neighbours'; one that shows only the direct figure has shown Lac Qui Parle County's radon level on the strength of two houses. `[Prototype flag]` Whether the scene's own people can see their place in a shrunk figure — whether they recognise it, in the failure register's sense — is a thing to test and no literature can answer.

### P-056 · Chapter 6 · L392 · Two reasons for an asterisk · Validate with users

The two reasons call for two different things on a screen. ### P-070 · Chapter 7 · L144 · When places are not comparable at all · Validate with users

A last method for place against place refuses to force the comparison. In partial-order analysis, two places are compared on several indicators at once; if one exceeds the other on all, it dominates; if each exceeds the other on some, they are *incomparable*, and the analysis says so instead of weighting the indicators into a tie-break. Bruggemann and Carlsen: "Any decision support system mapping the set of indicators into one ranking index will lose this specific information" — the information being that "the appearance of incomparabilities, say x || y, indicate that there are conflicts in data" (read once). Carlsen and Bruggemann: "The evidence of conflicts is smashed out by aggregation methods to obtain a single quantity, which allows a ranking." Fattore's work on multidimensional deprivation, reached at its abstract, puts evaluation "in terms of multidimensional comparisons among achievement profiles, rather than through attribute score aggregations". † The method's own limit is stated by its advocates: "When the number of incomparabilities overwhelms comparabilities, the situation becomes uncomfortable from a partial order point of view" (read once). For a dashboard, incomparability is a display state: *these two places cannot be ordered on these measures*, which Chapter 5's price list showed is the truthful answer whenever the parts trade off. No usability research on whether lay readers can read "incomparable" was found. `[Further research]` `[Validate with users]`

### P-071 · Chapter 7 · L158 · 5 · The funnel: the comparison that is not a league table · Validate with users

The funnel resolves section 3's tension with the chart standard. A funnel's points can be sorted by anything — size, usually — and the sorting is visibly not the finding; the finding is distance from the limits. "Always rank bars by value" and "it is inappropriate to rank" are reconciled by a display in which order is a reading aid and position inside or outside the funnel is the claim. It also settles what counts as a band. The funnel's "alert" and "alarm", and the ONS's "higher", "lower" and "similar", are categories of statistical separation from an expectation: each says how far this place is from what would be expected of a place like it, and a place can move between them without any other place moving. A quintile is a category of position: it says where this place stands among the others, and a place moves between quintiles when the others move. The first is what this chapter means by comparison without a league table; the second is a league table in bands. Both, cropped, say one word about a place; only the first says something the place can act on alone. Whether a first-time reader reads a funnel that way is not settled by the literature. `[Validate with users]`

### P-072 · Chapter 7 · L342 · 11 · The studio's own comparisons, tested · Prototype flag

**"Default to the place-centred view: one place versus itself over time and versus peers it chose."** The first half is section 6 and 7's territory, and it is weaker than version 1 assumed. ### P-138 · Chapter 12 · L90 · Residents' neighbourhoods against the units · Validate with users

### P-141 · Chapter 12 · L188 · What form an identity profile takes · Prototype flag

Version 1 called the forms "profiles, fingerprints, small multiples" and left them Decorative. The evidence narrows them. On radar charts: in the one head-to-head on composite indicators, comparing radar, flower and circle charts as small multiples under a task taxonomy, "the Radar chart was the least effective and least liked", with "strong preference of participants toward the Flower chart" (Albo, Lanir, Bak & Rafaeli 2016 †; sample not visible). Feldman's objection is geometric and goes to the heart of this section: "Filled radar charts are not appropriate for such comparisons because the size of the area within the polygon is not invariant to the placement of the measures on the axes", so that "altering the placement of the measures on the axes can result in reversing the order of observations" (2013 †) — a filled radar *is* a ranking, by area, and the ranking depends on an arbitrary choice. ### P-168 · Chapter 14 · L340 · 8 · The deaf reader of a dashboard about sound · Prototype flag

What follows is three rules, one distinction and one observation. Any sound that is content — a recording, a sonified series, a narrated route — has a text document equivalent to it, at Level A, with no exemption for music; any sound that accompanies video is captioned, including non-speech sound; and no sound starts by itself for more than three seconds without a stop.
