# Chapter 5 — Comparing places and scenes without a league table

*Dashboard Design · Part 1: The display*
*Draft for steer · 17 August 2026*

---

## The decision

**How are places and scenes compared so that every place gains leverage — and none gains a ranking to lose?**

---

## The 60-second version

> Comparison is the instrument's political payload. The purpose, in the house's words: a
> **transferable metric** by which any place — Margate, Stroud, Swansea, Croydon — can *"pressure
> local policymakers into better supporting their area's unique sonic personality, through
> universally agreed data."* That is comparison as leverage for everyone. A league table is the
> opposite: leverage for the top ten and a demotion notice for the rest.
>
> The evidence on rankings is unusually one-sided. Ranked fields **change to fit the rank**:
> the mechanisms are documented — resources follow position, unmeasured qualities become
> invisible, institutions drift toward what scores. And ranks are largely **noise at small
> differences**: the standard audit method puts confidence intervals on rank, and in one flagship
> index a fifth of the ranked places' positions were artefacts of the modelling choices. The
> World Bank's *Doing Business* died of these diseases, and its post-mortem recommendation is
> this chapter's foundation: **keep the data, drop the ranking.**
>
> Two more things make the non-ranking comparison not just safer but *truer*. Scenes are defined
> by **differentiation** — what makes Croydon sound like Croydon — so half of any honest
> comparison is identity, which can be different but never better. And the felt layer's
> intensity metrics don't scale with population, so the hyperlocal view genuinely reorders
> things: **a borough with a scene beats a borough with a superclub.** The display's job is to
> make that reordering legible as the finding, not mistakable for an error.

---

## How the decision is made

### Step one — know what a ranking does to the ranked

Espeland and Sauder's study of law-school rankings is the standard account, and its mechanisms
transfer directly. **[Evaluated — documented field study; agent-fetched, superseded-phase
sweep.]** Rankings are *reactive*: resources and applicants follow position; a one-tier drop was
described inside a school as "devastating" though nothing real had changed; schools reverse-
engineered the formula and drifted toward what scored — **mission drift**, where the unmeasured
parts of the mission quietly stop being done. Campbell's law is the compressed version: the more
a quantitative indicator is used for decision-making, the more it distorts the process it
monitors.

Translate to this domain and the stakes are explicit in the house's own documents: the ambition
is *"not about smoothing out the scene, homogenising music experiences"* **[axiom — Vibe Setting
Framework]**. A national nightlife league table would be a homogenisation engine: every place
optimising toward whatever the index counts, which is precisely the monoculture the instrument
exists to resist. This is not a hypothetical — it is what rankings have done to every field that
adopted them.

**And the terminal case is recent.** *Doing Business* was discontinued in 2021; among its
structural defects, the CEPR post-mortem notes that under relative ranking *a country that
improves can still fall* — genuine improvement registering as failure — and recommends keeping
the underlying data while abandoning the rank. **[Evaluated; agent-fetched.]** That
recommendation is available to this instrument at design time rather than after the scandal.

### Step two — treat ranks as noise until proven otherwise

Where the field audits composite indices, it puts **confidence intervals on rank**: the Joint
Research Centre's audit protocol runs thousands of Monte Carlo perturbations and treats a rank
interval *narrower than ten positions* as reliable — and in its audit of a flagship global
index, dozens of economies carried intervals twenty to thirty positions wide. The EU's own
cultural cities monitor concedes that for roughly **one city in five, the rank reflects
modelling choices rather than data**. **[Measured; agent-fetched.]**

The display rule follows immediately: **a rank is never shown without its uncertainty, and at
neighbourhood scale it is usually not shown at all** — because at hyperlocal n, with emerging
data, the intervals will overlap almost everywhere, which means the honest display of rank is
no display of rank. What survives is position *within a distribution*: "where your place sits in
the spread" is defensible when "you are 47th of 331" is fiction.

### Step three — set the comparison unit where the scene lives

The house steer states the finding this chapter must make displayable: thinking of UK music,
the mind goes to Manchester, Bristol, Liverpool, Leeds — but even within London's 33 boroughs,
vibe measured hyperlocally *"picks up Croydon, Hackney, or Romford, over Farringdon (where
Fabric is) or Enfield (where Drumsheds is)."* **[House steer, 17 August 2026.]**

Unpack that and it contains three of this book's rules at once. **A venue is not a scene**
(Chapter 3): Fabric is world infrastructure hosted in Farringdon, not evidence of a Farringdon
scene — big infrastructure inflates its host geography exactly as the arena inflates the
aggregate. **The intensity metric reorders** (Chapter 4): belonging and participation per
head don't scale with population or with venue capacity, so the hyperlocal cut is where small,
strong scenes become visible. **And the unit is chosen, not inherited** (Chapter 3's boundary
rule): borough, ward, neighbourhood or scene-defined area are different instruments — the
comparison states its unit and what that unit hides.

The general rule: **comparison happens at the scale where the scene lives, and the display says
which scale that is.** City-level comparison flatters infrastructure; hyperlocal comparison
finds culture. Both are legitimate; silently conflating them is how Enfield "beats" Romford.

### Step four — split the comparison: identity is never ranked, conditions always gated

Scenes are defined by differentiation — what a place sounds like that nowhere else does. That
means an honest comparison has **two different objects in it**, and the display must never let
them merge:

**Identity axes — different, never better.** Genre mix, sonic personality, the shape of a
scene's week, who its audiences are. These are *nominal*: Croydon is not more or less than
Hackney on them, it is *else*. Their display forms are profiles, fingerprints, small multiples —
side-by-side portraits inviting recognition, not ordering. This is where "what does your city
sound like?" lives, and ranking it would be a category error the instrument's whole philosophy
forbids: *all vibes are valid* **[axiom]**.

**Condition axes — better or worse, and policy-actionable.** Space, support, funding, licensing
friction, affordability, security of tenure. These are *ordinal* and comparing them is the
point, because they are what policymakers control. Here the style guide's rule is already
absolute and this chapter inherits it as the gate: *"Every chart comparing two or more places
must state whether the indicator comparability gate has been cleared… A comparison without this
note is not publishable."* **[Axiom — §12, backed by Comparative Methodology v1.0's
eight-criterion gate.]**

**The advocacy sentence is the two halves joined.** *"Croydon's scene runs at this intensity
with this support; scenes of similar intensity elsewhere get more."* Identity establishes that
there is something real; conditions establish that it is under-supported; the comparison
delivers the ask. That sentence — not a rank — is what pressures a policymaker, because it
cannot be answered with "so climb the table."

### Step five — default to the place-centred view

Chapter 1's rule applied to comparison: the default view is the product, and the default
comparison is **one place versus itself over time and versus peers it chose** — never the
all-places table.

The precedents are already in the audit and the evidence. The Office for National Statistics'
Explore Local Statistics service compares an authority to a **cluster of statistically similar
authorities** and shows values within the national spread — comparison as context, not as
position. The Better Life Index, published by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and
Development (the OECD — the Paris-based club of 38 mostly-rich countries whose statistics
underpin much international comparison), lets the reader set the weights, and the finding from a
decade of it is that the reweighting became a *collection channel* — tens of thousands of shared
weightings telling the organisation what people value. **[Both agent-fetched.]** For this instrument, peer choice does the same double duty:
a place choosing its comparators (similar size, similar scene-stage, aspirational twin) makes
the comparison fair *and* reveals who each place believes its peers are — which is itself scene
knowledge nobody currently collects.

Partial-order thinking supplies the honest structure underneath: with multiple dimensions and no
imposed weights, some places simply **are not comparable** — A is ahead on some conditions,
behind on others, and the truthful display says so rather than forcing a tiebreak.
**[Evaluated — the poset literature; agent-fetched.]** "Genuinely incomparable" rendered
honestly is not a weakness of the instrument; it is the instrument refusing to invent a
tiebreak the data cannot support.

### Step six — design against the screenshot

A display that *can* be cropped into a league table *will* be — by a journalist, a press
office, or a hostile council. The Decision Book's breakout-statistic rule (the most-screenshotted
element travels furthest, so everything it needs must be attached to it) applies here with a
comparison-specific edge: **no view of this instrument should survive cropping as a ranking.**
If a top-to-bottom ordered list of places exists on any screen, the crop exists; therefore
ordered all-places lists do not exist on any screen. Distributions with places marked,
peer-groups, profiles and dominance statements resist the crop structurally — there is no
table to screenshot.

## Worked example

*Illustrative — one borough's comparison view; fixes nothing about the design.*

**The place: Croydon.** The default comparison screen answers Chapter 1's question in Croydon's
voice: *"What does Croydon sound like — and is it backed?"*

**Panel one — identity, unranked.** Croydon's sonic fingerprint beside Hackney's and Romford's:
genre mix, scene-week shape, self-placed belonging marks from the Chapter 4 instrument. Three
portraits, side by side, deliberately unorderable. The reading invited is recognition — *that's
us* — and difference, not defeat.

**Panel two — conditions, gated.** Space per scene-participant, support per head, tenure
security, licensing friction — Croydon against its **chosen peer group** (similar scene
intensity, similar scale) and against its own five-year trail. Comparability gate stated on the
face: which criteria cleared, which required harmonisation. Where Croydon and a peer are
genuinely incomparable — ahead on space, behind on funding — the display says exactly that.

**Panel three — the sentence.** *"Croydon's scene runs at Hackney-level intensity on
Enfield-level support."* Ten words, the whole ask, and — deliberately — nothing on the screen
for a sub-editor to crop into "London's best and worst boroughs for nightlife."

**The Farringdon check.** Farringdon's own page renders honestly under the same rules:
world-class infrastructure, thin residential scene — a *different kind of place*, stated as
such, not a winner or a loser. The unit note says what borough-scale hides.

**The five-second test:** a Croydon scene member says "that's us, and we're under-backed." A
policymaker reads an actionable gap, not an insult. A journalist finds no table.

## Pressure points

**Where there is real latitude.**

*Peer-group design.* Statistical similarity, scene-stage similarity, aspirational twins, or
let-the-place-choose — all defensible, each with different politics. The chapter requires only
that the scheme is named.

*Self-comparison as the spine.* "Croydon versus Croydon, 2021–2026" is the least gameable, most
policy-legible comparison there is, and no reactivity evidence tells against it.

*The identity portraits.* Sonic fingerprints are an open design space no incumbent occupies —
and the one place in this chapter where "make it look like art" has no evidential cost at all.

**Where it looks rigorous and fails.**

*The top-ten listicle.* Even as a "celebration." It is the league table wearing a party hat, and
every reactivity mechanism applies.

*The blended city score with a rank.* Chapters 2 and 4's forbidden composite, now with
Espeland-and-Sauder consequences attached.

*Ranking on ungated indicators.* A comparison across places whose data fails the comparability
gate isn't a bold estimate, it's not publishable — the style guide already says so.

*The medal-colour map.* Gold-silver-bronze choropleth of boroughs: the moral gradient (Ch 2),
acreage bias (Ch 3), and the crop-ready ranking, all in one artefact.

*"Above/below national average" as the only frame.* It quietly re-imposes a single axis and
makes half of all places losers by construction. The spread display exists to avoid exactly
this.

**What proves a break works.** If some ordered view is genuinely needed — say, for internal
prioritisation of support — it lives behind the professional's login, carries rank intervals,
and is tested with the crop: screenshot any region of the screen and check that what travels
carries its uncertainty and its gate with it. Then run Chapter 4's represented-not-used test at
place scale: show a low-intensity place its own page. If the page reads as *here is what's real
and what would help*, the comparison is doing advocacy for everyone. If it reads as *you lost*,
the league table got in through a side door.

## Checklist

- [ ] No ordered all-places list on any screen
- [ ] Any unavoidable rank carries its interval; none at hyperlocal scale
- [ ] Comparison unit stated, with what it hides
- [ ] Identity and condition axes visually and structurally separate
- [ ] Identity never ordered; "all vibes are valid" holds on screen
- [ ] Comparability gate on the face of every cross-place condition chart
- [ ] Default view: self-over-time plus chosen peers
- [ ] Peer-group scheme named
- [ ] Genuine incomparability displayed as such
- [ ] The advocacy sentence present, ten words or fewer
- [ ] Crop test run on every comparison view
- [ ] The losers' test: a low-intensity place's page read by its own scene
