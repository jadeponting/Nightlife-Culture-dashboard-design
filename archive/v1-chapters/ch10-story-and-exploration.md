# Chapter 10 — Story and exploration

*Dashboard Design · Part 2: The interaction*
*Draft for steer · 17 August 2026*

---

## The decision

**How are authored narrative and reader-driven exploration sequenced in one instrument — and where does the story actually live?**

---

## The 60-second version

> The field has a beautiful vocabulary and no evidence. The canonical taxonomy of narrative
> visualisation — seven genres, and the three named patterns: the **martini glass** (authored
> stem, then open exploration), the **interactive slideshow**, the **drill-down story** — comes
> from a study of 58 professional pieces that contains **no user research at all**, and its
> authors said so. Scrollytelling, the era's default form, is **essentially unevaluated** as a
> comprehension technique. These are menus, not findings; a genre is chosen as a bet and named
> as one.
>
> What *is* known reshapes the question. Story built as a gate **suppresses** exploration (the
> Chapter 9 null). Authored defaults are already rhetoric — *"default views provide an initial
> point of interpretation"* — so the instrument is telling a story before any narrative layer
> is added. The strongest institutional precedent chose **writing**: the Office for National
> Statistics, with every interactive tool available, told its census results as 331
> semi-automated *written* local stories, because *"we need to pull out the narrative and the
> story to help guide our users through the numbers."* And the best interactive-story finding
> in the sector's own family is that a decade of reader reweighting became a **collection
> channel** — the story readers told back.
>
> So the resolution: **the story is the authored spine this book already built** — the default
> sentence, the portrait, the bridging line — generated *per place*, in writing, with
> exploration standing beside it, skippable in both directions. Not story *then* explore.
> Story *beside* explore, and the story is five words where it can be.

---

## How the decision is made

### Step one — treat the genre taxonomy as a menu, and choose bets knowingly

Segel and Heer's design space — seven genres, the author-driven/reader-driven axis, the three
sequencing patterns — is the field's shared vocabulary and carries **no evaluation**; the
authors flagged the absence of reader-centric research themselves. **[Evaluated as taxonomy;
unmeasured as prescription.]** The martini glass is the structurally obvious bet for an
evidence instrument (authored claim, then exploration), and this book takes it *as a bet*,
recorded as one — with one amendment the evidence forces (step two).

Scrollytelling's status is worse than unproven: the raw research found it **essentially
unevaluated for comprehension** — one adjacent study in another domain found engagement gains
with comprehension merely equal. **[Absence, verified in the sweep.]** It is not banned; it is
priced: a scrollytold piece is an *editorial* artefact the studio might publish *from* the
instrument, not the instrument's own spine.

### Step two — never build the story as a gate

The Chapter 9 null bites sequencing directly: narrative introductions ahead of exploration
halved exploration time, and only a third of readers followed the authored order. Bret
Victor's design principle — the oldest in the explorable-explanations tradition — said it
first: **interactivity, and narrative, remain optional.** The reader who wants the answer gets
it at once (Chapter 1's default sentence *is* the story's first line); the reader who wants to
wander wanders without toll.

So the martini glass is amended to the house shape: **not a stem you must drink through, but a
poster beside an open door.** The authored layer is short, front-loaded and complete in
itself; the exploratory layer is reachable from the first screen; each links into the other at
every step (a finding in exploration links to its written line; every written line links to
the view that shows it — which Chapter 9's stable state addresses make possible).

### Step three — the defaults are already the narrative; author them as one

Hullman and Diakopoulos named it: sequence, selection and default views are procedural
rhetoric — the story is being told by what is shown first, compared by default and omitted.
**[Evaluated.]** This book has already authored those choices deliberately (Chapters 1, 2, 5,
6). This chapter adds only the honesty rule: **the instrument's narrative positions are
stated, not embedded.** The two-columns frame, the value-first ordering, the intensity
denominators — a "how this instrument argues" page says what the design asserts and why, in
the same register as the methodology. The alternative — letting the rhetoric sit unacknowledged
in the defaults — fails the hostile-reader test the moment a sceptical analyst notices it, and
they will.

### Step four — the written story scales; the house already knows how

The strongest precedent is the one from the Chapter 1 evidence, now given its full weight: the
Office for National Statistics faced the same problem at national scale — every place wanting
its own story — and solved it with **semi-automated written narrative**: 331 local stories,
generated from data through authored templates, because prose *"pull[s] out the narrative…
to help guide our users through the numbers."* **[Practice, institutional.]**

This is the house pattern waiting to be claimed. The Croydon sentence of Chapter 5 — *"scene
at Hackney-level intensity on Enfield-level support"* — is a **template with authored voice
and per-place values**: written story, generated for every place, in the studio's register,
governed by §03/§04 and the five-word discipline where headlines allow. The felt layer's
testimony (Chapter 4) supplies the human line no template can, with its consent architecture
already built. Story, at scale, without a scrolly in sight — and every generated sentence
carries Chapter 6's discipline, because a generated claim is still a claim.

### Step five — the reader's story flows back

The Better Life Index's decade-long lesson: letting readers reweight became *"an important
back channel"* — tens of thousands of shared configurations telling the publisher what people
value. **[Practice, sustained.]** Chapters 4 and 7 built this instrument's versions: the
placed mark, the guess, the chosen peers, the taste profile. This chapter names the loop as
narrative: **the instrument's story includes what its readers told it back** — published in
aggregate, consented, as its own layer ("what ten thousand of you said the night is worth").
The story is not only told *to* the city; it accumulates *from* it. That closes the loop with
the Vibe Setting Framework's own principle — visible, evidenced listening — at product scale.

## Worked example

*Illustrative — one place's story, assembled from parts this book already specified.*

**The authored spine, generated:** *"Croydon's night runs at Hackney-level intensity on
Enfield-level support."* Beneath it, three templated paragraphs in house voice — identity (the
sound, from the portrait), conditions (the gap, gated), trajectory (the series) — each
sentence linking to the exact view that shows it. One consented testimony line, attributed,
with its "what this changed."

**The open door, beside it:** the portrait, the peers, the filter walk — reachable without
scrolling through a word of the story. Every explored view links back: *read this as a
sentence.*

**The story told back, beneath:** *"2,140 Croydon marks placed this year — belonging spread
shown; the public's guess at the night's worth ran 40% under the measured figure."* Aggregate,
consented, its own provenance chip.

**The test:** a reader who reads only the story knows the finding. A reader who only explores
reaches the same finding. Neither was forced through the other. Both can cite exactly what
they saw.

## Pressure points

**Where there is real latitude.**

*The editorial layer above the instrument.* Scrollytold essays, films, posters — published
*from* the instrument's states, carrying its provenance, at the studio's full creative
register. The instrument is the source; the storytelling around it is free and unlimited.

*Voice in the templates.* Generated prose does not have to sound generated — the register
rules and the contested-terms table are precisely the guardrails that let templated sentences
sound like the house. This is Chapter 13's ground, opened here.

*The annual story.* "What the city's night said this year" — the reader-contribution layer as
a yearly publication — is a retention beat, an advocacy artefact and a dataset release in one.

**Where it looks sophisticated and fails.**

*The scroll toll.* Any design where reaching the number requires consuming the narrative. The
null, built deliberately.

*The unacknowledged frame.* Rhetoric in the defaults with no "how this argues" page — rigour
theatre that one sceptical reading collapses.

*Generated overreach.* Templates that assert beyond their data slots ("thriving", "booming")
— §04's contested-terms discipline applies to machines too.

*The stakeholder-demo genre choice.* Picking scrollytelling because funders find it modern —
the exact reflex the Decision Book's dashboard chapter was written against, one genre over.

**What proves a break works.** Give one reader the story only and one the exploration only,
and ask both what the place's night is like. If their answers agree, the two layers are one
instrument. If they diverge, the rhetoric and the evidence have come apart — and the "how this
argues" page is where to look for the crack.

## Checklist

- [ ] Genre bets recorded as bets
- [ ] Answer and exploration both reachable from screen one; no narrative gate
- [ ] Every story line links to its view; every view links to its line
- [ ] "How this instrument argues" page drafted and maintained
- [ ] Per-place templates in house register; contested-terms pass run on template text
- [ ] Generated claims carry recipe, base and boundary
- [ ] Testimony present under consent architecture, with return visible
- [ ] Reader-contribution layer published in aggregate, consented, chipped
- [ ] Scrollytelling confined to editorial outputs, priced as unevaluated
- [ ] The two-readers test run
