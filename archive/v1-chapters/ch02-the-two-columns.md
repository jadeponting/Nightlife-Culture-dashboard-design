# Chapter 2 — The two columns on one screen

*Dashboard Design · Part 0: The glance*
*Draft for steer · 16 August 2026*

---

## The decision

**How do measured-harm data and cultural-value data share a display without the harm frame winning?**

---

## The 60-second version

> The deck's slides 7 and 8 are a display problem nobody has solved on screen. One column is what
> systems count: risk, incidents, licensing, nuisance, footfall, spend. The other is what culture
> holds: belonging, trust, scene-building, creative risk, care, talent incubation, collective joy.
> The first column arrives with years of administrative data behind it. The second is the reason
> the instrument exists.
>
> The framing evidence says the obvious layout is the wrong one. Audiences are *"invulnerable to
> facts that do not fit within a narrative that they can understand and embrace"*, and repeating
> the opposing frame — even to refute it — strengthens it. A screen that opens on the harm column
> and then answers it has already conceded the frame. **Lead with the value column**, which is
> also the house's own operating principle: lead with abundance.
>
> The harder problem is evidential asymmetry. The harm data will *look* more real, because
> robustness reads as truth. The resolution is not to dress the value column up — *"strong design
> must never make weak evidence look stronger than it is"* — nor to visually diminish it. It is
> **equal visual dignity with honest provenance**: both columns rendered with the same craft, each
> carrying its evidence tier in text, and every gap in the value column rendered as a visible gap.
> Because in this instrument, **the gaps are the argument.**

---

## How the decision is made

### Step one — decide which column speaks first, and know what the order asserts

The Decision Book's Chapter 16 carries the framing evidence, from the Othering & Belonging
Institute: many audiences are *"invulnerable to facts that do not fit within a narrative that they
can understand and embrace"*, the same fact resolves differently depending on which story is
active, and — the operative rule — **never repeat the opposing frame in order to deny it**,
because repetition strengthens it. **[Evaluated — framing research synthesis, read directly.]**

Translated to a screen: a display that opens on incidents-licensing-nuisance and then presents
cultural value as the rebuttal has structured itself as a reply. The reader's active story remains
"the night is a risk to be managed," and the value column becomes special pleading against it.

The house already has the answer as an operating principle — *lead with abundance, begin with the
value already being created* — and Chapter 1's rules give it teeth: the default view's sentence is
written first, and here that sentence belongs to the value column. The harm column is context the
reader already holds; the deck says so — it is *what systems already count*. Nobody arrives at
this instrument ignorant of the harm frame. They arrive knowing nothing else.

**This does not mean hiding the harm column.** Omitting it reads as advocacy and fails the
hostile-reader test in one glance. It means the value column owns the headline and the harm
column is present, honest and second — the shared ground, not the story.

### Step two — give unequal evidence equal dignity

The two columns are not evidential equals and will not be for years. The counted column rests on
administrative series; the held column rests on emerging measures, qualitative work and, in
places, nothing yet. Two symmetric traps follow.

The first is inflating the value column — the trap the style guide already bars: *"Strong design
must never make weak evidence look stronger than it is."* **[Axiom.]**

The second is subtler and undocumented in the guide: **robustness reads as reality.** A column of
crisp administrative numbers next to a column of ranges, estimates and gaps will be read as the
real one, whatever the layout intends — not because anyone decided it, but because precision is
received as a property of the *thing* rather than of the *measurement*. The Decision Book's
Chapter 6 names the mechanism from the other side: precision styling on imprecise data *"is a
claim the data cannot support"* — and its converse operates here: honest imprecision on the
value column, next to false-precision neighbours, punishes honesty.

The resolution is a two-part rule. **Equal visual dignity**: both columns are set with the same
craft, the same type scale, the same chart quality — the value column is never the sketchy one.
**Honest provenance, carried in text**: every figure in both columns wears its provenance chip —
the style guide's existing furniture, tier stated as a text code, colour never the sole carrier —
so the evidential difference is *stated* rather than *performed*. The difference between the
columns becomes something the instrument says, not something it looks like.

And the harm column's own weaknesses are stated with the same candour. Administrative robustness
is partly costume — a point the instrument can make *only* if its own standard is visibly
even-handed, which is the entire credibility architecture of this project.

### Step three — check the direction of every encoding

The Urban Institute's *Do No Harm* guidance — practitioner consensus, derived from interviews
rather than experiment **[evaluated]** — supplies the checks that bite here: which group or frame
is treated as the reference category; how things are ordered (deliberately, not by inherited
default); and whether a deficit framing has been designed into the encoding itself.

The Decision Book's Chapter 7 worked example shows the move in practice: an outcome colour ramp
running *"neutral-to-positive rather than negative-to-positive, because a deficit framing would
encode 'these young people were problems that were fixed.'"* **[Read directly.]**

For the two columns, the checks become specific:

- **The harm column is not the reference category.** The value column is not displayed as
  deviation from a norm of risk.
- **No red-to-green moral gradient across the columns.** The style guide already encodes ordinal
  strength as filled steps in ink precisely to avoid a judgement ramp; the same discipline holds
  here. The harm column is not "the red side."
- **Order within each column is chosen** — by magnitude, by evidential strength, by the argument —
  and stated, never alphabetical by accident.

### Step four — render the missed as measured absence, not blank space

The value column will have holes for years. How those holes are displayed is the chapter's most
consequential craft decision, because the empirical evidence says readers do not notice missing
data on their own: in the CALVI study of misleading-chart literacy, **missing data and missing
normalisation were the hardest defects for readers to detect** — baseline correctness 0.39 on
trick items. **[Measured.]** A silent gap in the value column will simply read as *less value*.

The Decision Book's rule is the foundation — *a gap in the data is a finding*, shown as hatched
fill or a visible break, never silently omitted **[axiom, carried into Style Guide §12]** — and
its Chapter 7 supplies the escalation this instrument needs: **deliberate incompleteness** as a
named creative technique — *"showing the gaps, the unmeasured, the refused"* — which *"requires
confidence from the commissioner."*

Here the commissioner is the instrument's own thesis. *Culturally powerful but institutionally
under-evidenced* is the claim; the value column's rendered gaps are that claim made visible.

And the value column's contents are not a designer's invention — **the scene has already named
them**. The *Seeing the System* workshop (Vibe Setting Framework, unpublished — confidential)
was explicit that a useful map *"cannot only show buildings, venues, jobs and economic outputs"*
and must make the case for what *"usually goes unnoticed, undocumented or undervalued: the
'aunty networks', the chats, the favours, the care, the genre knowledge, the joy, the wellbeing,
the feeling of being in a music space."* That list is the value column, authored by the people it
describes — which means its rendered gaps are not a display convention. They are a commitment
the community has already asked for. A
row reading **belonging — not yet measured anywhere** is not a weakness of the display. On this
screen, it is the finding. The design rule: every gap in the value column is rendered at full
size, labelled with *why* it is empty (never measured · suppressed · not yet collected), so the
column's shape shows both what culture holds and how little of it anyone has counted.

### Step five — never let the columns share a scale

The two columns count different things in different units — incidents per year against, say, a
belonging measure that does not yet exist. Any shared axis, common index or single blended score
on screen asserts a commensurability nobody has established, and quietly re-runs the composite-
index problem at the level of pixels: merging the columns into one number is aggregation, with
every exchange-rate commitment that implies, made silently by a chart.

So: **juxtaposition, never superimposition.** The columns sit side by side, each on its own
scale, each internally honest, with the relationship between them carried where the evidence says
meaning is actually carried — in the words. Titles and text dominate what readers take away
**[measured — Borkin et al., Chapter 1]**, so the bridging sentence does the work the shared axis
must not: *"[Place] recorded n incidents last year. It also holds m venues, k crews, and a scene
nobody has counted."* Ten words or less if it can; five if the poster rule can be met.

## Worked example

*Illustrative — applying the chapter to one ward-level screen; fixes nothing about the design.*

**The screen.** One place, one view: the two columns for a single ward.

**The bridging sentence, written first** (Chapter 1's rule): *"What gets counted, and what the
night actually holds."* Seven words, and it is the deck's own argument compressed.

**Left — what culture holds, speaking first.** Venues, events, crews, free-entry nights, years of
continuous operation — each with its provenance chip (`ADM-OPEN`, `SECTOR`, `PRI-DOCUMENTED`).
Then the rendered gaps, full-size, labelled: *belonging — not yet measured anywhere* ·
*talent progression — not yet collected* · *care practices — documented, not counted*. The
column's shape says: rich, and largely uncounted — which is the thesis, on screen.

**Right — what systems count, second.** Incidents, licensing actions, noise complaints — same
craft, same scale discipline, own units, each with its own honesty label: *incident data —
recorded where policing is deployed; deployment is not uniform*. The harm column's context
stated as plainly as the value column's gaps.

**No shared axis anywhere.** The eye compares shapes; the sentence carries the relationship;
neither column is red.

**The five-second test** (Chapter 1): shown the screen cold, the intended reader should say
something like *"there's more happening here than gets counted."* If they say *"this area has a
lot of incidents"*, the harm frame won and the layout has failed regardless of its craft.

## Pressure points

**Where there is real latitude.**

*How the gaps are drawn.* Hatched fills, ghosted containers, empty vessels at full size —
genuinely open craft territory, and the place where this instrument's visual language can be
most its own. The constraint is only that absence must be unmistakable as *measured absence*.

*The bridging sentence's voice.* This is where the studio's register lives — culturally literate,
plainspoken, no consultancy sludge. Free within §03.

*Whether the columns are literal columns.* Side-by-side is the logical relationship, not
necessarily the layout. Any composition that keeps the scales separate and value first is open.

**Where it looks sophisticated and fails.**

*The reconciliation chart.* A single clever graphic merging both columns into one visual field —
a dual-axis chart, a blended score, a "night health index" dial. Reads as synthesis; is silent
aggregation.

*The red column.* Harm in warm alarm colours, culture in cool virtue colours. A moral gradient
the style guide already bars, and it hands the harm column the drama.

*Advocacy by omission.* Dropping the harm column, or shrinking it. One glance from a licensing
officer and the instrument is filed under campaigning.

*Dressing the gaps.* Making the value column's holes decorative — soft illustrations where data
should be — converts the strongest claim on the screen into wallpaper.

**What proves a break works.** Run the five-second test on someone who holds the harm frame
professionally. If they can state the screen's claim accurately and without hostility — the
Decision Book's Chapter 16 test, applied to a display — the two columns are doing their work.

## Checklist

- [ ] The bridging sentence is written first, ten words or fewer
- [ ] The value column speaks first; the harm column is present, honest, second
- [ ] Both columns set with equal craft; neither is the sketchy one
- [ ] Every figure carries its provenance chip; tier in text, never colour alone
- [ ] The harm column's limitations stated as candidly as the value column's gaps
- [ ] Every value-column gap rendered full-size, labelled with why it is empty
- [ ] No shared axis, no blended score, no reconciliation graphic
- [ ] No moral colour gradient across the columns
- [ ] Order within each column chosen and stated
- [ ] Five-second test run on a holder of the harm frame

## Style Guide v1.4 — conflict log

**Agrees.** §06 evidence standard is this chapter's spine. §12's gap-rendering and provenance
rules extend cleanly from charts to columns. "Lead with abundance" resolves step one before
research was needed.

**Conflicts.** None found.

**Silent on.** Cross-panel colour politics (the "red side" problem); evidential-asymmetry display;
absence as a first-class display element at layout scale rather than within a chart.
