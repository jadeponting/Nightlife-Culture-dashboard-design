## Before you start

This is the fifth chapter of a research book written for Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio led by Carly Heath, about how dashboards work and why. The book exists to support the Vibe Map — a planned public, non-commercial, interactive account of the people, activity, value and support systems that sustain a place's nightlife culture, built first for the West of England. You do not need to have read Chapters 1 to 4 to read this one. Four things from them recur: the working definition — *a dashboard is a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader*; Chapter 1's finding that the most reliable thing a published measure does is change the behaviour it measures; Chapter 2's finding that there is no reader in general; and Chapter 4's rule that a number is undefined until its unit, period and base are stated. Where this chapter refers to Carly, it means the studio's founder; "the brief" is her written brief for this book; where it refers to the Vibe Map, it is illustrating, not specifying. The studio's own rulebook for design decisions is called the Decision Book, and its visual rules live in a design system (Style Guide v1.5). One decision this chapter does not make and could not: whether the studio's measure of nightlife culture should be an index, a profile, a scoreboard, a ledger or something else. Version 1 of this book recorded that the studio has kept that question open on purpose; this chapter says what each form does and what each must show, and leaves the choice where it belongs.

Two conventions, explained fully in Chapter 0. Every rule in this book carries a **kind** — *Standard* (an external rule nobody here wrote), *Evidence* (a named study), *Reading* (our interpretation, open to challenge), *Practice* (a practitioner's framework or craft knowledge, not tested as research) or *Nightlife Culture* (a decision or finding of the studio's own) — and a **tier**: *Fixed* is a hard line with a right answer; *Adaptable* is a stated default you may argue with and must log if you leave; *Decorative* is free. "Fixed as a stance" marks a rule the studio adopts and will not reopen on a given display though its source is a reading or a practice; "Fixed as a procedure" fixes the steps and not the parameter. Flags in square brackets mark loose ends: `[Further research]`, `[Prototype flag]`, `[Validate with users]`, `[Revisit → Ch n]`, `[Revisit at end]`, `[Rich reference]` and `[Counterbalance → Ch n]`. A dagger (†) after a source means it was read at the authors' published abstract only; "secondary" means a quotation reached this chapter through another named document.

Three words are used in a fixed sense. A *construct* is the thing you want to measure and cannot see directly — deprivation, innovation, human development, the quality of a night out. An *indicator* is something you can count that stands for part of it. A *composite indicator*, or *index*, is what you get when several indicators are combined into one number. Most of this chapter is about the distance between the first and the third, and about what has to travel with the third for a reader to see that distance.

## The question

**When several things are counted and one number is wanted, what is actually being built — and what has to be shown with that number for it to be a measure rather than an opinion with decimal places?**

---

## The 60-second version

> A composite index is "formed when individual indicators are compiled into a single index on the basis of an underlying model", and the handbook most indices cite says their construction "owes more to the craftsmanship of the modeller than to universally accepted scientific rules". Its own list of what can go wrong is the best argument for showing how one was made.
>
> Four things are decided when indicators become an index, and each is a claim. **Normalisation** puts them on one scale, and the scale chosen quietly sets the exchange rate between them. **Weights** are "essentially value judgements"; equal weights are the commonest choice and "does not mean 'no weights'". And weights are not importance: in the Times university ranking, an indicator given 20% of the weight explained 21% of the variance in the result while one given 40% explained 81%, and when the parts all move together "the weights are little more than cosmetic". **Aggregation** decides whether a strength can pay for a weakness: under addition, a place scoring (21, 1, 1, 1) equals a place scoring (6, 6, 6, 6). The Human Development Index moved to a geometric mean in 2010 to stop that; the UK's deprivation index transforms its domains "to reduce 'cancellation effects'"; a prosperity index uses means that "punish weakness" because its parts "are poor substitutes for one another". Any aggregation is a hidden price list: even after its 2010 reform the HDI valued a year of life in Zimbabwe at $0.51 and in the richest countries at about $9,000. **Robustness** is whether the ranking survives other defensible choices: audited properly, an index "is no longer a magic number", and a rank comes with an interval — in the 2025 Global Innovation Index, 85 of 139 economies had a 90% rank interval narrower than ten places and seven had one wider than twenty.
>
> A measure that is used changes what it measures. Goodhart's "law" was a joke in a subordinate clause; the sentence everyone quotes is Marilyn Strathern's; Campbell's is the one with the evidence. In the English targets regime, a third of ambulance trusts "corrected" response times to fall under eight minutes, and nine NHS trusts adjusted 6,000 waiting-list records — and waiting times really did fall, faster than in Scotland and Wales, and the comparative studies (read at abstract) found less gaming than the stories suggest. Both are true. Three of the four ways a proxy fails need no cheat at all.
>
> Validity is "the degree to which evidence and theory support the interpretations of test scores for proposed uses", and it is unitary: the number is not valid, a reading of it is, for a use. It includes what the measure does once used. "Recognition" — the people measured seeing themselves in it — is, in the measurement tradition, face validity and credibility: it corrects facts, confirms resonance, and is not evidence that the construct exists; the one study found that tested community-chosen indicators against field science found most of them valid and not all of them reliable. The official precedent for measuring a felt quality is four questions the ONS refuses to combine, "because they are all individually important".
>
> The cultural-value literature mostly declines a single score and says why: "instead of 'measuring' it is more appropriate to speak of 'evaluation', of which measurement is but one component". The leading UK quality instrument reports twelve dimensions from three perspectives and is "not a clapometer". The newest official guidance recommends "that composite indices should be accompanied by dashboards … that give information about the development in the underlying components". The one composite in the studio's own sector publishes its parts and not its recipe, so a reader cannot reproduce its headline from its published parts — and three of the five place indices with published methods publish no sensitivity analysis either.
>
> So the canonical truths: a composite is a model and is shown as one; weights are judgements and are not importance; aggregation is a price list and the prices are declared; a rank carries its interval; a measure that is used changes what it measures, so the audit is designed with the measure; validity is of a reading for a use, including its consequences; and recognition is necessary and is not validity. The recipe goes on the face.

## 1 · What a measure is

A dashboard that shows a "score" has done something a dashboard that shows a count has not. It has taken several things that were measured and decided, on the reader's behalf, how they combine. Chapter 4 asked what a count must carry to be evidence. This chapter asks the same of a combination, and the answer is longer, because every step from indicators to index is a decision that could have gone another way.

The governing text is the *Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators*, published jointly by the OECD and the European Commission's Joint Research Centre in 2008 and still the method most indices cite. Its definition: "A composite indicator is formed when individual indicators are compiled into a single index on the basis of an underlying model." Its candour about what that involves is unusual for a handbook: "Composite indicators are much like mathematical or computational models. As such, their construction owes more to the craftsmanship of the modeller than to universally accepted scientific rules for encoding." And its warning: "composite indicators can send misleading policy messages if they are poorly constructed or misinterpreted. Their 'big picture' results may invite users (especially policy-makers) to draw simplistic analytical or policy conclusions." `[Rich reference]`

The handbook lists what a composite is for and what it risks; six of its nine pros and all six of its cons are reproduced here, because the second list is the chapter's case. Pros: composites "Can summarise complex, multi-dimensional realities with a view to supporting decision-makers"; "Are easier to interpret than a battery of many separate indicators"; "Can assess progress of countries over time"; "Reduce the visible size of a set of indicators without dropping the underlying information base"; "Facilitate communication with general public (i.e. citizens, media, etc.) and promote accountability"; "Help to construct/underpin narratives for lay and literate audiences". Cons: they "May send misleading policy messages if poorly constructed or misinterpreted"; "May invite simplistic policy conclusions"; "May be misused, e.g. to support a desired policy, if the construction process is not transparent and/or lacks sound statistical or conceptual principles"; "The selection of indicators and weights could be the subject of political dispute"; "May disguise serious failings in some dimensions and increase the difficulty of identifying proper remedial action, if the construction process is not transparent"; "May lead to inappropriate policies if dimensions of performance that are difficult to measure are ignored." Three of the six cons end with the same condition — *if the construction process is not transparent*. The handbook's own remedy for its own list is the recipe on the face.

The cultural-value literature arrives at a different starting point and the chapter should say so before building anything. The Arts and Humanities Research Council's Cultural Value Project, the largest UK inquiry into the question, concluded in 2016 that "When it comes to cultural value, instead of 'measuring' it is more appropriate to speak of 'evaluation', of which measurement is but one component." It set out to "analyse cultural value without assuming that all registers of value are commensurable, or that they can be reduced to one common denominator", and it sought "a framework within which sits a variety of components of value, each of them to be evaluated by appropriate and often different qualitative and quantitative methodologies." That is a position against a single score, from the field's own research council, and any measure of a cultural quality has to answer it. The chapter does not argue that the position is wrong. It argues that whatever form is chosen — index, profile, set — the same disciplines apply to each number in it, and a profile of twelve declared numbers is better than one undeclared one.

## 2 · Why people believe what they believe

The beliefs about measures are more deeply held than those about charts, because a number that has been computed looks as though it has been found. Each is stated fairly here and met in the sections that follow.

### Belief one: a score is more objective than a judgement

If a number came out of a formula, it was not anyone's opinion. Section 3 shows that the handbook describes the formula's every step as a judgement, and section 5 that the formula is a set of exchange rates nobody announced.

### Belief two: equal weights are neutral

Giving every indicator the same weight feels like declining to choose. Section 4: "equal weighting does not mean 'no weights'", and a measure with uneven groups of indicators weights the groups unequally without anyone deciding to.

### Belief three: the weights say what matters

A published weight of 40% means that indicator drives 40% of the result. Section 4: it may drive 81%, or 21%; nominal weights "are hardly ever found to match" effective ones.

### Belief four: adding is just adding

A total of scores is the natural way to combine them. Section 5: addition is the one method that lets any strength fully pay for any weakness, and the field has spent two decades building alternatives for that reason.

### Belief five: a target focuses effort on what matters

Publish the measure and people will improve the thing. Section 7: they will improve the measure, which is sometimes the same thing and sometimes not, and the honest evidence is that both happen at once.

### Belief six: if the people measured recognise it, it is valid

A measure of a scene that the scene agrees with has passed the test. Section 9: in the measurement tradition that is face validity, which the official standards do not count as validity at all; recognition does something important and it is not that.

### Belief seven: decision-makers want one number

The ranking is what gets read. Section 11: the policy bodies that have thought hardest about it recommend a dashboard beside any index, and one of the handbook's own authors describes the spread of composites as "mostly advocacy-driven".

## 3 · The handbook's own account

### Ten steps

The handbook's method has ten steps: theoretical framework; data selection; imputation of missing data; multivariate analysis; normalisation; weighting and aggregation; uncertainty and sensitivity analysis; back to the data; links to other indicators; visualisation. The Joint Research Centre's current web guide has quietly revised the list — weighting and aggregation are now separate steps, "back to the data" has gone, and the robustness step is called sensitivity analysis — so two canonical lists are in circulation; this chapter follows the 2008 numbering where it cites pages and the web guide where it cites the toolbox. The first step is the one dashboards skip. The theoretical framework "provides the basis for the selection and combination of variables into a meaningful composite indicator": what is the construct, which indicators stand for it, and why these. Without it, the handbook's steps are arithmetic on whatever was to hand, which is the thing Martin Ravallion named: a "mashup index", "defined as a composite index for which existing theory and practice provides little or no guidance to its design", so that "the index has an unusually large number of moving parts, which the producer is essentially free to set."

### Normalisation

Indicators measured in different units — pounds, counts, percentages, scores — are put on a common scale before they are combined. The handbook and the JRC's web guide between them name the options and their costs. Ranking "is not affected by outliers and allows the performance of countries to be followed over time in terms of relative positions (rankings)" but loses the distances (handbook). Standardised scores (z-scores) are, in the web guide's words, "the most commonly used because it converts all indicators to a common scale with an average of zero and standard deviation of one", and "an indicator with extreme values will have intrinsically a greater effect on the composite indicator." Min-max scaling stretches each indicator between its lowest and highest observed values, so "the minima and maxima could be unreliable outliers, and have a distortion effect" (web guide). Distance to a reference "measures the relative position of a given indicator vis-à-vis a reference point" (handbook). "The normalisation method should take into account the data properties, as well as the objectives of the composite indicator" (handbook). What the handbook says more quietly, and Giuseppe Munda and Michela Nardo show with a worked table, is that the normalisation also sets the trade-off between dimensions: change the unit or the scaling of one indicator and the implied rate at which it exchanges for another changes with it. Section 5 returns to this.

### Weighting

The handbook's paragraph on equal weights is worth giving whole, because it is the single most-ignored paragraph in the literature:

> "Most composite indicators rely on equal weighting (EW), i.e. all variables are given the same weight. This essentially implies that all variables are "worth" the same in the composite, but it could also disguise the absence of a statistical or an empirical basis, e.g. when there is insufficient knowledge of causal relationships or a lack of consensus on the alternative. In any case, equal weighting does not mean "no weights", but implicitly implies that the weights are equal. Moreover, if variables are grouped into dimensions and those are further aggregated into the composite, then applying equal weighting to the variables may imply an unequal weighting of the dimension (the dimensions grouping the larger number of variables will have higher weight). This could result in an unbalanced structure in the composite index."

And the sentence that governs every alternative: "Regardless of which method is used, weights are essentially value judgements." The alternatives are weights from the data's own structure (principal components or factor analysis, where, as the web guide puts it, weighting "only intervenes to correct for the overlapping information of two or more correlated indicators, and it is not a measure of importance of the associated indicator"); "benefit of the doubt" weights, where each unit is scored on the weights most favourable to it, which the handbook calls "extremely parsimonious about weighting assumptions" while noting that "with BOD weights are country specific and have a number of estimation problems"; expert allocation, where, in the web guide's words, "experts are given a budget of N points, to be distributed over a number of indicators, paying more for those indicators whose importance they want to stress"; pairwise comparison (the analytic hierarchy process); and conjoint analysis of stated preferences. The 2019 review by Greco and colleagues confirms the practice: "Equal weighting is the most common scheme appearing in the development of composite indicators", for five reasons they list — "simplicity of construction", "a lack of theoretical structure to justify a differential weighting scheme", "no agreement between decision makers", "inadequate statistical and/or empirical knowledge", and "alleged objectivity" — and they add that "equal weights miss the point of differentiating between essential and less important indicators by treating them all equally." The Social Progress Index states the null that most builders leave unspoken: it "equally weights components for constructing a dimension-level score because there is no clear theoretical or empirical reason to weight any of the components more highly than any other." That is a declared judgement, and declaring it is the point.

### Sensitivity

"Several judgements have to be made when constructing composite indicators, e.g. on the selection of indicators, data normalisation, weights and aggregation methods, etc." The handbook's seventh step tests them: "Uncertainty analysis focuses on how uncertainty in the input factors propagates through the structure of the composite indicator and affects the composite indicator values. Sensitivity analysis assesses the contribution of the individual source of uncertainty to the output variance." And the output of the test is a less tidy and more defensible number: "The results of the robustness analysis are generally reported as country rankings with their related uncertainty bounds, which are due to the uncertainties at play." The web guide puts the questions a builder must be able to answer: "How do country ranks (or composite indicator scores) compare to the most likely ranks under all scenarios in building the composite indicator?" and "Which countries are the most volatile and why?" — adding that "A plurality of scenarios (all with their implications) should be initially considered, because no scenario for the development of a composite indicator is a priori better than another …". Section 6 shows what this looks like when done.

### Presentation

The handbook's tenth step is the thinnest, and this book's Chapter 3 is the reason it can be. "The way composite indicators are presented is not a trivial issue as the visualisation can influence (or help to enhance) interpretability." "Composite indicators are ultimately a communication tool, which can be greatly enhanced by proper visualisation, both static and interactive (online)." Online tools "give full transparency to the data set and allow users to drill down to underlying data." "Graphical representation of composite indicators should provide clear messages, without obscuring individual data points." What the handbook does not say in its visualisation step, and its sensitivity step requires, is that the rank's interval is shown with the rank. Section 12 makes that a rule.

## 4 · Weights are not importance

### The finding

In 2013 Paolo Paruolo, Michaela Saisana and Andrea Saltelli published a paper whose title asked whether ratings and rankings were "voodoo or science". Its abstract states the problem exactly: "Composite indicators aggregate a set of variables using weights which are understood to reflect the variables' importance in the index. In this paper we propose to measure the importance of a given variable within existing composite indicators via Karl Pearson's 'correlation ratio'; we call this measure 'main effect'. Because socioeconomic variables are heteroskedastic and correlated, relative nominal weights are hardly ever found to match relative main effects" (heteroskedastic: their spread differs from one part of the range to another). The *main effect* of an indicator is "the expected fractional reduction in variance of the composite indicator that would be obtained if that variable could be fixed" — how much of the index's movement that one indicator accounts for, estimated by regressing the index on the indicator alone and asking how much of the index's variation that explains. The *nominal weight* is the number the builder published. The two are different things, and the paper measures the gap.

The gap is large. In the Times Higher Education world university ranking as it was then constructed, the academic peer review carried a nominal weight of 0.40 and a main effect of 0.81; the teacher–student ratio carried a nominal weight of 0.20 and a main effect of 0.21. An indicator given a fifth of the weight explained a fifth of the result; an indicator given two-fifths explained four-fifths. In the Shanghai ranking, indicators weighted 0.10 and 0.20 turned out to matter almost equally: "For ARWU the main effects Si are more similar to each other than the nominal weights". In the 2009 Human Development Index, "Although the 2009 HDI developers assigned weights varying between 11% and 33%, all four indicators are roughly equally important." The mechanism is correlation: when the parts move together, the weights cannot separate them, and "In cases like this, where the variables are strongly and roughly equally correlated with the overall index, each of them ranks the countries roughly equally, and the weights are little more than cosmetic." The paper's conclusion is a list of what a builder can do: "avoid associating nominal weights with importance, but inform users of the relative importance of the variables or pillars"; "abstain from aggregating pillars when these display important trade offs"; move "from the linear one (which is fully compensatory) to a partially- or fully-non-compensatory alternative"; or "assess different weighting strategies, so as to select the one that leads to a minimum discrepancy statistic between target weights and variables importance." A 2017 paper by Becker and colleagues builds the tools for the last option — measuring importance, isolating each indicator's effect, and "an optimisation procedure … which allows weights to be fitted to agree with pre-specified values of importance" — so that the gap between what a builder meant and what the arithmetic did can be closed rather than discovered. `[Rich reference]`

### What a developer does when audited

The Joint Research Centre audits other people's indices — "over 100 statistical audits", by its own count, including the Environmental Performance Index, the Global Competitiveness Index and the Corruption Perceptions Index. Its audit of the 2025 Global Innovation Index, the fifteenth consecutive year, shows the vocabulary that results. The developers have stated since 2012 that their weights "were to be used as scaling coefficients and not importance coefficients, with the aim of arriving at sub-pillar and pillar scores that were balanced in their underlying components" — a developer disowning, in its own report, the reading of weights that section 2's third belief assumes. The audit explains why: "the ratio of two nominal weights gives the rate of substitutability between two indicators, and hence can be used to reveal the relative importance of individual indicators. This importance can then be compared with ex-post measures of a variable's importance, such as the non-linear Pearson correlation ratio." A weight is a price, and the audit checks whether the price did what the builder meant.

### What this means for a display

A dashboard that shows a composite and lists its weights has told the reader something that is true about the recipe and false about the dish. The weights are what the builder decided; the importances are what the arithmetic did; and the handbook's equal-weights paragraph says even the first can be an illusion when the groups are uneven. The rule that follows is in section 12: show the nominal weights as what they are, and show the effective importances beside them.

## 5 · Aggregation is a price list

### Compensability

The handbook's passage on aggregation is the chapter's centre and is given at length. "In both linear and geometric aggregations, weights express trade-offs between indicators. A deficit in one dimension can thus be offset (compensated) by a surplus in another. This implies an inconsistency between how weights are conceived (usually measuring the importance of the associated variable) and the actual meaning when geometric or linear aggregations are used. In a linear aggregation, the compensability is constant, while with geometric aggregations compensability is lower for the composite indicators with low values." And: "To ensure that weights remain a measure of importance, other aggregation methods should be used, in particular methods that do not allow compensability. Moreover, if different goals are equally legitimate and important, a non-compensatory logic might be necessary." Munda and Nardo, whose work the handbook cites, define the term: "Compensability refers to the existence of trade-offs, i.e. the possibility of offsetting a disadvantage on some variables by a sufficiently large advantage on another variable." Under addition, "the substitution rates equal the weights of the variables up to a multiplicative coefficient", and "The use of non-compensatory aggregation rules to construct composite indicators is compulsory for reasons of theoretical consistency when weights with the meaning of importance coefficients are used."

The handbook's toolbox gives the illustration that version 1 of this book carried and this chapter has now read at the Joint Research Centre's own reproduction (the printed Part II was beyond the fetch tool's reach — `[Further research]`): "if an hypothetical composite indicator was formed by inequality, environmental degradation, GDP per capita and unemployment, two countries, one with values (21,1,1,1) and the other with (6,6,6,6) would have equal composite indicator value (=6) under an additive aggregation. Obviously the two countries would represent very different social conditions that would not be reflected in the composite. Geometric aggregation (i.e. the product of weighted indicators) is a less compensatory approach. In our simple example the first country would have a much lower composite indicator value (=2.14) than the second country (=6.00) under the geometric aggregation." A place with one spectacular strength and three collapses equals a place that is merely sound on everything, if you add. That is not a flaw in addition. It is what addition means, and a builder who adds has claimed it.

The price list can be computed from any additive recipe, and it is worth seeing how, because the normalisation is in it. Suppose two parts, each scaled by min-max to 0–1 and given equal weight: one is a count of events per month, observed between 0 and 200; the other is years in operation, observed between 0 and 40. A rise of one year adds 1/40 to the second part's scaled value; a rise of one event per month adds 1/200 to the first; with equal weights, one year of operation therefore exchanges for five events per month, and the index is indifferent between them. Halve the weight on years, and one year buys two and a half events; widen the observed range of events to 400, and one year buys ten. Nobody chose any of those prices; the ranges and the weights set them together. That is the rate the card must state, in the parts' own units, for every pair of parts that can trade.

### The hidden price list

Martin Ravallion's two 2010 papers make the exchange rate visible by computing it. Any index that combines income with life expectancy "implicitly puts a monetary valuation on an extra year of life, where that valuation is defined by the tradeoff between longevity and income, i.e., the extra income needed to compensate for a year less of life expectancy, keeping the HDI constant". Under the old additive index the implied value was, by his account, a roughly constant share of each country's income. He computed it for the reformed 2010 index — the geometric-mean version described below — and found the reform had moved the price rather than removed it: "The HDI's value of longevity in the poorest country, Zimbabwe, is a remarkably low $0.51 per year, representing less than 0.3% of that country's (very low) mean income in 2008. Thus the 2010 HDI implies that if Zimbabwe takes a policy action that increases national income by a mere $0.52 or more per person per year at the cost of reducing average life expectancy by one year, then the country will have promoted its 'human development.' … The value tends to rise with income and reaches about $9,000 per year in the richest countries". His general lesson: "An important lesson for future composite indices is the need for transparency about the implicit tradeoffs, especially in more complicated indices." And on the mashup family as a whole: "We need to know the tradeoffs … built into a composite index if it is to be properly assessed and used." The United Nations' own researchers replied that "it is inappropriate to label the trade-offs implicit in the HDI as 'values,'" and that the ratio "merely says that the HDI puts very low weight on increases in income in rich countries, compared to the very high value on health improvements" — a live dispute about whether an implied substitution rate is a value judgement or an artefact, which this chapter records without settling. What is not in dispute is that the rate exists, can be computed, and was not on the face.

### What builders have done about it

The Human Development Index changed its arithmetic in 2010 to end perfect substitutability — and, as Ravallion's figures show, an index that allows imperfect substitution still carries a price list; it is a different one. The report's own words: "A key change was to shift to a geometric mean (which measures the typical value of a set of numbers): thus in 2010 the HDI is the geometric mean of the three dimension indices. Poor performance in any dimension is now directly reflected in the HDI, and there is no longer perfect substitutability across dimensions." The technical note: the new formula "embodies imperfect substitutability across all HDI dimensions. It thus addresses one of the most serious criticisms of the linear aggregation formula, which allowed for perfect substitution across dimensions. Some substitutability is inherent in the definition of any index that increases with the values of its components."

Two UK and international place indices show the same instinct worked out further. The Legatum Prosperity Index combines its three domains with a power mean whose exponent is chosen to encode non-substitutability: "the domains (Development, Freedom, and Society) are poor substitutes for one another as they are all distinct and indispensable components of prosperity"; "When p is less than one, a decrease in one dimension is not fully compensated for by an equal and opposite increase in another, and the lower the value of p the more the overall p-mean decreases, i.e. lower values of p punish weakness more than higher values of p"; "We have elected to set p equal to –1 when calculating the p-mean of the domains". The exponent rises as the parts become better substitutes — minus one at the top, zero (the geometric mean) in the middle, one (plain addition) at the bottom. Whatever one thinks of the index, the aggregation is a stated judgement about which parts may pay for which. The English Indices of Deprivation reach the same end by a different route: domain scores are ranked and then exponentially transformed, a method that "was explicitly designed to reduce 'cancellation effects'", so that "the Indices are specifically constructed to identify deprivation and not affluence" — a one-tailed instrument by design. That is a decision a cultural measure would also have to make, and the arithmetic follows from it. An instrument built to find shortfall should let a bad part dominate (a low exponent; a transformation that stretches the deprived end); one built to find what thrives should let a good part show through; one built for both needs a symmetric rule and a reader who can see the shape. The same parts, under the three designs, rank places differently, and none of the three is neutral.

The survey evidence on how often any of this is done is discouraging. Böhringer and Jochem reviewed eleven sustainability indices in policy use and concluded that "these indices fail to fulfill fundamental scientific requirements making them rather useless if not misleading with respect to policy advice", because the normalisation, weighting and aggregation steps did not meet the conditions under which the arithmetic means anything.

## 6 · The robustness of a rank

### What an audit does

Saisana, Saltelli and Tarantola's 2005 paper in the Royal Statistical Society's journal is where the method of auditing a composite was set out, on the United Nations' Technology Achievement Index. "The construction of composite indicators involves stages where judgement has to be made: the selection of sub-indicators, choice of model, weighting indicators and treatment of missing values etc." The audit varies every one of those judgements at once, many thousands of times, and reports what happens to each country's rank. For the index in question most ranks were stable — "For most countries, the original TAI value is very close to the MCTAI median value of the distribution" — but one was not: "There is, however, one country, the Netherlands, that is strongly favoured in the original TAI … its performance falls at the ninth position, behind Singapore, the UK and Australia." The paper's own summary of the result: "The index is no longer a magic number corresponding to a crisp normalization, weighting scheme and weight assignment, but reflects uncertainty and ambiguity in a more transparent and defensible fashion." And its purpose: the method "could provide information on whether the countries' rankings measure anything meaningful and could reduce the possibility that composite indicators may send misleading or non-robust policy messages."

Twenty years on, this is routine for the indices that submit to it. The 2025 Global Innovation Index audit ran "5,000 runs" varying "the treatment of missing data, the aggregation formula and weights applied to the pillar level", and reported: "For 85 of the 139 economies the width of the 90% GII rank confidence interval is less than 10 positions in rank"; "Seven economies … have 90 percent confidence interval widths of more than 20 positions"; "73 percent of the economies are found to shift fewer than three positions with respect to the median rank". And the reading instruction: readers should "consider an economy's ranking … not only at face value, but also within the 90 percent confidence intervals." The United Nations' own uncertainty analysis of the Human Development Index found "the shifts in ranking are relatively minor and seem not to compromise the robustness of the HDI" while identifying that "the weights applied to income and education are the main drivers of uncertainty". A rank without an interval is a point estimate presented as a fact.

### Scores, ranks and digits

Three habits follow. First, a rank is a coarser object than a score, and the English deprivation index uses ranks on purpose: domains are standardised "by ranking the Lower-layer Super Output Areas from least deprived … to most deprived … separately for each of the seven domains" (the small statistical areas of about 1,500 people the index is built on), and sensitivity testing found that alternative weightings "did not have a large impact on the final Index of Multiple Deprivation ranks." Second, a score's digits are a claim about precision, and the official guidance is now, in all but name, the two-significant-digits rule Chapter 3 took from Ehrenberg: the ONS style guide says "You should round the numbers for comparison to two effective digits", that the level of rounding "should be effective, and chosen according to the intended use", and "Always use the lowest level possible for the intended user." The Analysis Function adds the cost: "Rounding does reduce precision. This usually means that the reported totals no longer equal the sum of the component parts." A composite reported to two decimal places on a ten-point scale has claimed a resolution of one part in a thousand from inputs that were ranks, sliders and expert guesses. Third, the Analysis Function's worked example of how to say a range — "between 220 and 250 people were killed … with a central estimate of 230 deaths" — is the sentence form for a rank with its interval: *between eighth and nineteenth, most likely twelfth*.

## 7 · When the measure becomes the target

### Who said what

Chapter 1 recorded that the most reliable thing a published measure does is change the behaviour it measures. This chapter went to the sources of the two "laws" that are quoted for it, and the record is worth setting straight because the canon is misquoted almost everywhere.

Charles Goodhart's sentence of 1975 was an aside, inside a subordinate clause, in a paper on British monetary policy, and he named it after himself in jest. As Chrystal and Mizen report it (the original conference volume was not reachable — secondary), the text reads: "Ignoring Goodhart's law, that any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes". Three independent reproductions agree on the words. The sentence everyone now quotes — "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" — is not Goodhart's. It is the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern's, in a 1997 article on audit in British universities, where she follows it with the application: "The more a 2.1 examination performance becomes an expectation, the poorer it becomes as a discriminator of individual performances." She does not attribute it to Goodhart; she writes that "Hoskin describes this as 'Goodhart's law', after the latter's observation on instruments for monetary control which lead to other devices for monetary flexibility having to be invented." So: Goodhart stated a regularity about monetary indicators; Keith Hoskin, in 1996, generalised it under Goodhart's name (his chapter was not reachable); Strathern, in 1997, supplied the sentence. Strathern's own thesis is the one this chapter needs: "audit does more than monitor — it has a life of its own that jeopardizes the life it audits."

Donald Campbell's law has the evidence attached. From his 1976 paper, read at source: "The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor." He illustrates with crime statistics — a well-publicised crackdown "had as its main effect the corruption of crime-rate indicators" — and with schooling: "achievement tests may well be valuable indicators of general school achievement under conditions of normal teaching aimed at general competence. But when test scores become the goal of the teaching process, they both lose their value as indicators of educational status and distort the educational process in undesirable ways." His remedy, offered with a doubt attached: "Many commentators, including myself (1969b), assume that the use of multiple indicators, all recognized as imperfect, will alleviate the problem, although Ridgeway (1956) doubts this." Steven Kerr's 1975 paper on "the folly of rewarding A, while hoping for B" gives the organisational mechanism — organisms "seek information concerning what activities are rewarded, and then seek to do (or at least pretend to do) those things, often to the virtual exclusion of activities not rewarded" — and names the cause that bears on dashboards most: "Fascination with an 'Objective' Criterion". "Nonetheless, many individuals seek to establish simple, quantifiable standards against which to measure and reward performance. Such efforts may be successful in highly predictable areas within an organization, but are likely to cause goal displacement when applied anywhere else."

### The taxonomy, and the evidence

The best-evidenced study of what targets do is Gwyn Bevan and Christopher Hood's 2006 paper on the English public services under what they call "targets and terror". They define gaming as "reactive subversion such as 'hitting the target and missing the point' or reducing performance where targets do not apply", and they name three kinds. "Ratchet effects refer to the tendency for central controllers to base next year's targets on last year's performance" — so that a manager who expects to be in post next year has "a perverse incentive not to exceed targets even if they could easily do so". "Threshold effects refer to the effects of targets on the distribution of performance" — a uniform target gives those above it an incentive "to allow their performance to deteriorate to the standard". And "Attempts to achieve targets at the cost of significant but unmeasured aspects of performance result in output distortions." Underneath all three is what they call synecdoche — "taking a part to stand for a whole" — the assumption "that the part on which performance is measured can adequately represent performance on the whole".

The evidence is specific. "A study by the Commission for Health Improvement (2003c) found evidence that in a third of ambulance trusts, response times had been 'corrected' to be reported to be less than eight minutes", and the distribution showed "a curious 'spike' at 8 minutes – with the strong implication that times between 8 and 9 minutes have been reclassified to be less than 8 minutes." Hospitals held patients "in queues of ambulances outside A&E Departments until the hospital in question was confident that that patient could be seen within four hours". "The National Audit Office (2001) reported evidence that nine NHS trusts had 'inappropriately' adjusted their waiting lists … affecting nearly 6000 patient records." The parliamentary committee that reviewed the regime in 2003 found that "Some ambulance trusts were massaging their response times", that at one eye hospital waiting-time targets "have been achieved at the expense of cancellation and delay of follow-up appointments", and — the figure about the measurers rather than the measured — that "in over 80% of such 'first time' validations" the National Audit Office "found that the organisation had materially misstated their achievements or had failed to disclose potentially material weaknesses with their data." In schooling, Daniel Koretz defines score inflation as "a gain in scores that substantially overstates the improvement in learning it implies", documents the "sawtooth" pattern when a new test is introduced, and reports that "Typically, gains on high-stakes tests have been 3 to 5 times as large as gains on other tests (such as the National Assessment of Educational Progress) with low (or lower) stakes." His conclusion: "score gains achieved under high-stakes conditions should not be accepted at face value."

### The symmetry

And yet the measured things improved, and the book must say so with the same force. Bevan and Hood record it themselves: "In 2002, 23 per cent of patients spent over four hours in A&E departments, but in the three months from April to June 2004 only 5.3 per cent stayed that long"; by March 2003, "when virtually no patient in England was reported as waiting more than 12 months for an elective admission, the equivalent figures for Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland were 10, 16 and 22 per cent." Their verdict: "Although there were indeed dramatic improvements in reported performance, we do not know the extent to which these were genuine or offset by gaming that resulted in reductions in performance that was not captured by targets." The economists who tested it found less gaming than the public-administration scholars feared, and all of what follows was read at the authors' abstracts. Kelman and Friedman, using data from all 155 English hospital trusts (abstract †): "we find dramatic wait-time performance improvements between 2003 and 2006 and no evidence for any of the dysfunctional effects that have been hypothesized in connection with this target." Propper and colleagues, comparing England with Scotland (abstract †): "targets met their goals of reducing waiting times without diverting activity from other less well monitored aspects of health care and without decreasing patient health on exit from hospital." Besley, Bevan and Burchardi, comparing England with Wales (abstract †): the policy "did indeed reduce waiting times in England as compared with Wales. However, there is some evidence there was in England, initially, some shuffling of prospective patients to meet specific targets". And the cleanest natural experiment runs the other way: when Wales abolished school league tables in 2001 and England kept them, Burgess, Wilson and Worth found (abstract †) "significant and robust evidence that this reform markedly reduced school effectiveness in Wales relative to England." Bevan and Hamblin add the twist that cuts both ways: of the four UK nations, "only in England was this target integral to a ranking system of 'star rating', which inflicted reputational damage on services that failed to hit targets, and only in England has this target been met." The mechanism that produced the gaming produced the delivery.

The summary across the literature is this: every rigorous comparison found the targeted thing really improved; every audit found the numbers were also massaged at the margin; and nobody can say how much of the improvement was real, because nobody built the independent check that would say — Hood's own diagnosis being that "the slow and half-hearted approach to developing independent verification of performance data might itself be interpreted as a form of gaming by the central managers." The centre, by not verifying, was gaming too.

### Without a cheat

A 2018 taxonomy by Manheim and Garrabrant is useful because three of its four ways a proxy fails need nobody to behave badly. *Regressional*: "When selecting for a proxy measure, you select not only for the true goal, but also for the difference between the proxy and the goal." *Extremal*: "Worlds in which the proxy takes an extreme value may be very different from the ordinary worlds in which the relationship between the proxy and the goal was observed." *Causal*: "When the causal path between the proxy and the goal is indirect, intervening can change the relationship between the measure and proxy." Only the fourth, *adversarial*, needs an agent who "react[s] in different ways to create Goodhart-like effects". A dashboard that ranks places by a proxy will see the first three happen on their own — the top of the ranking will be the places where the proxy and the goal diverge most, and acting on the proxy will loosen its link to the goal — before anyone has gamed anything.

### Reactivity, and the cultural sector

Wendy Espeland and Michael Sauder's 2007 paper gives the general word: *reactivity*, "the idea that people change their behavior in reaction to being evaluated, observed, or measured", through two mechanisms, "self-fulfilling prophecy and commensuration" (the paper's definitions of the two were not reachable; the book is in Chapter 2). Jerry Muller's name for the policy pathology is "metric fixation", "the seemingly irresistible pressure to measure performance, to publicize it, and to reward it, often in the face of evidence that this just doesn't work very well" — with the proviso, in his own words, that "The problem is not measurement, but excessive measurement and inappropriate measurement".

The cultural sector has written its own version. Eleonora Belfiore and Oliver Bennett, reviewing the social-impact literature: "research on both value and impacts has usually been underpinned by an advocacy agenda, even when the research agenda has been disguised as one of dispassionate enquiry"; "considerably more time and resources have been spent on looking for 'proof' of impacts than on actually trying to understand them"; and the risk of "yet another round of policy-based evidence-making". John Holden, in 2004, quoting a Cabinet Office strategy paper: "those things that [are] easy to measure tend to become objectives, and those that [are not, are] downplayed or ignored"; and in his own words in 2006: "Money thus flows into measurement, and only those things that can be measured get measured." The Centre for Cultural Value's 2021 evaluation principles ask the sector to "prioritise learning over monitoring and foreground understanding over judgement", and their authors' launch essay observes that "Lots of effort goes into producing low-value monitoring and reporting, but too little time is dedicated to the kind of deep reflection and learning required to develop critical understanding and enrich creative practice." Every one of these is Campbell's law in the sector's own voice, and every one bears on a public measure of a cultural quality: once it exists and is read, the scene will be measured by it and may begin to arrange itself toward it. Chapter 10 owns the ethics; this chapter's rule is that the audit — the independent check on whether the measured thing moved — is designed with the measure and not after it. `[Revisit → Ch 10]`

## 8 · Validity — what a measure must show

### The unified view

The measurement tradition that has thought longest about whether a number means what it claims is psychometrics, and its vocabulary transfers whole. The founding statement is Cronbach and Meehl's 1955 paper: "Construct validation is involved whenever a test is to be interpreted as a measure of some attribute or quality which is not 'operationally defined.'" A construct "is some postulated attribute of people, assumed to be reflected in test performance", and it is given meaning by the web of expected relationships it sits in — "to 'make clear what something is' means to set forth the laws in which it occurs" — so that "A necessary condition for a construct to be scientifically admissible is that it occur in a nomological net, at least some of whose laws involve observables." The line to build a measure on: "Unless the network makes contact with observations, and exhibits explicit, public steps of inference, construct validation cannot be claimed". The same demand this chapter makes of a composite — that the steps from parts to number be explicit and public — was made of psychological constructs in 1955.

Samuel Messick's 1995 statement is the one modern standards rest on. "Validity is an overall evaluative judgment of the degree to which empirical evidence and theoretical rationales support the adequacy and appropriateness of interpretations and actions based on test scores or other modes of assessment" (the fetch tool returned the sentence in two halves; the join is verified). "Validity is not a property of the test or assessment as such, but rather of the meaning of the test scores." He names six aspects of construct validity — "content, substantive, structural, generalizability, external, and consequential" — and two ways a measure fails: *construct underrepresentation*, where "the assessment is too narrow and fails to include important dimensions or facets", and *construct-irrelevant variance*, where "the assessment is too broad, containing excess reliable variance associated with other" things. The consequential aspect is the one that matters for a public display: it "appraises the value implications of score interpretation as a basis for action as well as the actual and potential consequences of test use, especially in regard to sources of invalidity related to issues of bias, fairness, and distributive justice".

The 2014 *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* — the joint statement of the American Educational Research Association, the American Psychological Association and the National Council on Measurement in Education — define the term as "the degree to which evidence and theory support the interpretations of test scores for proposed uses of tests", and insist: "It is the interpretations of test scores for proposed uses that are evaluated, not the test itself." "Validity is a unitary concept. It is the degree to which all the accumulated evidence supports the intended interpretation of test scores for the proposed use." They name five sources of evidence: test content ("the themes, wording, and format of the items, tasks, or questions"); response processes ("the fit between the construct and the detailed nature of the performance or response actually engaged in by test takers" — the official home of cognitive testing, of asking whether respondents understood the question as meant); internal structure ("the degree to which the relationships among test items and test components conform to the construct"); relations to other variables, where "Relationships between test scores and other measures intended to assess the same or similar constructs provide convergent evidence … whereas relationships between test scores and measures purportedly of different constructs provide discriminant evidence"; and consequences of testing. On consequences they draw a line a policy dashboard should know: "Although information about the consequences of testing may influence decisions about test use, such consequences do not, in and of themselves, detract from the validity of intended interpretations" — a consequence counts against validity only if it can be traced to a flaw in the measure, such as underrepresentation or irrelevant variance. And they do not mention face validity at all.

Denny Borsboom and colleagues' 2004 paper is the realist dissent (reached as quoted in two later papers — secondary): "a test is valid for measuring an attribute if and only if (a) the attribute exists, (b) variations in the attribute causally produce variations in the outcomes of the measurement procedure"; "validity is about ontology; validation is about epistemology." For a measure of a felt quality that is the sharpest question there is: does the thing exist, and does it move the number?

### Reliability

Reliability is "the consistency of scores across replications of a testing procedure", and the *Standards* add that "the need for precision increases as the consequences of decisions and interpretations grow in importance." Chapter 4 found that official statistics means something else by the word (how little a number moves on revision); a measure built from judgements needs both senses and should say which. Where people rate things — venues, nights, qualities — the statistic that must be reported is inter-rater reliability, "the extent to which data collectors (raters) assign the same score to the same variable", and simple percentage agreement "does not take account of the possibility that raters guessed on scores. It thus may overestimate the true agreement"; Cohen's kappa exists to correct that for categories, and the intraclass correlation for continuous ratings (the latter's standard bands reached only by paraphrase †). A measure can be perfectly reliable and wrong — "consistent but wrong", in the methods-text phrase — so reliability is where validation starts and not where it ends.

### Convergent and discriminant

The practical test for a new indicator is Campbell and Fiske's 1959 discipline (the original was not reachable; the definitions are the *Standards'* and a methods text's, and the one phrase of theirs — "more than one method must be employed in the … validation process" — is as quoted by Rönkkö and Cho): convergent validity is "the degree to which concepts that should be related theoretically are interrelated in reality", discriminant validity "the degree to which concepts that should not be related theoretically are, in fact, not interrelated in reality". Translated: a measure of a place's cultural life should move with other ways of seeing that life (attendance, longevity, what people say) and should *not* move with things it is not supposed to measure (population size, rateable value, how many people use the platform the data came from). Chapter 4's warning about platform feeds is a discriminant-validity warning: if the measure correlates with platform coverage, some of it is measuring the platform.

### The official precedent

There is an official UK precedent for measuring a felt quality, and it is instructive in every detail. Since April 2011 the ONS has asked four questions on its largest household survey: "Overall, how satisfied are you with your life nowadays, where 0 is 'not at all satisfied' and 10 is 'completely satisfied'?"; "Overall, to what extent do you feel that the things you do in your life are worthwhile …?"; "Overall, how happy did you feel yesterday …?"; and "On a scale where 0 is 'not at all anxious' and 10 is 'completely anxious', overall, how anxious did you feel yesterday?" The questions were designed to cover three kinds of wellbeing — "evaluative, eudemonic, and affective experience" — on the recommendation of Dolan, Layard and Metcalfe, whose paper for the ONS concluded that "there are stable and reliable patterns in SWB, even over the course of many years" and that "The time has certainly come for regular measurement of SWB in the largest standard government surveys". The questions were cognitively tested: "Cognitive testing was undertaken to understand how respondents chose their score on the 11-point scale, and what they considered to be 'high', 'low', and 'average' ratings". And — the decision that bears most on this chapter — "It is not appropriate to combine these questions. This is because they are all individually important and together, they give a balanced approach to well-being." The national statistician measures a felt quality with four tested questions and declines to make them one number. `[Rich reference]`

## 9 · Validation by recognition

The studio's position, from its own published work and carried into the brief, is that a measure of a scene is valid only if the scene recognises itself in it. This section tests that position against the two traditions that have argued about it — qualitative research's "member check" and the participatory-indicator literature — and arrives at a careful answer: recognition does something essential, and it is not what the word "valid" means.

### The member check

Lincoln and Guba's 1985 *Naturalistic Inquiry* called the member check "the most crucial technique for establishing credibility" (reached as quoted with page number in two later sources — secondary): returning "data, analytic categories, interpretations, and conclusions" to "members of those stakeholding groups from whom the data were originally collected", giving them the "opportunity to correct errors of fact and challenge what are perceived to be wrong interpretations". Three decades of practice have split the field. Birt and colleagues' 2016 commentary, whose title asks whether member checking is "a tool to enhance trustworthiness or merely a nod to validation", finds that "Member checking is often mentioned as one in a list of validation techniques" and that "This simplistic reporting might not acknowledge the value of using the method, nor its juxtaposition with the interpretative stance of qualitative research"; it raises (in a partial copy of the body) "the changing nature of interpretations of phenomena over time" and the question "Are research data 'owned' by the researcher or does it always 'belong' to the participant?", and proposes a structured, late, synthesised version instead. Janice Morse, the method's sharpest critic, writes (with colleagues, in 2002) that "member checks may actually invalidate the work of the researcher and keep the level of analysis inappropriately close to the data", because findings are "synthesized, decontextualized, and abstracted from (and across) individual participants, so there is no reason for individuals to be able to recognize themselves or their particular experiences" — and so "it is actually not a verification strategy". David Thomas's 2017 review (the publisher page was blocked; the abstract was reached only in reconstruction and through a later summary) found, in that summary's words, "limited evidence demonstrating improvements in research findings, credibility or trustworthiness". A 2024 study that tried it reports the texture: eleven of thirteen participants returned their transcripts, nine unchanged, and the edits were "corrections to grammar and punctuation".

The line through this is clear enough to state as a rule. A member check validates *resonance* and corrects *fact*. It does not validate an *interpretation* across people, and it does not establish that a *construct* exists. Where the thing measured is a person's own experience, their recognition is strong evidence; where the thing measured is an abstraction across many people — a scene, a vibe, a place's cultural life — no individual is positioned to recognise it, and their failure to do so is not a failure of the measure.

### Participatory indicators

The participatory tradition makes stronger claims and has started to test them. Fraser and colleagues, from three case studies (quotations string-verified by the research agent; the paper is not open and the verifier could not reach it): "the process of engaging people to select indicators also provides an opportunity for community empowerment that conventional development approaches have failed to provide"; "The extra accuracy and detail that participatory processes bring to indicator selection make an apparently simply job (choosing performance indicators) very complex"; and the warning that "Multi-stakeholder processes must formally feed into decision-making forums or they risk being viewed as irrelevant". Mark Reed's 2008 review of the evidence (likewise string-verified by its agent and unreachable to the verifier) separates the claims from the proof: "by involving stakeholders, it is argued that the quality and durability of decisions is likely to be greater"; "However, such claims have rarely been tested, and there is growing disillusionment among environmental managers and conservationists who have failed to see these claims realised"; and the verdict — "Although there is evidence that stakeholder participation can lead to more effective and durable decisions, there is little empirical evidence to support many of the other claims that have been made." A 2016 comparative study of 24 cases by de Vente and colleagues found that "factors associated with process design were more significant" than context, that what mattered was "Legitimate representation of stakeholders; professional facilitation including structured methods for aggregating information and balancing power dynamics among participants; and provision of information and decision-making power to all participants", that "Increased trust and learning were perceived as major outcomes", and — the tension a public body should know — that "processes initiated or facilitated by government bodies led to significantly less trust, information gain, and learning", though their decisions "were more likely to be accepted and implemented."

The one study found that directly tested community-chosen indicators against field science is Reed, Dougill and Baker's 2008 work with pastoralists on land degradation (abstract reached in reconstruction — treat the wording with care): "Although most indicators suggested by pastoralists were validated through empirical work, they were not always sufficiently accurate or reliable for objective degradation assessment, showing that knowledge cannot be accepted unquestioningly"; "by combining participatory and ecological approaches, it is possible to derive more accurate and relevant indicators than either approach could achieve alone." Mostly valid, not always reliable, and best combined: that is the evidential status of "validation by recognition" as this chapter can find it.

### What recognition is, then

In the psychometric vocabulary, a measure the measured people recognise has *face validity*: in a standard methods text, you "look at the operationalization and see whether 'on its face' it seems like a good translation of the construct", and this is "probably the weakest way to try to demonstrate construct validity" because "it is essentially a subjective judgment call". The *Standards* do not list it among the sources of validity evidence. In the qualitative vocabulary the same thing is *credibility*, and it is the first criterion of trustworthiness. Each tradition is right about its own object. Recognition is necessary for a public measure — Chapter 2 found that a reader who does not see their place in a display does not use it, and Carly's failure register says the same — and it is where facts get corrected and where the response-process evidence (did people understand the question as meant?) is gathered. It is not evidence that the construct exists or that the number tracks it. A measure that the scene recognises and that correlates with platform coverage is a recognised measure of the platform.

Two further voices settle where the authority to choose the parts of a measure lies, which is a different question from whether the measure is valid. Amartya Sen, explaining why he will not fix a list of the capabilities that matter, objects to "insisting on one pre-determined canonical list of capabilities, chosen by theorists without any general social discussion or public reasoning", partly because he cannot see "how the exact lists and weights would be chosen without appropriate specification of the context of their use (which could vary)", and because "To insist on a 'fixed forever' list of capabilities would deny the possibility of progress in social understanding, and also go against the productive role of public discussion". The disability movement's principle, "Nothing About Us Without Us", in its own book's words "expresses the conviction of people with disabilities that they know what is best for them", and the United Nations records that the movement's involvement in drafting the disability convention "has proved to be an excellent example of how the principle of full participation can be put into practice". Who chooses the dimensions and the weights is a matter of legitimacy, and the people measured have the first claim. Whether the resulting number is valid is a matter of evidence, and recognition is one source among five. The studio's position survives this section as two rules instead of one: the scene has the authority over what counts, and the measure still owes the other four kinds of evidence.

## 10 · How culture and place are measured now

This section reads instruments for their published method only — what they combine, how, and what they disclose — and never for their findings about any place.

### The cultural frameworks

The AHRC's position was given in section 1. Three UK instruments show the forms that follow from it. The Department for Culture, Media and Sport's Culture and Heritage Capital programme defines its object, after Throsby, as "An asset which embodies, stores or gives rise to cultural value in addition to whatever economic value it may possess", and aims to "create publicly available statistics and guidance that will allow for improved articulation of the value of the culture and heritage sectors in decision making" through "an approach to aid decisions on public funding that is consistent with Social Cost Benefit Analysis principles published in HM Treasury's Green Book" — with the caveat that "economic methodology should be used alongside other information, both quantitative and qualitative". Its form is not an index of a place but a bank of monetary values per kind of asset, for appraisal: Chapter 2's official reader, answered in the official reader's currency.

Arts Council England's quality metrics — the Impact & Insight Toolkit, descended through Manchester and a Western Australian public-value framework — are the clearest UK example of a *profile* instrument. The 2016 national test used twelve dimension statements, each rated on a slider by the makers before and after, by invited peers, and by the public: *Concept* "It was an interesting idea"; *Presentation* "It was well produced and presented"; *Distinctiveness* "It was different from things I've experienced before"; *Challenge* "It was thought-provoking"; *Captivation* "It was absorbing and held my attention"; *Enthusiasm* "I would come to something like this again"; *Local Impact* "It is important that it's happening here"; *Relevance* "It had something to say about the world in which we live"; *Rigour* "It was well thought-through and put together"; and, for makers and peers only, *Risk*, *Originality* and *Excellence*. Each evaluation "was then summarised with 5 numbers by respondent/time category (self prior, self post, peer and public) per dimension … using medians and interquartile ranges", and read by triangulation: where "self prior scores … are in close alignment with self post, peer and public responses then the work is delivering against the organisation's creative expectations." No single score is produced, and the designers say why: "The dimension scores for individual organisations, or in aggregate, are not a clapometer, in which a successful piece of work has to be seen to score highly on every single dimension." They warn against the comparison the form invites — "comparing apples, pears and pineapples … should only be done in ways that are appropriate" — and record that "Organisations acknowledged that the use of standardised metrics could create anxiety around particular pieces of work being 'judged' in particular ways." The current framework has grown into six domains, five of outcomes and one of "qualities", with outcomes "measuring a change in the survey respondent" and qualities "measuring their personal opinion about the qualities that the work possessed." The report cited is the delivery team's; the independent academic critiques were not reachable. `[Further research]`

UNESCO's Culture|2030 indicators — four dimensions, twenty-two indicators — are a third form. "The Indicators are meant to provide evidence of change over time in the same place, rather than global comparability or ranking between different countries or cities"; the framework "is more aspirational than normative, recognizing opportunities for aspiration and improvement, rather than aiming at measuring achievement or failure against absolute benchmarks." No composite is produced. This is the longitudinal self-profile: a place against its own past, not against other places. John Holden's triad is the vocabulary most of this writing uses — "Publicly funded culture generates three types of value – intrinsic, instrumental and institutional", with intrinsic values those "that relate to the subjective experience of culture intellectually, emotionally and spiritually" — and his 2004 diagnosis of the whole field is still the sharpest: "the identifiable measures and 'ancillary benefits' that flow from culture have become more important than the cultural activity itself: the tail is wagging the dog".

The two UK instruments that chose not to produce one number — the ONS's, from a public body, and the Thriving Places Index, from a charity — enact the choice and do not explain it; three of the five (Thriving Places, Social Progress, Legatum) publish no sensitivity analysis, and so fail this chapter's robustness rule as plainly as any sector instrument; the explicit arguments live in the international literature of section 11. And only one instrument in the table hands the weights to the reader.

### The sector's own composite, as method

One composite index exists in the studio's sector, and version 1 of this book named it. This chapter reads its public methodology only for what it discloses, reports nothing about any city, and applies to it exactly the test it has applied to the five instruments above. Whether a named method audit of a sector instrument belongs here or in Chapter 15, *Learning from the best*, is for Carly.

Creative Footprint's methodology page states: "We measure a city's Creative Footprint using 15 criteria in three categories: Space, Content, and Conditions." It says who scores — "Some information is objective (ex. size, events per month etc.), but others is more subjective (ex. strength of creative output, overall concept etc.), therefore we employ the services of local experts to accurately assess each and every venue" — and an early overview says the data "is run through an algorithm in order to generate a tangible score out of 10". A 2024 edition's methodology section says interviewees "respond to specific statements using a psychometric response scale called the Likert scale", lists fifteen indicators that differ in name and content from the website's and from the early overview's, and says the data "are also integrated with CFP's proprietary algorithms to calculate the CFP score." Not disclosed anywhere read: the per-criterion scale, the normalisation, the weights, the aggregation, any sensitivity analysis, or a changelog for the indicator set across editions. The one test a reader can run from the published numbers is to ask whether the headline can be reproduced from the parts, and the first draft of this chapter ran it badly, so the fair version is given here. The 2024 report prints three category scores of 8.58, 5.90 and 5.41 and a headline of 6.10. The simple means do not give 6.10 — arithmetic 6.63, geometric 6.49, harmonic 6.37 — but several recipes this chapter has described would: a weighted arithmetic mean with about 15% of the weight on the highest category; a power mean with an exponent near −4, of the family Legatum uses to "punish weakness"; or equal weights across the fifteen criteria rather than the three categories, which, by the handbook's own paragraph on uneven groups, would not be recoverable from three category means at all. The headline is consistent with any of these and the published method does not say which. That is the finding, and it is a finding about disclosure, not arithmetic: a reader cannot reproduce the number, and so cannot tell what one part is worth in terms of another, which is what the price-list rule exists to show. On that rule the instrument fails; on the sensitivity rule it fails alongside three of the five instruments in the table; on the parts-list rule it passes. The chapter says nothing about whether its judgements of any place are right, because nothing published allows anyone outside to say.

## 11 · The form question

Whether to build a composite at all is a choice the literature has argued for the better part of two decades, and the studio's own framework keeps open — version 1 recorded that the result "may be an index, a diagnostic, a framework, a value ledger, a modular evidence toolkit or a hybrid — or a decision that a scored index is not useful". This section gives the forms and the arguments; the choice is Carly's.

The forms this chapter has found exemplars for are five; the studio's "value ledger" and "diagnostic" would be variants of the second and third. A **composite**: one number per place, with a rank. A **scoreboard** or **dashboard**: the component indicators side by side, with no combination. A **profile**: the components drawn as a shape — a radar, a doughnut, a row of sliders — so the reader sees the pattern. A **user-weighted composite**: the components with sliders for the weights, so the reader builds the number. A **longitudinal self-profile**: each place against its own past, with no cross-place comparison at all. Each has a published exemplar in section 10.

The Stiglitz–Sen–Fitoussi Commission of 2009, convened by the French government to rethink the measurement of economic performance and social progress, gave the policy-level answer. On sustainability its eleventh recommendation reads: "Sustainability assessment requires a well-identified dashboard of indicators. The distinctive feature of the components of this dashboard should be that they are interpretable as variations of some underlying 'stocks'." Its analogy: "when driving a car, a meter that added up in one single number the current speed of the vehicle and the remaining level of gasoline would not be of any help to the driver. Both pieces of information are critical and need to be displayed in distinct, clearly visible areas of the dashboard." On quality of life it was plural — "no single measure can summarize something as complex as the well-being of the members of society, our system of measurement must encompass a range of different measures" — and it handed the combining to the reader: "Statistical offices should provide the information needed to aggregate across quality-of-life dimensions, allowing the construction of different indexes", while admitting that "there are strong demands to develop a single summary measure." On the composite health indices then in use: "they all inevitably rest on ethical judgements that are controversial, and on weights for various medical conditions whose legitimacy is not always clear."

The newest official statement is the UN Economic Commission for Europe's 2025 guidelines on measuring well-being, whose chapter on composite indicators defines the alternative with unusual precision: "In a dashboard, the entire set of indicators is presented in such a way that the reader can (and indeed must) deduce developments and comparative levels of well-being from the visualization or database." "A dashboard provides the set of component series but places the onus on the user to consider the overall outcome and trade-offs between the components." "Different stakeholders and other users have different needs. Some users and some purposes are better served by the dashboard model, while other users and purposes benefit more from a composite indicator." It names compensability as the assumption to watch — "In a composite time series that combines two indicators (A and B), the implicit assumption is that an increase in A can be offset by a decrease in B" — and it recommends: "composite indices should be accompanied by dashboards (or other visualizations or dashboards) that give information about the development in the underlying components." And on the absence of objective weights: it "does not necessarily lead to rejection … as long as the entire process is transparent." `[Rich reference]`

The dissenting voice on the composite's purpose is Andrea Saltelli's — one of the handbook's authors — whose 2007 commentary (abstract †) describes "the mostly advocacy-driven spread of these measures in recent years" and asks "to what extent composite indicators, capable of aggregating multi-dimensional processes into simplified, stylised concepts, are up to the task of underpinning the development of data-based narratives for political advocacy." Ravallion's conclusion is blunter: "A composite index is not essential for many of the purposes of evidence-based development policy-making", against "the 'dashboard' alternative of monitoring the components separately." No empirical study was found of whether decision-makers use or trust a composite more than a dashboard; the belief that they do is, on this chapter's evidence, Practice. `[Further research]`

What the chapter can say about the choice is this. A composite answers one question — *which is better overall?* — and only that one, and only under its price list. A dashboard answers many questions and asks the reader to do the combining, which Chapter 3 found most readers will not do unaided. A profile shows the shape and invites the reader to see imbalance, which is exactly what addition hides. A user-weighted composite is the handbook's transparency carried to its end: the reader is the builder, and the exchange rates are theirs. A longitudinal self-profile avoids the ranking that Chapter 1 found acts on the ranked, at the cost of the comparison Chapter 2 found some readers need. The one thing every official body agrees on is that whichever is chosen, the components travel with it — which is the subject of the next section.

## 12 · The recipe on the face

Version 1 of this book stated one Fixed rule for whatever form the studio chooses: *the recipe on the face*. This chapter has found that rule stated, independently and in different words, by the handbook ("if the construction process is not transparent" is the condition on three of its six cons), by Cronbach and Meehl for psychological constructs ("explicit, public steps of inference"), by Paruolo and colleagues ("inform users of the relative importance of the variables"), by Ravallion ("transparency about the implicit tradeoffs"), by the UNECE ("accompanied by dashboards … that give information about the development in the underlying components"), and by the Code of Practice for Statistics (Chapter 4's practices on methods and quality). The rule is a Nightlife Culture rule with Standard behind it. What follows is what "the recipe" contains, assembled from the sources, and what "on the face" means.

The recipe has nine lines.

1. **The construct, in a sentence**, and the theoretical framework that connects it to the parts — the handbook's first step; Cronbach and Meehl's net.
2. **The parts**: each indicator, with Chapter 4's provenance set for each (unit, period, base, source, status).
3. **The normalisation**, and the fact that it sets exchange rates (Munda and Nardo).
4. **The nominal weights and who set them** — statistics, experts, the public, the reader, or nobody ("equal weighting does not mean 'no weights'") — **and the effective importances beside them** (Paruolo; Becker).
5. **The aggregation rule and the price list it implies**: for an additive index, the rate at which each part exchanges for each other, in the parts' own units — the refusal test version 1 posed and this chapter keeps as a Nightlife Culture rule: *how many new venues does the measure say compensate for the loss of one forty-year-old institution?* If the display cannot answer, the measure is not ready. (The test is a values test, not a method test: method says the rate exists and must be shown; whether a given rate is acceptable is the studio's judgement, and the test makes the studio state it.)
6. **The rank with its interval**, from a published sensitivity analysis over the defensible alternatives (Saisana; the JRC audits), and the score at no more digits than the inputs support (two effective digits; the ONS).
7. **The validity evidence** under the five headings — content, response process, internal structure, relations to other variables (convergent and discriminant, including against platform coverage), consequences — with reliability stated, including inter-rater reliability wherever a judgement was scored.
8. **The recognition record**: who was asked whether they see themselves in it, what they corrected, and who chose the parts (Sen's public reasoning; the member-check literature's limits).
9. **The audit design**: how it will be known whether the measured thing moved and not only the measure — the independent check Hood found missing — and what the builder will do when the measure starts to be gamed (Campbell's "multiple indicators, all recognized as imperfect").

"On the face" is bounded by Chapter 3 and Chapter 4 together. Nine lines cannot all sit beside every number, and Chapter 4 proposed the resolution: the *fact* that a number is composed, and that its recipe exists, is visible on the face; the recipe is one tap away, and it is a document, not a tooltip. The adjacent domain of machine learning has built three such documents and they are the right models, marked Practice. *Datasheets for datasets*: "every dataset be accompanied with a datasheet that documents its motivation, composition, collection process, recommended uses, and so on", with the insistence that "the process of creating a datasheet is not intended to be automated … our objective of encouraging dataset creators to carefully reflect on the process". *Model cards*: "short documents accompanying trained machine learning models that provide benchmarked evaluation in a variety of conditions, such as across different cultural, demographic, or phenotypic groups", with sections for intended use, factors, metrics, evaluation data, ethical considerations and "Caveats and Recommendations", and the rule that "Quantitative analyses should be disaggregated". The *dataset nutrition label*: "a distilled yet comprehensive overview of dataset 'ingredients'", which "creates an expectation of explanation, which will drive better data collection practices." A card — the recipe in nine lines, in a standard form, one tap from the score — is the dashboard's version of all three; its design is Chapter 11's and the design system's. The datasheet authors' insistence that the document not be automated applies to it: a builder who cannot fill it in has learned something about the measure.

Finally, the studio's own rule that *no composite cultural-value score exists without an adopted method*. This chapter can now say what "adopted" must mean, as a Nightlife Culture rule with the handbook behind it: the ten steps walked and written down; the weights and aggregation chosen as judgements and declared as such; the sensitivity analysis run; the validity evidence gathered under the five headings; the recognition recorded; and the card published. A score published before that is a mashup, in Ravallion's precise sense: a number whose producer "is essentially free to set" its moving parts.

## 13 · What holds true regardless

### The seven

**1 · A composite is a model, and is shown as one.** It is "formed … on the basis of an underlying model" whose construction "owes more to the craftsmanship of the modeller than to universally accepted scientific rules"; three of its six documented risks are conditional on transparency. The construct and the framework come first, or the result is a mashup. *Standard* (the OECD/JRC handbook) and *Evidence* (Ravallion). **Fixed.**

**2 · Weights are judgements, and they are not importance.** "Weights are essentially value judgements"; equal weights "does not mean 'no weights'" and weight uneven groups unequally; nominal weights "are hardly ever found to match" effective ones, and can be "little more than cosmetic". Show both. *Standard* + *Evidence* (Paruolo et al.; Becker et al.; the JRC audits). **Fixed.**

**3 · Aggregation is a price list, and the prices are declared.** Addition lets any strength pay for any weakness — (21,1,1,1) equals (6,6,6,6) — and sets an exchange rate between the parts that can be computed and usually is not ($0.51 against $9,000). The rule is chosen as a judgement about which parts may compensate which, stated in the parts' own units, and answerable to the refusal test. *Standard* + *Evidence* (handbook; Munda & Nardo; Ravallion; HDR 2010; Legatum; IMD) + *Nightlife Culture* (the refusal test). **Fixed.**

**4 · A rank carries its interval, and a score no more digits than its inputs support.** A composite audited over its defensible alternatives "is no longer a magic number"; ranks are reported with confidence intervals and read "not only at face value"; two effective digits unless the inputs justify more. *Evidence* (Saisana et al.; the JRC audits) + *Standard* (ONS; Analysis Function). **Fixed.**

**5 · A measure that is used changes what it measures, so the audit is designed with the measure.** Campbell's law, with the evidence that both things happen at once: the measured thing improves and the numbers are massaged, and three of the four ways a proxy fails need no cheat. The independent check on whether the thing moved is part of the measure's design, not a later repair. *Evidence* (Campbell; Bevan & Hood; Koretz; Kelman & Friedman; Propper; Burgess; Manheim & Garrabrant). **Fixed** as a procedure.

**6 · Validity is of a reading for a use, including its consequences — and recognition is necessary and is not validity.** "It is the interpretations of test scores for proposed uses that are evaluated, not the test itself"; five sources of evidence; reliability first and not last; a consequence counts against validity when it traces to a flaw in the measure. The people measured have the first claim on what counts, and their recognition corrects fact and confirms resonance; it is face validity and credibility, and the measure owes the other four kinds. *Standard* (AERA/APA/NCME 2014) + *Evidence* (Messick; Cronbach & Meehl; Reed 2008; Reed, Dougill & Baker 2008†; Thomas 2017 — reconstructed; Morse et al. 2002) + *Nightlife Culture* (the scene's authority over what counts). **Fixed.**

**7 · The recipe goes on the face.** Nine lines, in a standard card, one tap from any composed number; the fact of composition visible beside it; no composite cultural-value score without the adopted method the card records. *Nightlife Culture* with *Standard* behind it (handbook; UNECE 2025; Code of Practice) and *Practice* for the form (datasheets; model cards; nutrition labels). **Fixed.**

### The record beneath them

- The field's governing text is candid: composites "May disguise serious failings in some dimensions" and "May be misused … to support a desired policy" where construction is not transparent. (OECD/JRC 2008.)
- Normalisation sets the exchange rate as surely as weights do; z-scores let extreme values dominate; min-max lets outliers set the range. (Handbook; Munda & Nardo.)
- Equal weights are the commonest scheme, for five reasons including "alleged objectivity". (Greco et al. 2019.)
- In the Times university ranking as audited, 20% of weight explained 21% of variance and 40% explained 81%; in the Shanghai ranking, 10% and 20% weights mattered almost equally; the 2009 HDI's four parts were "roughly equally important" whatever their weights. (Paruolo et al. 2013.)
- A developer under annual audit now calls its weights "scaling coefficients and not importance coefficients". (GII 2025 audit.)
- The 2010 HDI switched to the geometric mean so that "Poor performance in any dimension is now directly reflected"; the UN's own researchers dispute whether the implied exchange rates are "values". (HDR 2010; Klugman et al.)
- The English deprivation index is "specifically constructed to identify deprivation and not affluence" — a one-tailed instrument; a cultural measure must decide which tail it is for. (IoD 2019.)
- Eleven sustainability indices in policy use were judged "rather useless if not misleading" on their arithmetic. (Böhringer & Jochem 2007.)
- In the 2025 Global Innovation Index, 73% of economies shift fewer than three rank places across 5,000 alternative constructions; seven shift more than twenty. (JRC audit.)
- Goodhart's sentence was a subordinate clause; Strathern's is the aphorism; Hoskin made the bridge; Campbell's is the law with evidence. (Chrystal & Mizen — secondary; Strathern 1997; Campbell 1976.)
- A third of ambulance trusts "corrected" times to under eight minutes; nine trusts adjusted 6,000 waiting-list records; in "over 80%" of first-time validations the NAO found achievements misstated or data weaknesses undisclosed. (Bevan & Hood; PASC 2003.)
- Waiting times fell faster in England than in Scotland and Wales; school effectiveness fell in Wales after league tables were abolished; the target was met only where it carried reputational sanction. (Propper et al.†; Burgess et al.†; Bevan & Hamblin†.)
- High-stakes test gains run "3 to 5 times" the gains on low-stakes audit tests. (Koretz 2005.)
- Messick's two failures — construct underrepresentation and construct-irrelevant variance — are a diagnostic language for any indicator. (Messick 1995.)
- The *Standards* do not mention face validity; "response processes" is the official home of cognitive testing. (AERA/APA/NCME 2014.)
- The ONS measures a felt quality with four cognitively tested questions and refuses to combine them. (ONS.)
- Member checks correct fact and confirm resonance; the evidence that they improve findings is thin; their critics say they "keep the level of analysis inappropriately close to the data". (Birt et al.; Thomas — reconstructed; Morse et al. 2002.)
- Participation's proven effects are acceptance, durability and learning; the one direct test found community indicators mostly valid, "not always sufficiently accurate or reliable", and best combined with field measurement. (Reed 2008; de Vente et al. 2016; Reed, Dougill & Baker 2008.)
- The ACE quality metrics report twelve dimensions from three perspectives as medians and interquartile ranges and are "not a clapometer". (Knell & Whitaker 2016.)
- UNESCO's indicators measure "change over time in the same place, rather than … ranking". (Culture|2030.)
- The one sector composite publishes its parts and not its recipe; its headline is consistent with several recipes and the published method does not say which. (Creative Footprint methodology; 2024 report — method only.)
- No empirical study was found of whether decision-makers use composites more than dashboards; one of the handbook's authors describes their spread as "mostly advocacy-driven". (Saltelli 2007†, a commentary.)

## 14 · Where the choice is yours

**Which form.** Composite, dashboard, profile, user-weighted, longitudinal self-profile, or a hybrid — the UNECE's "side-by-side". The chapter gives what each does; the studio's framework keeps the choice open and this chapter does not close it.

**Which construct, and which parts.** The theoretical framework is the studio's and the scene's. The chapter's rule is only that it is written first and that each part carries Chapter 4's provenance.

**Who sets the weights.** Statistics, experts, the public through a deliberative process, the reader through sliders, or equal weights as a declared null. Each is defensible; each is declared; none is neutral.

**Which aggregation, and which tail.** Additive with its price list shown; geometric; a power mean with a stated exponent; non-compensatory; or none. And whether the instrument is built to find what thrives, what struggles, or both.

**How much of the card on the face.** The fact of composition and the existence of the card, at minimum; which of the nine lines sit beside the number is Chapter 11's layout decision. `[Revisit → Ch 11]`

**How recognition is gathered.** Member checks of fact; a synthesised late check of interpretation; a deliberative choice of parts; or all three — and how its results are recorded on the card.

**How the audit is run.** Which independent measure stands beside the composite to show whether the thing moved; how often; by whom.

**Whether the sector composite's method audit sits here or in Chapter 15.** For Carly.

## 15 · What this means for a nightlife dashboard

An illustration, not a specification, and not a decision about the measure's form. The sector knowledge is Carly's; the method is the chapter's.

Suppose, for the illustration only, that several things about a place's night have been counted at Chapter 4's standard — venues at a stated grain, events in a stated period, attendance against a stated base, a felt quality asked in tested questions — and that someone wants one number. The chapter's first demand is a sentence: what is the construct, and why these parts? If the sentence cannot be written, the number is a mashup and should not be built. If it can, the handbook's steps follow and each is a judgement. The normalisation decides whether a city with one enormous venue is an outlier or the scale; z-scores would let it dominate, ranks would not. The weights are set by someone — and one candidate, on the studio's own position and Sen's argument, is the scene, through a process the chapter has found earns trust when it is well facilitated and loses it when a public body runs it alone. Whoever sets them, the display shows the nominal weights and the effective importances beside them, because the parts of a cultural ecology move together and the weights may be cosmetic.

Then the aggregation, which is where the studio's values become arithmetic. Under addition, a place with one dominant strength and three collapses can equal a place that is sound on everything — (21,1,1,1) and (6,6,6,6) — and the display has claimed they are the same. Version 1's refusal test stands as the studio's values test: the card must say how many new venues the measure thinks compensate for the loss of one forty-year-old institution, in venues, and if the answer is a number the studio would not defend in public, the aggregation does not express the studio's judgement and should change until it does. The deprivation index's one-tailed design poses a question the studio has not yet had to answer: is the measure built to find where nightlife culture thrives, where it is at risk, or both — because the arithmetic differs.

Then the rank, if there is one. It carries its interval from a sensitivity analysis over the other defensible choices, and the display says *between eighth and nineteenth, most likely twelfth* rather than *twelfth*, at two effective digits. Then the validity evidence, gathered under the five headings before anything is published: content (do the parts cover the construct, or is a register of value missing — Messick's underrepresentation?); response process (did people understand the felt-quality questions as meant — the ONS's cognitive testing?); internal structure (do the parts hang together as the framework says?); relations to other variables (does the measure move with longevity, return visits, what people say — and *not* with population size or ticketing-platform coverage?); consequences (what will the scene do once the measure exists?). Then the recognition record, which does two things and not a third: it corrects the facts a scene knows and the display got wrong, and it confirms that the picture resonates; it does not establish the construct, and the card says so.

And then the audit, designed now. Chapter 1 and this chapter agree that the most reliable thing the measure will do is change the scene. The card names the independent check — the measure that is *not* on the dashboard, against which the dashboard's movement will be tested — and names what the studio will do when the first venue arranges itself toward the score. That is where this chapter hands over: to Chapter 6, which asks how value is found in data before any score is built, and to Chapter 10, which owns what the measure may do to the people in it.

## What proves a break works

The Fixed rules above are mostly about what is declared, and the test for breaking one is the same as Chapter 4's: **hand the composed number and whatever accompanies it to someone who was not in the room, and ask them to reconstruct how it was made, what would change it, and what one part is worth in terms of another.** If they can — from the face and one tap — the break has cost nothing. If they cannot say what the weights did, what a strength paid for, or how far the rank could move, the number cannot bear the weight, and the rule was right. For the audit rule the test is harder and later: a year on, can anyone say whether the measured thing moved, from a source that is not the measure? Log the break in the four-line record Chapter 16 will describe (what rule, why, for whom, what proved it).

## Checklist

- [ ] The construct is written in a sentence and the framework connects it to every part
- [ ] Every part carries its unit, period, base, source and status
- [ ] The normalisation is named and its exchange-rate effect noted
- [ ] Who set the weights, and how, is declared; equal weights declared as a judgement; uneven groups checked
- [ ] Effective importances shown beside nominal weights
- [ ] The aggregation rule is chosen and declared as a compensability judgement
- [ ] The price list is published in the parts' own units; the refusal test answered
- [ ] One-tailed or two-tailed, declared
- [ ] Sensitivity analysis run; every rank shown with its interval; most-likely value stated
- [ ] Scores at two effective digits unless justified
- [ ] The independent audit is designed, owned and scheduled before publication
- [ ] Ratchet, threshold and distortion effects anticipated; distributions near thresholds watched
- [ ] Multiple imperfect indicators; no single sanctioned target
- [ ] Validity evidence gathered under all five sources; reliability stated, inter-rater where judged
- [ ] Discriminant validity tested against size and platform coverage
- [ ] Consequences traced and classified
- [ ] Felt-quality questions cognitively tested; presented as a panel unless combination is validated
- [ ] Recognition gathered, recorded, and marked as what it is
- [ ] The people measured chose the parts, or the card says who did
- [ ] Community indicators combined with field measurement
- [ ] The components accompany any composite
- [ ] The card exists, is standard, is one tap away, and the fact of composition is on the face
- [ ] No score published before the method is adopted

## Looking back

Against Chapters 0 to 4, the archived version 1, and the studio's Decision Book.

- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's truth that a published measure changes the behaviour it measures, with Campbell at source, Bevan and Hood's taxonomy and the audited figures — and **counterbalances** it: every rigorous comparison found the measured thing really improved, and three of the four ways a proxy fails need nobody to game anything. Chapter 1's truth should carry both halves. `[Counterbalance → Ch 1]`
- **Strengthens** Chapter 2's finding that the official reader needs the official currency (the Culture and Heritage Capital bank is that currency, built) and that advocates want a single number — while **opening a thread**: the composite's spread is described as advocacy-driven, and the sector's own research council prefers evaluation to measurement. The form question is partly the reader question.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 3's uncertainty rule (a rank carries its interval, countable, with a most-likely value) and its "show the shape" (a profile is addition's hidden imbalance made visible). **Strengthens** Chapter 3's precision rule with the ONS's "two effective digits" — Ehrenberg's rule is now official house style.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 4 throughout: a composite inherits every part's provenance; normalisation is a denominator choice; the funnel and the rank interval are one discipline; discriminant validity against platform coverage is Chapter 4's big-data paradox stated as a test. **Opens a thread** on "reliability", which now has three senses in the book.
- **Weakens** version 1's Chapter 6 in one respect and **strengthens** it in three. Version 1 cited the 20%/21% and 40%/81% figures without naming the index; this chapter finds them in Paruolo et al.'s Table 2 and they are the Times Higher Education ranking's, not the HDI's — the HDI is the well-behaved case in that paper. Version 1's "recipe on the face", its refusal test and its (21,1,1,1) illustration are confirmed at source or at the JRC's reproduction, and the sector composite's figures (8.58, 5.90, 5.41 against 6.10) are confirmed from the 2024 report — with version 1's inference corrected: the headline is not the mean of its parts, but several undisclosed recipes would produce it, so the finding is about disclosure and not arithmetic. Version 1's "intensity not volume" and "never merge identity with conditions" are the one-tailed and non-compensatory questions in this chapter's terms, and survive as Nightlife Culture rules for Chapter 6 and Chapter 9.
- **Opens a thread** for Chapter 0's glossary and legend: "reliability" in three senses; "validity" as of a reading for a use; "recognition" as face validity and credibility, which is neither nothing nor validity; and the fifth kind, *Practice*, used here for the belief that decision-makers prefer composites.
- **The Decision Book.** Chapters 6, 8 and 9 were searched for rules on composites, weights, aggregation and scores. **Silent on all of it.** Its breakout-statistic rule (a mandatory source line) and its precision rule (decimal places are a claim) apply to a composite's score and are consistent with this chapter; nothing in it governs how a score is built or what must accompany one. ## Design system (Style Guide v1.5) and Decision Book — conflict log

**Agrees.** "Every big number carries a source line. A statistic without one is a claim, not a finding" is, for a composed number, the card. "Illustrative or placeholder figures say so, in the chart, every time" covers a score published before its method is adopted: it is illustrative and must say so. "Every comparison between places declares whether the comparability gate was cleared" extends to a comparison of composite scores, which must additionally clear the aggregation gate — same recipe, same price list. "Strong design must never make weak evidence look stronger than it is" is, here, the rank shown without its interval and the score shown to two decimal places.

**Conflicts.** None direct. One tension: the design system's status chips and the Decision Book's tiers mark a claim's firmness, but neither has a component for the firmness of a *composed* number — the width of its rank interval, the gap between its nominal and effective weights. A "robustness" mark for a score, kin to the provenance code, would be the design system's part of truths 2 and 4. `[Counterbalance → design system]`

**Silent on.** Everything in sections 3 to 9 and 12: the ten steps, weights, aggregation, sensitivity, the audit, validity, recognition and the card. The Decision Book's data chapters stop at the chart and the table; the composite between the data and the chart has no chapter. This chapter's rules table is the candidate.
