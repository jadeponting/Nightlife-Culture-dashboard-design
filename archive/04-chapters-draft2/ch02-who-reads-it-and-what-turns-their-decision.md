# Chapter 2 — Who reads it, and what turns their decision

*Dashboard Design · Part 0: Foundations*
*Version 2, draft 2 · 6 October 2026 · revised after a cold read and an independent verification pass (research log, Entry 2)*

---

## Before you start

This is the second chapter of a research book written for Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio led by Carly Heath, about how dashboards work and why. The book exists to support the Vibe Map — a planned public, non-commercial, interactive account of the people, activity, value and support systems that sustain a place's nightlife culture, built first for the West of England. You do not need to have read Chapter 1 to read this one, though it helps to know its working definition: *a dashboard is a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader.* This chapter is about the reader. Where it refers to Carly, it means the studio's founder; where it refers to "the brief", it means her written brief for this book, which lists eight ways a dashboard fails and asks, among other things, whether other sectors articulate their benefit particularly well; where it refers to the Vibe Map, it is illustrating, not specifying. One term from Chapter 1 recurs: the *club flyer*, which that chapter used as the image of a display that turns information into a decision quickly and with pleasure, and which the brief holds up as the standard a nightlife dashboard's interactive layer should reach.

Two conventions, explained fully in Chapter 0. Every rule in this book carries a **kind** — *Standard* (an external rule nobody here wrote), *Evidence* (a named study), *Reading* (our interpretation, open to challenge), *Practice* (a practitioner's framework or craft knowledge, not tested as research — a kind this chapter needs and Chapter 0 will carry from its next revision) or *Nightlife Culture* (a decision or finding of the studio's own) — and a **tier**: *Fixed* is a hard line with a right answer; *Adaptable* is a stated default you may argue with and must log if you leave; *Decorative* is free. Flags in square brackets mark loose ends: `[Further research]`, `[Prototype flag]` (something the Vibe Map can settle with its own data), `[Validate with users]`, `[Revisit at end]`, `[Rich reference]` (worth your own time) and `[Counterbalance → Ch n]` (a finding that pulls against an earlier chapter's, recorded rather than resolved). A dagger (†) after a source means it was read at the authors' published abstract only.

A note on dates. Both of the Treasury's manuals were rewritten this year — the Green Book on 5 February 2026 and the Magenta Book in May 2026 — and the editions most practitioners still have on their shelves are withdrawn. This chapter quotes the current ones.

## The question

**Who reads a dashboard, how does each kind of reader take in evidence, and what moves them from reading to deciding?**

---

## The 60-second version

> There is no reader in general. A policy officer, a councillor, a regeneration team, a developer, a funder, an investor, a trade body and a promoter each make a different decision, are trained or required to accept different evidence, and work to a different clock. The same display will be graded by all of them at once, by rules that contradict each other.
>
> The official reader has a written grammar. In the Treasury's current guidance a proposal is judged on its *social value* — "all the benefits, costs and risks that affect people's wellbeing" — in five cases, with benefits monetised where possible, quantified where not, described in words where neither is feasible; and the government "does not choose projects solely on the basis of which have the highest benefit-cost ratios." The same guidance draws a line that matters more to this book than any other: *monitoring* answers "Did we do what we said we would do? How are our success metrics changing over time?"; *evaluation* answers "Did the intervention work as expected? What was the impact, on who, and why?" A dashboard is monitoring. On every evidence ladder the government and its funders use, a display that shows numbers moving sits on the first or second rung, and the climb to "it worked" needs a comparison the dashboard can host but cannot supply.
>
> What actually moves the reader is better evidenced than most people would like. Across 145 studies, the barriers to evidence use are access and timing; the facilitators are relationships and collaboration; almost all of it is self-reported. At local level, local evidence beats rigorous evidence, and public health officers describe councillors' definition of evidence as what their ward tells them — one director of public health recounts being told, "we'll do what our voters tell us. Welcome to democracy". In Parliament, the credibility of the source is ranked first and nobody sorts research by method; charities out-compete universities because they "understand the timing". A senior government researcher's half-joking hierarchy of what reaches a minister puts research ninth, behind "Cabbies' evidence".
>
> The experiments on persuasion by data are smaller than the folklore. Charts persuade readers who have not made up their mind — about 25 points more of them moved than with a table of the same numbers; for readers already against the message the effect reversed in one study, and the plain table did better. The "trivial graph" that made a claim look scientific did not replicate. Stories move intention and numbers move belief, both weakly, and the celebrated finding that one identifiable victim out-pulls a statistic has shrunk, on review, to a small pooled effect that a preregistered replication could not find at all. Rankings act on the ranked, not the reader. Maps persuade through the denominator — counts or rates — not the map. Quantified uncertainty costs almost no trust and vague hedging does, and authors leave uncertainty off anyway. The best field evidence of a display changing behaviour is a mailed bar chart that cut household electricity use by about 2%.
>
> Place readers have already fought the indicator war this book is walking into. Creative placemaking built a set of "vibrancy indicators" in 2012 and dropped them within two years; the critique published in between rests on three grounds that are general: the concept was fuzzy, the data were proxies at the wrong geography, and the indicators rewarded places that were already winning. Other sectors escaped being read as a cost line by one of two routes — monetise the benefit (parks worth £34.2 billion a year; sport £4.20 for every £1) or refuse the single number and define the construct instead — and every monetised headline carries caveats that the next press release drops. Funders require more evidence than they use; their ladders start with a convincing account; and the strongest academic argument in the field hands the job of proving *impact* to the collective, not the single organisation. Lived experience is admitted as evidence in principle and re-filed as "qualitative data to supplement official evidence" in practice. Recognition — the community sees itself — is a legitimate test of credibility, not of accuracy, and only when it is a dialogue that can change the account.
>
> So the chapter's rule is more than "know your audience". It is: **name each reader, the decision they make, the evidence they are obliged to accept and the clock they keep; choose openly whose grammar the first screen speaks, because it will speak somebody's; and make each of the other readers' rules reachable rather than hidden.** Whether one display can carry that for eight graders is a question this book cannot answer until its later parts have been written, and it is flagged as such.

## 1 · There is no reader in general

Chapter 1 ended on *a named reader*. This chapter begins by naming them, and by noticing that they do not agree about what counts.

Ask a Treasury-trained appraiser what evidence is, and you will be told about options, counterfactuals and social value. Ask a public health officer what councillors mean by it, and — in a study this chapter returns to — you will be told, "what people and wards tell them, because that's what they know to be true." Ask a developer's viability consultant, and evidence is comparable sales values, build costs and a 15–20% return. Ask a foundation programme officer, and it is a theory of change and a conversation. Ask a promoter who has run a room for fifteen years, and it is the room. None of these people is wrong about their own decision. Each of them will open the same dashboard.

The table below is this chapter's spine. It is a *Reading* — a synthesis of what the sources say each reader is trained or required to accept, not a survey of real individuals — and it is deliberately blunt so that it can be argued with. The decisions are the ones the Vibe Map's own brief names: policy and politicians; economic development, placemaking, inward investment and developers; advocates and trade bodies; funders and investors; and the scenes themselves. Researchers are omitted as a reader class, because a research reader grades by the rules of their field, and those rules are the subject of Chapters 4 to 7. Several terms in the table — the five cases, a What Works Centre, section 106, the five dimensions — are explained in the sections that follow; the table is meant to be returned to.

| Reader | The decision they actually make | The evidence they are trained or required to accept | The clock they keep |
|---|---|---|---|
| Policy officer, central or local | Whether to fund, regulate, convene or ignore; what to put in the submission upward | Appraisal logic — options, social value, the five cases, a monitoring and evaluation plan; evidence graded by design where a What Works Centre is involved | The business-case cycle; the spending review; "during implementation is when there is the greatest opportunity for the evaluation to influence decisions" |
| Elected member | Whether to spend political capital; what to say to the ward | Constituents' testimony; local evaluation however small; national guidance discounted as "generic" | Four years, "if that, coming towards elections" |
| Economic development, regeneration, inward investment | Where to put public land, grant and attention; what to say to an investor | Productivity, jobs, business births, skills, house prices — the Centre for Cities' seventeen indicators and the ONS's thirty-eight; nothing cultural | The funding round; the investor visit; the masterplan |
| Developer and their viability consultant | Whether a scheme stacks up; what to concede in a section 106 | Residual arithmetic: gross development value minus costs, land and return; non-market value only if it moves rents or is imposed as a policy cost | The planning application; the lender's covenant |
| Funder and philanthropic investor | Whether to grant; whether to renew; what to report upward | A theory of change; outcomes on a ladder from "a convincing account" to "a comparison group"; increasingly, a conversation | The programme round; the portfolio-balancing meeting |
| Impact and place-based investor | Whether to commit capital; how to classify and report the impact | The five dimensions — what, who, how much, contribution, risk — under a narrative and a theory of change; "stakeholder voice" admitted | The fund's life; the quarterly report |

Two things follow from the table before any evidence is read, and a third has to be admitted. The first is that a single display cannot *persuade* all of these readers, because they are moved by different things; the most it can aim for is to be *legible* to all of them, and even that is an aim rather than a demonstrated possibility, because — as sections 4 and 6 show — the choice of what to count is already made in somebody's grammar. The second is that most of the readers are not the decision's final owner. The officer writes for the minister; the programme officer writes for the board; the viability consultant writes for the lender; the trade body writes for the window. A dashboard that is read by the person in the room is also read, second-hand, by whoever they report to, in a document that strips out the caveats. Section 6 shows how reliably that stripping happens. The admission is that this is a chapter about readers in which no reader was interviewed: the table is built from what the sources say each reader is trained to accept, and the behavioural evidence in section 5 comes from householders, parents, patients and survey panels rather than from officers and promoters. For two rows — developers, and advocates and trade bodies — no study of how they receive evidence was found at all. The table is therefore the first thing to test in conversation with the real readers, not the last. `[Validate with users]`

## 2 · Why people believe what they believe

Four beliefs about what turns a decision circulate wherever dashboards are built. Each is stated fairly here and tested in sections 3 to 9.

### Belief one: good evidence speaks for itself

The founding assumption of "evidence-based policy" is that if the evidence is rigorous enough and clear enough, decisions will follow it. The policy-studies version is the "two communities" picture — researchers on one side, policymakers on the other, and a gap to be bridged by better translation. The dashboard version is that a well-made display *is* the bridge: show the numbers plainly and the reader will act. The belief is attractive because it makes the designer's job the whole job.

### Belief two: a picture persuades better than a number

Everyone who has sat through a budget meeting believes it. The folklore says a chart is read faster, remembered longer and trusted more than a table, and that a map does what a chart cannot. The design consequence is that dashboards are mostly charts, that rankings are mostly league tables and that a map is the first thing most place dashboards show.

### Belief three: a story beats a statistic — and one killer number beats both

Two beliefs that pull in opposite directions and are held simultaneously. The first is the identifiable victim: one named person moves a reader more than a thousand statistical ones, so the dashboard should have a face. The second is the killer stat — the single number (£350 million a week; 97% of scientists) that, once lodged, carries the argument. Both beliefs put the burden on the display to find the right emotional unit.

### Belief four: if it is monetised, it will be funded

The reader at the top of the public sector is assumed to be a benefit-cost ratio, so the sector's job is to convert what it does into pounds — a social return on investment, a wellbeing value, an avoided NHS cost — and the dashboard's job is to show the conversion. Sport, parks and libraries have each done exactly this in the last decade, and the belief is that it worked.

Each of these beliefs is partly true, which is what makes them durable. The sections that follow say which part.

## 3 · How the official reader grades evidence

The most powerful reader of a public-interest dashboard in the United Kingdom is trained on two books. They set out, in plain words, what a government analyst is required to count as evidence and in what order. Neither mentions dashboards. Both describe one without naming it.

### Appraisal: the Green Book, 2026

*Appraisal* is the word for deciding in advance. HM Treasury's *Green Book* — guidance that "is mandatory for UK government departments and arm's length bodies to follow", that "many local authorities in England stipulate in their local assurance frameworks", and that charities "may be required to adhere to … when bidding for central government funding" — defines it as "the process of assessing the costs, benefits and risks of different options for achieving government objectives." Three words in that sentence do work a first-time reader can miss. *Options*: the appraiser is not asked whether a thing is good but which of several things is best, always against a *business as usual* case ("the outcome that is expected if current arrangements continue") and a *do minimum* ("the option that just achieves the proposal's objectives and goes no further"). *Risks*: the appraiser is required to assume the proposer is too hopeful — "Practitioners should explicitly adjust their appraisals for optimism bias. This is the proven tendency for appraisals to be over-optimistic about key assumptions" — and to correct for it by "increasing their estimates of social costs and project durations, and decreasing their estimates of social benefits." And *government objectives*: a proposal is read against what the reader already wants.

What the appraiser is weighing is *social value*. The 2026 text is worth quoting whole, because it is wider than most people expect: "The social value of a proposal is the value it brings to residents of the United Kingdom. This is not limited simply to financial returns, but all the benefits, costs and risks that affect people's wellbeing." The list that follows includes "economic prosperity, justice, security, the climate, the environment, people's health and wellbeing, as well as distributional effects." A dashboard that assumes the Treasury reader only counts money has misread the Treasury.

The proposal is then written as five *cases* — the structure every officer who has drafted a business case will recognise. The *strategic* case "sets out the rationale and objectives for the intervention" (the case for change, a theory of change, objectives that are "specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-limited (SMART)", business as usual, strategic fit); the *economic* case identifies "the preferred option that delivers the best value for money"; the *commercial* case is how the inputs will be sourced; the *financial* case "is concerned with the affordability of the proposal for the public bodies involved"; and the *management* case "sets out the practical arrangements for implementation" and includes a "Monitoring and evaluation plan". The withdrawn 2022 edition put the same five as questions, and they are the clearest form for a non-specialist: "What is the case for change, including the rationale for intervention?"; "What is the net value to society (the social value) of the intervention?"; "Can a realistic and credible commercial deal be struck?"; "What is the impact of the proposal on the public sector budget?"; "Are there realistic and robust delivery plans?" Notice that a dashboard can feed the first and last — the case for change and the monitoring plan — directly, and the economic case only through evidence that is not itself a dashboard.

Then the ladder. "Practitioners should aim to monetise social costs and social benefits where possible. If a social cost or social benefit cannot be monetised, then practitioners should aim to quantify it and express it in numerical terms." Where there is insufficient evidence for a reliable estimate, "practitioners should describe it qualitatively"; "A social cost or social benefit is qualitative if it cannot be feasibly expressed in numerical terms and can only be understood using words." And the escape clause, which is also an instruction: "It may be disproportionate to monetise or quantify every impact." This is the official order of preference — pounds, then numbers, then words — and it is the reason belief four exists. But the same book says: "The UK government does not choose projects solely on the basis of which have the highest benefit-cost ratios". That sentence is there because the Treasury found its readers believed otherwise. Its 2025 *Green Book Review*, which triggered the rewrite, promised that the new edition would say that "a BCR of less than one does not automatically constitute poor value for money" and that "HM Treasury will make clear that the Green Book does not endorse the use of arbitrary 'BCR thresholds'"; and it introduced "place-based business cases" that ask "what is the right project to improve growth in this area?" rather than "what is the best way to undertake this project?"

Two more things in the current edition bear directly on a place dashboard. The 2026 Green Book has a chapter on "Distributional analysis and place-based analysis": "Practitioners should assess how different groups or places are affected by a proposal. In particular, distributional analysis should be used to illustrate the effects of a proposal on those groups with the protected characteristics." *Who* benefits is now a required question, not an optional one. And the book is frank that its own evidence is usually poor: "The evidence that supports appraisal is often incomplete or inconclusive. Appraisal also involves assumptions about the future, which may or may not materialise in reality."

### Wellbeing, and the one official route from feeling to pounds

The 2026 Green Book does not mention the WELLBY or life satisfaction at all; a word search of the full text finds neither. Wellbeing survives in the definition of social value and in a supplementary guidance document from July 2021 that is still listed as live. That guidance is the only official source for the number most social-value practitioners quote. "A WELLBY equates to a one-point change in life satisfaction on a 0-10 scale, per person per year," and "The resulting change in life satisfaction can be converted to a monetary value by multiplying by £13,000 [Low: £10,000, High £16,000]." The life-satisfaction question is the first of the Office for National Statistics' four personal wellbeing questions: "Overall, how satisfied are you with your life nowadays?"

Read the whole guidance and the conversion rate is the least important thing in it. The Treasury grades wellbeing evidence by *causal design*: confidence is "highest" in "estimates from well-designed randomised control trials where wellbeing has been measured" or "where there are naturally occurring conditions that replicate randomisation such as a natural experiment"; there is less confidence in "a one-off cross-sectional analysis of choices which are deliberated" and in "estimates of a change in a global measure such as life satisfaction where the change is marginal"; and "almost no confidence in very small trials on relatively trivial interventions". Wellbeing evidence "where relevant and credible, can provide additional information"; "measurement and valuation should be proportionate"; and ongoing effects must be handled "to avoid double counting". A dashboard that shows a place's average life satisfaction rising a little has produced, in the Treasury's own grading, an estimate of a marginal change in a global measure — the rung marked "less confidence". Chapter 5 takes up what it would take to do better.

### Evaluation: the Magenta Book, 2026, and the sentence this book is built on

*Evaluation* is the word for finding out afterwards. The *Magenta Book* defines it as "a systematic assessment of the design, implementation and outcomes of an intervention", which "involves understanding how an intervention is being or has been implemented and what effects it has, for whom and why." It distinguishes *process* evaluation ("What can be learned from how the intervention was delivered?"), *impact* evaluation ("What difference has an intervention made?" — and "How much of these outcomes can be attributed to the intervention?") and *value for money* evaluation ("Is this intervention a good use of resources?"). The engine of impact evaluation is the *counterfactual*: "what would have happened in the absence of the policy", usually estimated from "an unaffected group" that "acts as a proxy for what would have happened to the affected group in the absence of the intervention". The book is explicit that there is no single best method — "No one evaluation approach can appropriately evaluate all types of intervention" — and admits *theory-based* evaluation, which "draw[s] conclusions about an intervention's impact through rigorous testing of whether the causal chains thought to bring about change are supported by sufficiently strong evidence." Its four quality criteria are *useful*, *credible* ("Transparency is crucial for credibility"), *robust* ("The approach to establishing impact should involve rigorous comparison either in time, between groups or to alternative theories") and *proportionate*.

And then, in a figure near the front, it draws the line. **Monitoring** is "Data collection to answer the questions: Did we do what we said we would do? How are our success metrics changing over time?" **Evaluation** is "Research and analysis to answer the questions: Did the intervention work as expected? What was the impact, on who, and why? Was it cost-effective?" The two are "closely related, and a typical evaluation will rely heavily on monitoring data."

Hold that beside Chapter 1's working definition. A dashboard, in the official reader's grammar, is a monitoring object. It can show, with complete honesty, that a metric moved. It cannot, by itself, say that the thing you did moved it, because that claim needs a counterfactual or a tested causal chain, and neither lives on a screen. This is not a limitation of good dashboards against bad ones; it is what the word means to the reader who holds the money. Everything a dashboard says about *impact* is either borrowed from an evaluation it links to, or is a claim the appraiser is trained to discount. The Magenta Book also says when evidence matters most: "During implementation is when there is the greatest opportunity for the evaluation to influence decisions", and "it is important to be clear from the outset what these questions are, how the findings from them are expected to be used, by whom, and when." Reader, decision, timing — the same three words as Chapter 1, in the Treasury's own hand.

### The ladders, and where a dashboard stands on them

Government's funders and What Works Centres grade evidence on explicit ladders. Three are in daily use and a first-time reader should know them, not because they are right — section 3's last part says why they are contested — but because they are how a dashboard's claims will be scored.

The **Maryland Scientific Methods Scale**, used by the What Works Centre for Local Economic Growth, has five levels. Level 1 is "Either (a) a cross-sectional comparison of treated groups with untreated groups, or (b) a before-and-after comparison of treated group, without an untreated comparison group." Level 3 adds "a comparison group used to provide a counterfactual (e.g. difference in difference)". Level 5 is "reserved for research designs that involve explicit randomisation into treatment and control groups". The Centre's scoring guide says, "We define SMS 3 methods to be the minimum standard for our reviews", because "The credibility with which evaluations establish causality is the criterion on which this review assesses the literature." A dashboard showing a place before and after an intervention is Level 1, and is screened out of the review before it is read.

**Nesta's Standards of Evidence**, written in 2013 and copied by many funders since, say the same thing in plain English. Level 1: "You can describe what you do and why it matters, logically, coherently and convincingly". Level 2: "You capture data that shows positive change, but you cannot confirm you caused this". Level 3: "You can demonstrate causality using a control or comparison group". Levels 4 and 5 are independent replication and the systems to repeat it. A dashboard is Level 2, exactly: data that shows change; no claim of cause. Two things about the Nesta ladder are easy to miss. Its first rung is a *convincing account*, not a number — the most rigour-minded funder ladder in Britain legitimises a well-told description as evidence. And its authors did not think the top was the end: "Evidence may only ever be partial or timebound, and needs to be seen as an ongoing exercise".

The **Education Endowment Foundation's padlocks** are among the most-used evidence summaries in the country and, for a designer, the most instructive of the three, because they show three things side by side: the cost of an approach, the size of its effect ("the number of additional months of progress made, on average") and the strength of the evidence, from one padlock ("Very limited evidence: Between 11 and 39 studies") to five. Padlocks are then *lost* for named threats to security, including that "studies that are delivered by researchers rather than teachers" or "evaluations conducted by organisations connected with the approach" make up a large share. The foundation docks confidence when the people delivering a thing are the people evaluating it. A sector's dashboard about that sector, read by this grader, starts with a padlock missing.

### The ladder's critics, and the hierarchy that reaches a minister

The ladders answer one question — *did it work?* — and the leading British scholars of research use have spent a career pointing out that it is not the only question. Sandra Nutley, Alison Powell and Huw Davies, in a short 2013 paper for the Alliance for Useful Evidence: "Hierarchies based on study design neglect too many important and relevant issues around evidence"; they "tend to underrate the value of good observational studies"; and "What counts as good enough evidence depends on how we envisage it being used." Policymakers, they write, "are interested in persuasive and actionable evidence but these qualities are not necessarily linked to particular study designs." Their conclusion is that "There is no simple answer to the question of what counts as good evidence. It depends on what we want to know, for what purposes, and in what contexts we envisage that evidence being used", and that a range of standards schemes is needed because "one size will not fit all the purposes and perspectives".

The same paper carries, as Box 6, a list. It is attributed to Phil Davies, a former Deputy Chief Social Researcher in government, speaking in 2007, and it is titled "One insider's view of policymakers' hierarchy of evidence":

> 1. Expert evidence (including consultants and think tanks). 2. Opinion–based evidence (including lobbyists/pressure groups). 3. Ideological evidence (party think tanks, manifestos). 4. Media evidence. 5. Internet evidence. 6. Lay evidence (constituents' or citizens' experiences). 7. Street evidence (urban myths, conventional wisdom). 8. Cabbies' evidence. 9. Research evidence.

It is half a joke, made by someone whose own job was the ninth item, and it is the counterweight to the ladders: the methods hierarchy is how evidence is *scored*; this is roughly the order in which it *arrives*. A dashboard that wants to be read by a minister competes for attention with items one to eight, and section 4 is about what wins that competition.

## 4 · What actually moves a decision

Belief one says good evidence speaks for itself. The research on evidence use — a field with its own journals, reviews and a remarkable degree of self-doubt — says otherwise, and says so with unusual consistency.

### What readers say moves them

The largest systematic review, by Kathryn Oliver and colleagues in 2014, covered 145 studies of policymakers' use of evidence. Its result sentence: "The most frequently reported barriers to evidence uptake were poor access to good quality relevant research, and lack of timely research output. The most frequently reported facilitators were collaboration between researchers and policymakers, and improved relationships and skills." The review's Table 1 counts the studies in which each factor was reported: among barriers, availability of and access to research (63 studies), the clarity, relevance and reliability of findings (54) and timing or opportunity (42); among facilitators, access again (65), collaboration (49), clarity and relevance (46), and relationships with policymakers (39) and with researchers (37). The barriers and the facilitators are the same things, present or absent. And "Contact, collaboration and relationships are a major facilitator of evidence use, reported in over two thirds of all studies." The review's own conclusion is that "Timely access to good quality and relevant research evidence, collaborations with policymakers and relationship- and skills-building with policymakers are reported to be the most important factors in influencing the use of evidence."

Read the caveats as carefully as the findings, because the review's authors did. Most included studies "reported perceptions or experiences of respondents … rather than documentary proof or observational results about the use of evidence in policy", and "These were all based on self-reports, however, so given the contentious nature of the topic combined with understandable fear of audit/performance monitoring these results may not be reliable." After a decade of research attention, "there is still a remarkable dearth of reliable empirical evidence about the actual processes and impacts of research and other evidence use in policy." What the field knows about what moves readers, it knows mostly from asking them. That is a weakness the field names, and this chapter inherits it.

### How the reader thinks

Paul Cairney, the British political scientist who has done most to carry policy theory to people who have to use it, describes the reader as working under *bounded rationality* — too much to attend to, too little time — and therefore using two kinds of shortcut: "'rational', pursuing clear goals and prioritising certain sources of information, and 'irrational', drawing on emotions, gut feelings, beliefs and habits to make decisions quickly." Evidence that wants to move such a reader is framed: "Persuasion or 'framing' strategies are effective because they appeal to the emotions and the familiar, combining facts with emotional appeals to prompt lurches of attention; telling simple and easily understood stories which manipulate people's biases, apportioning praise and blame and highlighting the moral and political value of solutions." His practical advice, written with the psychologist Richard Kwiatkowski, has three parts: "do not bombard people with evidence … Synthesise and frame evidence to help you tailor it to the ways in which policymakers demand and understand information"; "find the right time to act"; and "engage with real world policymaking rather than waiting for a 'rational' and orderly process to appear." And a warning Cairney repeats: "the ability of scientists to win the day with evidence should not be exaggerated."

That is a description of a reader with a job, not a cynical one. *Trusted sources* are one of the rational shortcuts: a reader triages by who is speaking before reading what is said — Chapter 1 found that public trust attaches to the body that produces a number rather than to the chart; here is the same fact from the reader's chair. And *timing* is a strategy, not a circumstance. The Oliver review found timing to be the most prominent barrier within its theme; Cairney makes it step two; and the Parliament study below finds it is the thing charities get right and universities get wrong.

### Local beats rigorous

At local level the pattern sharpens. Dylan Kneale and colleagues' 2017 scoping review of evidence use in English local public health — 21 studies — found "Three clear trends in evidence use … (i) the primacy of local evidence, (ii) the important role of local experts in providing evidence and knowledge, and (iii) the high value placed on local evaluation evidence despite the varying methodological rigour." Local evaluation was influential even when "based largely on an anecdote or a very small number of cases"; in one study "almost half of policy actors" called meta-analyses useful and "none reported using this evidence regularly." A member of a health and wellbeing board, quoted in an included study, said more people die of smoking than of anything on the corner, "but nobody complains to me about … smoking … But they will complain about the drug dealers on the corner."

Michael Kelly and colleagues interviewed 31 councillors and officers in four English authorities in 2014. Their quotations carry the finding. A director of public health, on trying to tell an authority what the national evidence says: "They say, well, thank you for your opinion. We'll weigh it up carefully and we'll do what our voters tell us. Welcome to democracy…" A public health officer on what members mean by the word: "What they mean by evidence will be what their people tell them, what people and wards tell them, because that's what they know to be true". A councillor on the other side of the same relationship: "Yes, we do listen to our public health [officers]. And, 95% of the times, we'll listen to them." Another director: "every politician works on an anecdote. We have to use evidence either to support or refute the anecdote. And sometimes you get overruled. If you manage to … ensure the evidence base is followed 75–85% of the time probably in this environment, we're doing pretty well." National guidance was discounted as "applying something blanket … too generic", and the authors' phrase for the whole picture is that "professional expertise finds itself up against democracy." A smaller 2024 study by James Woodall and colleagues in one northern authority adds the clock: "Within local authority we tend to work in four-year cycles really, if that, coming towards elections", and "research is valued in local authorities, but not always privileged".

None of this says local readers are unreasonable. A councillor's evidence *is* their ward; that is what they are for. It says that a dashboard which wants to be read by them must be able to show the ward — the place at the scale the member recognises — and must expect a weak local story to outrank a strong national one. Chapter 12 is about recognising your place; this is its political reason.

### Parliament: source first, method nowhere

The Parliamentary Office of Science and Technology's 2017 study, led by Caroline Kenny, of how research is used in the UK Parliament surveyed or interviewed 157 people, including 36 MPs and 16 peers. "A key finding of this project was the fact that people in Parliament generally did not distinguish between different types of research"; "Parliament was interested in evidence (and knowledge) of all kinds – practical, tacit, empirical and theoretical". When asked what made research useful, "The credibility of the source was ranked as the most important factor", followed by relevance, time constraints and ease of use. Research was used "To substantiate pre-existing views or hypotheses" and "to score political points". The House of Commons Library was used by 78% of MPs and praised because it "broke down [a legislative] Bill into digestible pieces"; academics were criticised as "poorly presented with overly technical jargon"; and a peer explained why charities out-compete universities in the written evidence that committees receive: they "understand the timing and the scheduling and what is going to be helpful". "Research is more useful early on in the inquiry."

Put the ladders of section 3 beside this. The Maryland scale and the Nesta standards are invisible to the elected reader. What that reader checks is who is speaking, whether it is relevant to the thing in front of them this week, and whether it is short.

### Enlightenment, windows and coalitions

Three older pieces of theory explain why the direct, instrumental use of evidence is rare and what the realistic alternative is. All three reached this chapter through secondary sources and are marked accordingly.

Carol Weiss, in 1979, listed seven "meanings" of research use and argued that the most common is *enlightenment* — not a finding that decides a case, but "concepts and theoretical perspectives" that "permeate the policy-making process", research "percolating through informed publics and coming to shape the way in which people think about social issues". The expectation that commissioned research solves a named problem she called "wildly optimistic"; and once positions have hardened, research becomes "grist to the mill" — the *political* model, evidence as ammunition for a side that has already decided. These quotations are relayed by a reference summary (AcaWiki) and a 2012 National Research Council volume; the original article has not yet been read. `[Further research]` The idea is the realistic ambition for a dashboard that wants to change policy: not an input to a decision but a slow reshaping of how a problem is seen.

John Kingdon's *policy window* explains when the reshaping becomes a decision. Problems, policies and politics run as separate streams; occasionally they couple, and "The policy window is an opportunity for advocates of proposals to push their pet solutions, or to push attention to their special problems." The people who do the coupling — policy entrepreneurs — "must be prepared, their pet proposal at the ready, their special problem well-documented, lest the opportunity pass them by." Quoted via a 2022 article by Michael Mintrom and Jacqui True that reproduces Kingdon's page numbers. In Kingdon's terms a dashboard's job is the phrase in the middle: to keep the special problem well-documented between windows, so that when one opens the case is already on the table.

Paul Sabatier's *advocacy coalition framework* explains why evidence rarely changes a reader's mind and sometimes changes their position. A coalition is "people from a variety of positions (elected and agency officials, interest group leaders, researchers) who share a particular belief system … and who show a non-trivial degree of coordinated activity over time". Its beliefs come in layers; the deep and policy cores barely move — changing them is "akin to a religious conversion" — while the *secondary aspects* (how, how much, who delivers) move readily. Learning happens "through the lens of deeply held policy beliefs"; in Sabatier's later work with Hank Jenkins-Smith it is "not a disinterested search for 'truth'" and technical information is used "primarily in an 'advocacy' fashion"; and, in Cairney's gloss, "coalitions learn on their own terms—selecting the information they hold to be most relevant and acceding only to change which does not undermine the coalition's main source of cooperation". Cross-coalition learning, where it happens, happens in professional forums where both sides' analysts accept the same data. For a dashboard that is to be read by people who disagree, that last clause is the design brief, and section 8 returns to it.

### Issue bias, and the audit nobody passes

Justin Parkhurst's *The Politics of Evidence* names a second way evidence goes wrong that has nothing to do with rigour. *Technical bias* is evidence use that "violates principles of scientific best practice". *Issue bias* is "how appeals to evidence can shift political debates to particular questions or marginalise policy-relevant social concerns." A dashboard commits issue bias by construction: it decides which questions are askable, and a question with no panel is a question that is not asked. Parkhurst's remedy is the "good governance of evidence" — "The use of rigorous, systematic and technically valid pieces of evidence within decision-making processes that are representative of, and accountable to, populations served" — which relocates legitimacy from the method to the process. Chapter 10 builds on it.

Finally, a ready-made grid. The Institute for Government's *Evidence Transparency Framework*, now run by Sense about Science, rates a government policy on whether it shows its evidence at each stage of making it — diagnosing the problem, proposing the action, implementing it, and testing and evaluating it — on a four-point scale that runs from no evidence shown, through evidence merely mentioned, to evidence linked and cited, to evidence assessed with its uncertainties acknowledged. When it was applied to six recent policies in 2024, "only three out of the six policies met that transparency test", and the same report found that "74% of British adults think it is important that the government shows all the evidence used to make important policy decisions." A dashboard can be audited against the same stages, and a dashboard that shows how a place is doing and says nothing about how anyone would know whether a policy worked is answering the first stage and leaving the last blank.

### What this section settles

Belief one does not survive. The field cannot show instrumental use of evidence at all, and what it can show is that source and timing outrank method. Evidence does not speak for itself; it is carried — by a credible source, at the right time, through a relationship, in a frame the reader already holds — and the field that studies this knows it mostly from self-report. The realistic ambition is enlightenment: a dashboard that, between policy windows, keeps a problem well-documented and slowly changes how it is seen. The design consequences are that the reader's own ward or patch must be visible, that the source must be checkable, and that release is timed to the reader's calendar.

## 5 · Persuasion by data: what the experiments say

Beliefs two and three are about the display itself — whether a chart, a story, a map or a single number changes what a reader thinks. Unlike the evidence-use literature, this is a field of experiments, so the findings are firmer, and they are specific. Read them with one habit: ask in each case *what* was moved (a belief about the data, an attitude, a stated intention, a behaviour), *for whom*, and *by how much*.

### Charts against tables

The study everyone cites is by Anshul Pandey and colleagues, published in 2014. Seven hundred and twenty people on Amazon Mechanical Turk, 240 per topic, were shown either a chart or a table carrying the same numbers on one of three topics chosen *not* to be inflammatory — corporate tax and jobs, incarceration and crime, video games and youth violence — and asked their attitude before and after on a seven-point scale. Among readers who began neutral or only weakly committed, the chart won clearly: "67.29% participants positively persuaded through charts and 42.68% through tables", a gap of about 25 percentage points, consistent across all three topics. The authors' own conversion of the mean shift is modest — "a potential improvement of 8%, ranging between 3% and 15%" of the scale — but the direction is not in doubt.

Then the reversal. Among readers who began *against* the message, "the table conditions have a higher percentage of participants with positive change than the charts across all three topics": "42.55% participants positively persuaded through charts and 78.72% through tables". The authors are careful: "Tables, on the other hand, seem to outperform charts when the participants have strong initial attitude against the persuasive message; although these results are much more uncertain and need further validation", because the hostile groups were small. Their summary is the sentence to remember: "the persuasive power of data visualization is dependent on the initial attitude of the participant." And they could not say *why* charts worked for the open-minded — "we do not know if the more persuasive effect of charts over tables … is mostly due to having more information available or just because the medium itself (its visual appearance) is more persuasive."

A later study answers that last question against the chart's aura. Tal and Wansink reported in 2014 that adding a trivial graph to an advertisement raised belief that a medication worked — an effect they attributed to "the association of such elements with science". Pierre Dragicevic and Yvonne Jansen tried to reproduce it in four online experiments with 623 participants and could not: "text with chart appeared to be no more persuasive – and sometimes less persuasive – than text alone", while "the chart's contribution to understanding was clearly larger than its contribution to persuasion." (Wansink's wider body of work has since been heavily retracted; this paper's status was not checked here, and its own figures are not reproduced.) Read together with Pandey, the lesson is that charts persuade when they carry information to a reader who is open to it, and not by looking scientific. A third study, by Markant and colleagues in 2023 using scatterplots and vaccine attitudes, confirms the moderator and adds a sobering split: "strong prior attitudes are associated with smaller belief changes when presented with data that contradicts existing views, and … visual uncertainty representations may amplify this effect", and "even when participants' beliefs about correlations shifted their attitudes remained unchanged." People can accept the data and keep the opinion.

Belief two, then, is true for the reader who has not decided and — in one study, in its least secure cells — reversed for the reader who has. The reversal is the most useful finding in this section for a studio whose dashboard will be read by sceptics, and the least settled: it should be treated as a hypothesis to test on real readers before it is treated as a rule. For the officer who already thinks the night time economy is a cost, the handsome chart may read as advocacy and the plain table as evidence. `[Counterbalance → Ch 1 §11; Ch 14]` `[Validate with users]`

### Stories against statistics

The identifiable-victim effect is the most-cited result in the "story beats statistic" literature. Deborah Small, George Loewenstein and Paul Slovic, in 2007, had students donate real money: a named seven-year-old drew an average of $2.83; "statistical victims" — the same need expressed as numbers — drew $1.17. Worse, adding statistics to the story reduced giving to the child without raising giving to the numbers: "statistical information dampens the inclination to give to an identifiable victim." Their conclusion was that "sympathy for identifiable victims diminishes with deliberative thought, but remains consistently low for statistical victims."

The result has not held up well. A 2016 meta-analysis of 41 studies by Seyoung Lee and Thomas Feeley found "an overall significant yet modest IVE (r = .05)" — a correlation of 0.05 is real but very small — concentrated in one configuration: a single photographed child, poverty, little responsibility, a request for money. A pre-registered replication of Small's study with 1,004 participants, by Maier, Wong and Feldman in 2023, found "no empirical support for the identifiable victim effect" and "publication bias in the literature", and proposed that what survives is better called *scope insensitivity*: "rather than providing more to a single identified victim, participants seem to be insensitive to the number of victims affected." (Their donations were hypothetical, which they acknowledge.) An earlier paper by Karen Jenni and Loewenstein had already found that most of the effect was explained by *proportion of the reference group* — saving 25 of 50 moves people more than saving 25 of 50,000 — which is a denominator effect, not a story effect.

So the first half of belief three dissolves into two smaller and more useful claims. People do not scale their response with magnitude, which means a big number on a dashboard does less work than its author thinks. And people respond to fractions of a group they can picture, which means the choice of denominator is a persuasive act every time it is made — a point Chapter 1 made about honesty and this chapter makes about effect.

What about the general claim that narrative persuades more than numbers? A 2015 meta-analysis of fifteen studies in health communication by Simon Zebregs and colleagues splits the difference precisely: "Statistical evidence was found to have a stronger influence than narrative evidence on beliefs and attitude, whereas narrative evidence had a stronger influence on intention." Two of the three pooled effects are borderline. Numbers move what people *believe*; stories move what they *intend to do*; neither wins big. In policy studies, the Narrative Policy Framework defines a policy narrative as "a story with a temporal sequence of events unfolding in a plot that is populated by dramatic moments, symbols, and archetypal characters that culminates in a moral to the story", and its central hypothesis is that "Narratives are theorized to persuade to the extent they comport with that individual's understanding of the world or life experience" and that "The plausibility of a story is conditioned by the extent to which individuals trust the source of the narrative." Note that neither clause is about the story's quality; both are about the reader. The framework's often-quoted line that "narratives are often more persuasive than science" rests, when traced, on a 2007 dissertation and one 1992 study, and should not be leaned on.

In visualisation research specifically, the tested effects of "data storytelling" on attitude are small. Johannes Liem, Charles Perin and Jo Wood showed 600 people a UK immigration flow map with and without a narrative structure and personal stories; both storytelling conditions moved attitude by "a point estimate smaller than 2%", and in the second experiment the structured narrative scored about 10% *higher* on perceived threat — the opposite of what the designers intended. Their conclusion is a sentence every dashboard designer should keep: "What we as a visualization community expect storytelling to afford is yet to be demonstrated empirically". Storytelling does reliably improve *comprehension*: a 2024 study found data-storytelling versions beat conventional charts on accuracy with no time penalty, and "these benefits were not associated with participants' visualisation literacy". Understanding, not persuasion, is the claim the evidence supports.

### Rankings and league tables

Chapter 1 established that rankings are reactive — they change the behaviour of the ranked. This chapter asks whether they move the *reader*. Wendy Espeland and Michael Sauder's study of American law-school rankings, built on 136 interviews, describes what a public ranking does: it "reduce[s] the disparate and variable characteristics that might influence the quality of a law school to a single number", produces "decontextualized, depersonalized numbers that are highly portable", and makes readers treat tiny differences as real. A faculty member at a top law school: "[Rankings] create inequality among schools that are rather hard to distinguish. They create artificial lines that then have the danger of becoming real."

On whether the public *uses* performance rankings, the evidence has been stable for a quarter of a century. Martin Marshall and colleagues' 2000 review in *JAMA*: "Consumers and purchasers rarely search out the information and do not understand or trust it; it has a small, although increasing, impact on their decision making … Hospitals appear to be most responsive to the data." The 2018 Cochrane review of twelve studies covering more than 7,570 providers found "low-certainty evidence that public release of performance data may make little or no difference" to patients' choice of provider, and that "the existing evidence base is inadequate to directly inform policy and practice." The ranking acts on the ranked. The reader, mostly, does not choose by it. (Dr Foster was the company whose annual *Hospital Guide* published English hospital mortality rankings from 2001; a measured effect of that guide on patient choice was searched for and not found, so none is asserted.)

The exception is instructive because of what it turns out to be. Justine Hastings and Jeffrey Weinstein ran a field experiment in Charlotte-Mecklenburg in which thousands of families choosing schools received a one-page sheet, "sorted by the academic ranking of schools (instead of alphabetically)", listing only the schools relevant to their child with test scores, instead of the existing three-page alphabetical spreadsheet required under the No Child Left Behind Act. "We find that receiving information significantly increases the fraction of parents choosing higher-performing schools" — by "5 to 7 percentage points", with chosen-school scores rising "0.05 to 0.10 student-level standard deviations". It is often cited as proof that re-sorting a list into a ranking changes decisions. The authors say something more careful: "we do not find evidence that our simpler one-page format had substantial added impact over the NCLB format", and "most of the effect on choices may come from providing clear statistics at the time of choice, rather than the exact format those statistics appear in." The lesson is not about sorting. It is the same lesson as section 4: clear numbers, in the hands of the person deciding, *at the moment of the decision*. The mechanism they propose is about cost: "If lower-income families face higher costs of gathering and interpreting statistics on academic achievement, they may choose schools based on easier-to-determine characteristics such as proximity". Cheap, timely information changed real decisions; the ranking was incidental.

### Maps

Two large studies from the pandemic year show what a map does and does not do. Claudia Engel, Jonathan Rodden and Marco Tabellini randomly showed 1,751 Georgians a county map of COVID case *counts* or case *rates per 100,000*. "Respondents who saw case rate maps were less likely to perceive COVID as mostly an urban problem and reported higher levels of concerns about the virus" — 44.5% against 53.2% saw it as mostly, or somewhat more, an urban problem — with a policy-support effect about half the size of partisanship and no effect on behaviour. Alistair Thorpe and colleagues showed 2,676 Americans six real published maps and found that map viewers were no better informed, were *more* optimistic that the pandemic would improve, and stated the same intentions as people shown nothing: "Simply providing maps with COVID-19 case information was not necessarily associated with improved public knowledge, risk perception, or reported intent." Together: the persuasive lever in a map is the *normalisation* — the choice between counting things and dividing them by something — and the map as an object does not reliably teach. Chapter 1's rule that size-dependent measures must be scaled and the denominator named is, on this evidence, also a rule about what the reader will believe.

### Uncertainty

Chapter 1 reported, from Anne Marthe van der Bles and colleagues' work, that uncertainty shown as a number does not cost trust while vague hedging does. This chapter adds the scale and the producer's side. Their 2019 review found that range estimates signalled "honesty and competence for sizeable portions of participants across studies (25–49%) but also dishonesty and incompetence for non-negligible minorities (8–20%)", and that showing uncertainty about a number "reduces trust in the number but not necessarily in the messenger", with "much greater reduction in trust in the number for verbal/less precise expressions of uncertainty … than for numerical expressions". John Kerr and colleagues then tested it across twelve countries and 10,519 people: numeric ranges caused only small decreases in trust in the number and none in the source, while "Explicitly acknowledging that uncertainty exists, without any quantification, consistently lowered the perceived trustworthiness" of numbers and sometimes of sources. In a decision setting, Susan Joslyn and Jared LeClerc found that giving road-salting crews an uncertainty forecast "improved decision quality overall and increased trust in the forecast".†

Why, then, is uncertainty so rarely shown? Jessica Hullman asked ninety visualisation authors. "Clear presentation of uncertainty is an exception rather than rule in media articles, data-driven reports, and consumer applications." The reasons: "not wanting to confuse or overwhelm viewers (53/85; 62%)"; "not having access to uncertainty information (40/85; 47%)"; "not knowing how to calculate uncertainty (22/85; 26%)"; and "not wanting to make data seem questionable (15/85; 17%)". Her model of what is going on names the editorial act plainly: visualization authors engage "in a rhetorical process involving editorial decisions to omit, emphasize, and otherwise transform results to guide an audience toward intended interpretations", in which "uncertainty functions as a question or seam in the message that threatens the validity of the signal." Only 17% admit omitting uncertainty to protect the data's authority; 62% say they are protecting the reader from confusion. Hullman's own reading is that both are editorial choices in the same rhetorical process; the trust experiments above suggest the reader did not need protecting.

### Numeracy, and the killer stat

Ellen Peters and colleagues found that more numerate people are "less susceptible to framing effects" and draw "generally stronger or more precise" meaning from numbers, while "The less numerate were influenced more by competing, irrelevant affective considerations."† Dan Kahan and colleagues then showed that on a politically loaded question, numeracy does not help; it polarises. Shown identical data labelled as a skin-cream trial or a gun-control study, "polarization did not abate among subjects highest in numeracy; instead, it increased" — "more numerate subjects would use their quantitative-reasoning capacity selectively to conform their interpretation of the data to the result most consistent with their political outlooks."† For a politically loaded dashboard, the most numerate readers are the ones best equipped to read the table their way. This pairs with Pandey's reversal: the display does not overcome the prior; it is read through it.

On the killer stat, the best-evidenced single number in persuasion research is the sentence "97% of climate scientists have concluded that human-caused global warming is happening". Sander van der Linden, Anthony Leiserowitz and Edward Maibach tested it on 6,301 people in a replication of their "gateway belief" studies. Effects here are given as *d*, the difference between groups measured in standard deviations, where 0.2 is conventionally small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. The one sentence moved perceived consensus by nearly 17 points (d = 0.88, large), belief that warming is happening by d = 0.14, worry by d = 0.11 and support for policy by d = 0.09 — with the consensus effect "stronger for conservatives" and no backfire. That is the honest size of a killer stat: it moves the thing it states a lot, and the thing you wanted a little. The other famous British number, £350 million a week, had reach without evaluated effect: 78% had heard it and 47% thought it true in June 2016, and the UK Statistics Authority's chair later wrote to the Foreign Secretary that repeating it was "a clear misuse of official statistics." Nobody has measured what it did to a vote. What it did to the authority of official statistics is Chapter 1's subject.

### Field effects: what a display has been shown to change

Hunt Allcott's evaluation of OPOWER's Home Energy Reports — a mailed bar chart "comparing their electricity use to that of their neighbors", with smiley faces for the frugal — covered "nearly 600,000 households in treatment and control groups", "one of the largest randomized field experiments in history", and reduced consumption by "1.4 to 3.3%, with an unweighted mean of 2%", concentrated among the heaviest users ("from almost zero for the bottom decile of baseline usage to 6.3% in the top decile"). Oliver James and Alice Moseley sent residents of two English councils absolute and comparative information about their recycling service and found a negativity bias — low performance lowered satisfaction; high performance did not raise it — and no increase in complaint or voice.† And the clinical-dashboard review Chapter 1 relied on found improvement "in contexts where dashboards were easily accessible to clinicians (such as in the form of a screen saver)", while admitting "it remains unclear exactly what characteristics of dashboards are related to improved outcomes."

Two per cent; five to seven points; a tenth of a standard deviation; d = 0.09 on policy. These are real, replicated, large-sample effects of displays on what people do. At the level of the individual reader they are modest; at the scale of 600,000 households a 2% cut in electricity use is a large result, and five to seven points of school choice from one sheet of paper is not nothing. What the field literature does not support is the register in which one chart changed everything. A display's realistic contribution to a decision is a nudge to the persuadable, at the moment of choice, with clear numbers and a fair denominator, resting on the relationships, timing and source credibility of section 4.

### What this section settles

Prior attitude is the master moderator: a dashboard persuades the persuadable. Charts work through the information they carry and may backfire on the hostile. Numbers move belief and stories move intention, both weakly; the big number does not scale and the denominator does the work. Rankings act on the ranked; maps act through normalisation; quantified uncertainty is cheap and vague uncertainty expensive; and authors leave it off anyway. Field effects are real, modest for the individual and large in aggregate. Belief two is a half-truth with a possible reverse; belief three is two small truths carrying a large claim.

## 6 · The place reader, and the indicator war already fought

The readers in the middle rows of section 1's table — economic development, regeneration, inward investment, developers — are the ones a cultural sector most often has to convince, and the ones whose native evidence contains least of what the sector does. This section reads what they already read, then tells the story of the one field that tried to build place indicators for culture and gave up, then looks at how other sectors stopped being a cost line.

### What they already read

The default dashboard of the British inward-investment conversation is the Centre for Cities' annual *Cities Outlook*, which ranks 63 urban areas on "17 indicators covering" population, productivity, business starts and closures, patents, wages, employment, claimants, skills, house prices, affordability, broadband and emissions. Nothing cultural; nothing about public life. The Office for National Statistics' *Subnational Indicators Explorer* tracks 38 indicators against the Levelling Up missions: pay, employment, qualifications, enterprise births, 4G coverage, travel times, school results, healthy life expectancy, and — under "pride in place" — the four personal wellbeing questions plus homicide, smoking and obesity. The official local scorecard operationalises pride in place as how satisfied, worthwhile, happy and anxious people say they are, and how many are murdered or smoke. It has no row for what a placemaker does.

The developer's reader is narrower still. The government's planning practice guidance on *viability* sets out what counts: gross development value, costs "based on evidence which is reflective of local market conditions", a benchmark land value, and a developer return for which "an assumption of 15-20% of gross development value may be considered a suitable return". It is a residual sum — value minus costs minus land minus profit — and non-market value enters it in exactly two ways: when it moves rents, prices or footfall, or when it is imposed as a policy cost. The second route exists because of the Public Services (Social Value) Act 2012, which requires an authority procuring services to "consider … how what is proposed to be procured might improve the economic, social and environmental well-being of the relevant area" — a duty to *consider*, not to measure or to weight. The measurement industry grew in that gap: the National TOMs framework ("Themes, Outcomes and Measures"), produced with "more than 120 organisations", prices social value per unit — pounds per apprentice-week, per volunteer hour — and sums it into a headline; HACT's Social Value Bank offers "a suite of 88 outcomes. Each outcome has a defined financial metric, which incorporates a wellbeing value, a health top up value and, where applicable, an Exchequer value", "Aligned with HM Treasury Green Book methodology". This is what a developer's "social value" line is: a price list.

One counter-dashboard exists and is worth knowing for its refusal. The Thriving Places Index, built by what was then Happy City in Bristol, measures the *conditions* for wellbeing across local conditions, equality and sustainability, chooses indicators for "amenability to local action", and states that it "is not designed to be used purely as a league table" and "creates domains to help prioritise action, not create winners and losers." Whether a reader obeys that instruction is another matter; Chapter 1's evidence on reactivity suggests not.

### The vibrancy indicators: a field tries place indicators for culture and stops

In 2010 Ann Markusen and Anne Gadwa wrote the white paper that named *creative placemaking* for the US National Endowment for the Arts: "partners from public, private, non-profit, and community sectors strategically shape the physical and social character of a neighborhood, town, city, or region around arts and cultural activities." The paper claimed a great deal — creative placemaking "animates public and private spaces, rejuvenates structures and streetscapes, improves local business viability and public safety, and brings diverse people together" — and, on page 16, admitted the problem that would undo its own indicators two years later: it is difficult to "determin[e] the precise impacts of a localized intervention, because so many other things are simultaneously influencing the environment", and "Surprisingly, there are almost no good studies of other types of urban interventions such as stadiums or public housing."

In 2012 the funder consortium ArtPlace announced "Vibrancy Indicators". Gadwa Nicodemus, writing from inside, lists them: "dense populations; high concentrations of employed residents; concentrations of creative workers and firms; concentrations of consumption/socializing-oriented businesses such as restaurants and bars, as well as independent businesses; abundant local job opportunities; walkability; a mix of jobs and residences; and high levels of activity on nights and weekends", with "Indicators related to property-value increases … still in the process of being developed." She noted that "Vibrancy has been dismissed on the grounds that it's clichéd, vague, hard to measure, and elitist", and that the indicators "open the door to contestations over value." A critic in the same year wrote that "creative placemaking still has an outcomes problem", and relayed the NEA's own evaluator saying its language "has been very carefully chosen to avoid suggesting that we are trying to design a way to specifically address the causal relationship."

Then Markusen, the field's founder, wrote the critique. "Fuzzy concepts, proxy data: why indicators would not track creative placemaking success" is the single most useful document this chapter found for a studio about to build a place dashboard, and its three objections are general. `[Rich reference]`

*The concept is fuzzy.* "Terms like 'vitality,' 'vibrancy,' and 'livability' are great examples of fuzzy concepts." "A fuzzy concept is one that means different things to different people, but flourishes precisely because of its imprecision." And the definition problem is not a wording problem: "One person's quality of life can be inimical to others'." Her own example is this book's territory, offered as an illustration of why one reader's value is another's cost — "Take the young live music scene in cities: youth magnet, older resident nightmare."

*The data are proxies at the wrong geography.* "efforts based on fuzzy concepts and indicators designed to rely on data external to the funded projects are bound to disappoint"; "most good secondary data series are not available at spatial scales corresponding to grantees' target places"; "we could not make reliable estimates of artist populations at anything near a neighborhood scale." Indicator by indicator she shows the proxy pointing the wrong way: home ownership does not protect against displacement "because as property values rise, property taxes do as well"; an upscale high-rise district whose residents "commute daily to jobs and nightly to bars and cafes outside the neighborhood" is "Not vibrant at all. But such areas would rank high using this measure"; a mobile-phone activity measure "is also likely to completely miss people attending jazz or classical music concerts, dramas, and religious cultural events where cell phones are turned off"; and "Would a thriving heavily Vietnamese city or suburb be considered non-vibrant because its residents choose to live and build their cultural institutions there?"

*The indicators reward the already-successful.* "Third, and most troubling, funders may begin favoring grants to places that already look good on the indicators." And the applicant's experience: "It could be baffling, even angering, to be served up a book of cooked indicators with very little fit to one's project and aspirations." Her position is not against evidence — "I'll confess I'm an evidence gal … So I embrace the project of evaluation, but am skeptical of relying on indicators for this purpose" — and her alternative is grantee-specific criteria, long horizons and peer learning. On attribution she is final: "In any particular place, changes in the proposed indicators will not be attributable to the creative placemaking intervention alone."

What happened next is the part people forget. ArtPlace's own retrospective records that from 2014, under a new director, it moved "from an economic-development paradigm centered around the Vibrancy Indicators to a more broadly based community-development model that allowed for a wider range of potential outcomes", ending in community-defined success measures. The debate was not won; the indicators were quietly dropped within two years. Meanwhile the more careful framework that preceded them — the Urban Institute's 2006 *Cultural Vitality in Communities* — had already shown the alternative order of operations: define the construct first ("evidence of creating, disseminating, validating, and supporting arts and culture as a dimension of everyday life in communities"), insist on the informal ("quilting bees, amateur bands and musicians, poetry slams"), grade the data by quality, and admit the gaps ("there are no immediately available data to assess all aspects of the phenomena we recommend tracking").

The lesson for a dashboard is a distinction, and it is this chapter's pivot. A place indicator set built from secondary data at council or city scale — which is every one reviewed here — is a perfectly good *description of a place* and fails, on Markusen's three grounds, as *evidence of an intervention*. A dashboard must know which of those it is claiming to be at every panel, and must never let a description be read as an attribution. Chapter 7 is about comparison, time and intervention; this is why it is needed. And "how might one measure a vibe" has just been given its first answer: define the construct before you reach for the proxy, or the proxy will define it for you. `[Revisit → Ch 9]`

### Two routes out of "cost"

The brief asks whether other sectors articulate their benefit particularly well. The answer the sources give is that sectors which were read as a cost line took one of two routes out, and that each route has a price.

**Route one: monetise the benefit.** The method is *wellbeing valuation*, worked out for the Department for Culture, Media and Sport by Daniel Fujiwara, Laura Kudrna and Paul Dolan in 2014: "Rather than relying on preferences to measure welfare, this method uses people's self-reports of their levels of wellbeing", and value is "how much money would be required to keep SWB constant in absence of the good" — SWB being subjective wellbeing, how people say they feel about their lives. Their headline table — arts engagement worth £1,084 per person per year; sport participation £1,127; dance £1,671; frequent library use £1,359 — is the ancestor of nearly every British "value of culture" number since. Their caveats are in the same report: values "should not generally be seen as actual amounts that people would be willing to pay"; "we can never be sure that we have controlled for enough of the differences"; the data are one cross-sectional wave; and in the companion volume, "we cannot fully attribute causality" and "unhealthy people may be more likely to engage in arts".

From there the method travelled. HACT turned it into the housing sector's price list. Fields in Trust applied it to parks in 2018 and produced the cleanest example this chapter found of the route's rhetoric and its cost. The report's frame is explicit — parks "tend to be valued within local budgets according to their maintenance costs rather than their true dividend to local communities" — and its method ran two valuations on the same survey of 4,033 adults. The wellbeing valuation: "An individual would need to be compensated by £974 a year to replace the life satisfaction they would have gained from using their local park or green space", summing to "£34.2 BILLION PER YEAR to the entire UK adult population." The willingness-to-pay valuation: "The Total Economic Value to an individual is £30.24 PER YEAR (£2.52 per month)." The same park, the same people, a thirty-fold spread between methods, and both presented in the same document without comment; and on page 8, "the values elicited in this study cannot be directly translated into cashable benefits". Sport England's social return study put sport's total social value at £71.61 billion, a return of 3.28 to 1 — of which wellbeing was £41.76 billion, 58%; the report called its estimates "conservative" and, by its own account, did not adjust for displacement or for "duration, drop-off and discounting". The 2025 press release, on a later model, said "£4.20 for every £1 spent" and carried no caveat at all. The state then institutionalised the route: the DCMS *Culture and Heritage Capital* programme, Green Book-consistent and modelled on natural capital, exists because without market prices cultural benefits are "implicitly valued at zero, potentially leading to sub-optimal decisions around investments and maintenance." Its architecture — stock of assets, flow of services, benefits valued by stated preference, revealed preference or wellbeing — is one a dashboard could mirror.

The route's price is credibility among the people who can check the arithmetic. Dan Corry, writing for the What Works Centre for Wellbeing: "If, for example, I don't go to the theatre I may do something else that gives me wellbeing nearly as high"; "We also have no idea whether the going to the theatre was what caused wellbeing to rise, or in fact doing any activity with other people would have had a similar impact"; "The monetising wellbeing approach ends up with extraordinarily high values for public and non-market goods"; and "We don't need to monetise to get a long way – and we stay in the realms of believability." A 2023 review of eleven wellbeing-valuation studies of sport, culture and leisure found annual values per person that "ranged between EUR 21,307 … and EUR 1606" across the activities studied, and relayed the methodological warning that "the monetary values will decrease substantially if the causal effect of income on SWB is fully accounted for." And in 2014 the What Works Centre for Local Economic Growth — the body whose job is to tell councils what grows a local economy — reviewed 36 evaluations and told its readers that "The measurable effects of sports and culture projects on a local economy tend to be small and are often zero", that "Sport and cultural projects have intrinsic value as well as promoting health and well-being, cultural enrichment, and prestige and branding", and that "Facilities are more likely to have a positive impact on local property prices." Read in sequence, the wellbeing valuations that followed look like the sector accepting the first sentence and building its case on the second.

**Route two: refuse the single number and define the construct.** The Urban Institute did it in 2006. Arts Council England's *Impact & Insight Toolkit* does it by having organisations "set your own definitions of success by describing your ambitions, and then … measure your progress against your ambitions", rated by audiences and peers against co-created dimensions. The Centre for Cultural Value's evaluation principles put it as a stance — "learning over monitoring and foreground understanding over judgement" — and define *beneficial* evaluation as "aiming for positive and actionable change over empty justification and advocacy". And a review of the economic contribution of libraries commissioned by Arts Council England declined to produce the number it was asked for: studies show "near universal consistency … in reporting positive benefit-cost ratios", yet "we do not know whether this is one-and-a-half, twice or 10 times the cost of provision", and "Attempting to derive a realistic and accurate overall monetary valuation for this is akin to the search for the holy grail. What it does show is that measuring libraries' short term economic impact provides only a very thin, diminished account of their true value." Route two wins the methods appendix and loses the press release.

Did either route get a sector funded? This chapter looked for evidence that the parks, sport or library numbers changed a budget decision and found none either way; belief four is, on the record here, untested rather than confirmed. `[Further research]` What is clear is that a dashboard builder has to know which reader they face, because the two routes are graded by opposite rules. The appraiser's ladder prefers route one and, read in full, discounts it for weak causal design. The community reader and the sector's own scholars prefer route two and are read by the appraiser as "qualitative". The honest position, which the Treasury's own order of preference permits, is to do both and label which is which: monetise where the design can bear it, quantify where it cannot, describe where neither will do — and show the caveats on the face.

### Headline-to-caveat decay, and the contested indicator

Two general findings fall out of the sector stories. The first is *headline-to-caveat decay*, and the Fields in Trust report is its clearest specimen: the same survey, two Treasury-sanctioned methods, £30 a year or £974 a year for the same park, presented side by side without comment, with the caveat that neither is cashable eight pages in and the £34.2 billion on the cover. More generally, every valuation report reviewed here carries its hedges — not cashable, not willingness to pay, correlation not cause, conservative by assumption — and every downstream release drops them. The report has two pages of caveats; the press release has none; the slide in the council chamber has the number. A dashboard inherits those caveats whether or not it displays them, and it is the last place in the chain where they can still be shown. That is a design responsibility, not a footnote.

The second is that *property value is the contested indicator*. It is Markusen's prime example of a measure that reads as success to the investor and as displacement to the resident; it is the one effect the economic-growth evidence centre finds culture and public-realm spending most reliably produce ("Public realm interventions may help create more attractive places to live, but this might lead to higher housing costs and displace existing residents"); and it is the indicator ArtPlace had "still in the process of being developed" when it stopped. The number most likely to move the inward-investment reader is the number most likely to be read as harm by the community reader, and a dashboard that shows it must decide, openly, whose reading it is serving. `[Validate with users]` `[Revisit → Ch 10]`

### Observation as a third way

One placemaking tradition answered "there is no data about people" neither by monetising nor by defining a construct, but by *counting*. The Gehl Institute's *Public Life Data Protocol* is "The very first open data specification for the collection, organization, and sharing of public life data" — "people moving and staying in public space" — built with Copenhagen, San Francisco and Seattle so that "engineers can no longer make the excuse of not having data about people when making decisions about the public realm." Its premise is that cities count traffic, property values and crime, and that information on how people use space is "typically excluded from policy and design decisions." It translated into the engineer's *habit* — counts, like traffic counts — rather than into the economist's currency. The Project for Public Spaces' *Place Diagram* is a practitioner heuristic rather than evidence (four qualities of a good place: accessible, activities, comfort and image, sociable), and the economic-growth evidence centre could find no evaluation evidence for public-realm intervention at all: "The lack of evidence meant we were unable to complete a full evidence review". Observation is a legitimate evidence base for a place dashboard; it is also, in Maryland terms, Level 1. Both things are true.

## 7 · The funder and the investor

Funders and investors are the readers whose evidence demands are most explicit and most often written down, which makes them easy to study and easy to misjudge. The frameworks say one thing; the behaviour says another.

### The grammar

Impact investors share a grammar, agreed through the Impact Management Project and now hosted by Impact Frontiers: five dimensions of impact. *What* — "what outcome the enterprise is contributing to, whether it is positive or negative, and how important the outcome is to the people and communities experiencing it". *Who* — "the people and communities experiencing the outcome across multiple characteristics such as gender, class, race, sexual orientation, and Indigenous status". *How much* — "how many people experienced the outcome, what degree of change they experienced, and how long they experienced the outcome". *Contribution* — "whether an enterprise's efforts resulted in outcomes that were likely better than what likely would have occurred otherwise" (the counterfactual again, in investor's clothes). *Risk* — "the likelihood that impact will be different than expected". IRIS+, the metrics catalogue most funds report against, "translates impact intentions into measurable results" and standardises the *definitions* of metrics and their bundling by theme, not the judgement about which matter. Notice the first dimension: the investor consensus already contains a test of how important an outcome is *to the people experiencing it*. The lived-experience test the policy literature says is missing is written into the investor's grammar.

The place-based version — the UK's Place-Based Impact Investing framework — asks for things in a telling order: "Overall Impact Goal and Narrative" first, then objectives, then a theory of change ("how specific inputs and activities lead to positive change"), then metrics, then reporting, then management; and its reportable data "combine quantitative and qualitative data, including stakeholder voice". A theory of change — "a tool that shows a charity's path from needs to activities to outcomes to impact", in New Philanthropy Capital's definition — had become a funder ask in the UK by 2013 and is framed as the step *before* measurement. The investor's ladder, like Nesta's, starts with an account.

### What funders ask for, and what they do with it

The UK's largest community funder has moved explicitly away from the ladder. The National Lottery Community Fund's evidence principles: "There's no one-size-fits-all kind of evidence"; "Measure what matters to you"; "Different types of evidence are right for different purposes"; evidence must be gathered "in a way that is appropriate, ethical, proportionate and doesn't cause harm"; experts include "people with lived experience, service users, practitioners, academics, other subject matter experts"; the aim is "improving as well as proving", with contribution accepted where attribution is not feasible. Esmée Fairbairn makes the conversation the reporting event: "We don't just want to hear about your successes. We want to know when things don't work out"; reporting "should be easy for you and valuable for both of us. We will only ask for information we need and use"; existing board papers will do; there are "mid-grant and end-of-grant learning conversations". The sector's own *Code of Good Impact Practice* asks organisations, "If appropriate, involve beneficiaries in reviewing and evaluating information and helping you to understand what it means" — beneficiaries as interpreters, with a softener.

Behaviour lags the principles. New Philanthropy Capital's 2012 survey found "75% of charities measure some or all of their work", that the two main drivers were funders' requirements and boards, that only 5% cited improving services as the main reason, and that "charities feel that funders' reporting requirements do not always correspond to their own needs." Its 2013 survey of funders is the clearest answer this chapter found to the question *do funders use what they require?* — "88% think that impact measurement makes charities more effective and 89% believe it makes funders more effective too", yet "less than half of funders are using the data for key elements such as selecting grantees (38%)" or "compiling programme-wide results (42%)", and "nearly two thirds of charities said their funders do not pay for impact measurement, while only a quarter of funders said the same." In the United States the Center for Effective Philanthropy, which runs the grantee surveys, found that only about 60% of grantees report discussing their submitted report with anyone at the funder, and that satisfaction tracks whether the funder discussed "how *your organization* would assess the results of the work". The report's value is the conversation it starts; two in five start none.

### The critics

Alnoor Ebrahim and Kasturi Rangan, in a widely cited 2014 paper, argue that "Not all organizations should measure their long-term impact, defined as lasting changes in the lives of people and their societies", that "Every organization should at least measure and report on its activities and outputs, as these results are largely within its control", and that "Impacts on systemic societal problems are unlikely to be achieved by organizations acting alone; it thus makes more sense for funders rather than operating organizations to take on the challenge of measuring those impacts." The attention to impact "is mainly driven by funders who want to know whether their funds are making a difference or might be better spent elsewhere." Their point is a contingency: the right level of measurement depends on what an organisation controls, and *impact* is measurable at the level that can see the whole system — the funder, the coalition, the place. This is the strongest academic warrant this chapter found for a shared, place- or sector-level evidence base rather than every venue proving its own impact. `[Rich reference]`

Toby Lowe's "paradox of outcomes" — "the more we measure, the less we understand" — argues that "a focus on outcome information distorts both the priorities and practice of organizations who deliver such support, resulting in poorer results for those most in need"†; his later field paper quotes a practitioner, "Everybody manipulates the outcomes it just wouldn't be honest", and proposes that the purpose of performance management in complex systems should be "to increase the adaptive capacity of the complex system under its purview" — building people's capacity to judge, rather than holding them to targets. And Malin Arvidson and colleagues on social return on investment: "The most common reason for charities to undertake an SROI is to attract funding" within "a competitive search for resources"; "Those carrying out SROI can therefore use their discretion to decide which cost to use"; the ratio serves "as a tool to close a credibility gap between the third sector and funders" while lending "unintentional support to a current dominant business-case perspective." Route one, seen from the grantee's side.

### What turns a grant

Arts Council England's own equality analysis of its 2023–26 investment programme describes a two-stage process: applicants are scored on "quality of the applicant's contribution to, and evidence of ability to demonstrate progress against, the Arts Council's Outcomes and Investment Principles", and then a "final balanced portfolio" is built considering "range of artforms and disciplines", "geographical spread" and diverse-led representation. The decision is made twice; a case can score well and lose to a portfolio constraint. That is a decision structure, not an evidence structure, and a dashboard cannot address it except by showing the geography. Panels also reward the known — the pattern sociologists call the Matthew effect, after the gospel line about those who have being given more. A study of fifty years of Flemish arts-grant decisions found "those who are already successful are more likely to receive state support than their less well-known colleagues"†, and a systematic review of grant criteria across twelve studies found peers assessing "the applicant's reputation, past performance, academic background, skills, and personality" among their criteria. On how foundation officers weigh relationships against reports, this chapter found no robust UK study; "relationships beat reports" is well supported by practitioner survey and thinly by peer review, and is marked as such. `[Further research]`

### What this section settles

Belief four — monetise it and it will be funded — is half of a picture. The funder's ladders start with a convincing account and admit lived experience; the investor's grammar asks what matters to the people experiencing the outcome; the Treasury's ladder prefers pounds and then grades them by causal design. Funders require more than they use and increasingly want a conversation. And the job of proving *impact* — as opposed to outputs and outcomes — belongs, on the best argument available, to whoever can see the whole system. For a sector that is many small organisations, that is the case for a shared evidence base, and a dashboard is one of the forms a shared evidence base can take.

## 8 · The advocate and the coalition

An advocate or trade body reads a dashboard for ammunition, and there is no point pretending otherwise. Sabatier's framework, in section 4, says that technical information is used "primarily in an 'advocacy' fashion" and that coalitions "learn on their own terms". The question for this book is not whether that is regrettable but what it means for a display that several coalitions will read.

Three things from the sources. First, a coalition's shared evidence changes *secondary* beliefs — how much, how, who delivers — far more readily than anyone's core; a dashboard that sets out to convert an opponent will fail, and one that sets out to settle the secondary questions across coalitions can succeed. Second, the forum matters as much as the data: cross-coalition learning happens where analysts from both sides accept the same figures, which means the figures must be checkable by the other side's analysts, not only persuasive to one's own. Chapter 1's rule that a dashboard is also a data release is, here, a condition of being believed by people who do not already agree. Third, the government reader's trust runs through people: in an Institute for Government roundtable, "Civil servants often rely on personal relationships with academics. These can be helpful in establishing the trust needed to communicate freely", "Ultimately, ministers set the tone for the standard of evidence expected in submissions – and who is trusted and brought into decision making", and, on evidence as an institutional responsibility, "Everyone owns this issue, and so no one does." The Parliament study's finding that charities understand "the timing and the scheduling and what is going to be helpful" is the same point: the advocates who succeed have a relationship and a calendar.

There is an ethical line in this section, and the sources draw it from both sides. The Centre for Cultural Value asks for "positive and actionable change over empty justification and advocacy". Parkhurst's *issue bias* says the choice of what to count is itself a political act. The UK Statistics Authority's letter about £350 million is the regulator telling an advocate that a true-ish number used for a purpose it cannot bear is "a clear misuse of official statistics". And the headline-to-caveat decay of section 6 is the mechanism by which an honest report becomes a dishonest slide. The distinction this book will carry is between *numbers for understanding* and *numbers for advocacy*: the first are produced with their caveats and released with their data; the second are selected from the first for a room. A sector's dashboard can only be the first kind, because it will be read by the people the second kind is used against. That is a Nightlife Culture position, consistent with the Code of Practice Chapter 1 adopted, and it has a cost: the dashboard will sometimes show a number the sector would rather it did not. Chapter 10 takes up when that cost falls on a community rather than on an argument.

On how British trade bodies' shared evidence is actually received by government, this chapter found no dedicated study and says so rather than stretching Cairney. `[Further research]`

## 9 · The scene as reader

The last row of the table is the one every other row talks about. The people who make the night — artists, DJs, promoters, crews, venue staff — are the subject of most of the data a nightlife dashboard would hold, and they are also readers of it, with a decision of their own: whether to take part, whether to trust it, whether they can see themselves in it. The brief says a dashboard fails "if they do not recognise their place within the front-end interface." This section asks what the evidence says about lived experience as evidence, and whether recognition is a legitimate test.

### Admitted, then re-filed

Lived experience is now named as evidence in principle by nearly every reader in this chapter. The National Lottery Community Fund lists "people with lived experience" among its experts. The investor's first dimension asks "how important the outcome is to the people and communities experiencing it". Parliament's committees, a fellowship study found, show "a growing role of lived experience, an emphasis on diversity of political views and witnesses' representativeness" — and an unresolved lack of "clarity regarding the purpose of 'lived experience'". The theoretical case for treating it as evidence is Jennifer Smith-Merry's: validity in evidence-based policy "is frequently conceptualised in terms of rigour deriving from scientific studies which adhere to highly structured processes", while knowledge from lived experience "is characterised by its tacit nature, unstructure and difficulty in transferring from one actor to another"; her proposal is that "validity should be similarly contextualised, with validity coming not from the rules of policy but from the context in which knowledge is enacted."†

What happens in practice is the finding. Clementine Hill O'Connor and Hayley Bennett studied three Scottish Government cases — a small base, read here through the authors' published summary — and found a People's Panel's reflections treated as "qualitative data to supplement official evidence", Social Security Experience Panels "instrumentalised as user-testing and data collection", and an advisory board framed as engagement: participation "shaped by governance-led processes that channel participation into institutional priorities", valued as "authentic knowledge" and then reshaped through "institutional norms". Lived experience is admitted at the door and re-filed, inside, as a supplement — a footnote to the statistics it was meant to stand beside. A dashboard that collects a scene's testimony and places it in a panel labelled "voices" beside the numbers has done the same thing. The scholarly caution runs the other way too: Ian McIntosh and Sharon Wright warn of "assumed authenticity" and "risks of essentialising" — the fact that someone was there does not make what they say representative, and a dashboard that treats one voice as the scene is making the opposite error.†

### Participation, control and voice

The participatory-evaluation tradition distinguishes two reasons to involve the people a programme is about. In Bradley Cousins and Elizabeth Whitmore's framing, *practical* participatory evaluation is "a practical approach to broadening decision making and problem solving through systematic inquiry", and *transformative* participatory evaluation is about "reallocating power in the production of knowledge and promoting social change"; they differ in "control, level, and range of participation." David Fetterman's *empowerment evaluation* is "the use of evaluation concepts, techniques, and findings to foster improvement and self-determination", with "Community ownership" and "Community knowledge" among its principles. Both are read here through a reference summary rather than the originals. The slogan that travels with this literature, "Nothing About Us Without Us", came into English-language disability activism, James Charlton records, from South Africa in 1993, where two leaders of Disabled People South Africa "had heard [it] used by someone from Eastern Europe"; its "power derives from its location of the source of many types of (disability) oppression and its simultaneous opposition to such oppression in the context of control and voice." Control and voice — not consultation. A dashboard that consults a scene and then decides what to show has met the letter and missed the point.

When does community-produced data become evidence that an official reader will accept? A study of citizen-generated air-quality data by Sachit Mahajan and colleagues is the most concrete answer found: "citizen-generated data is often questioned and challenged by scientists and policymakers"; "data requirements of CS [citizen science] projects don't always match the requirements of policymakers due to very specific project goals"; and success requires that "all stakeholders co-develop the research agendas, the data quality standards, and clearly outline how the data would feed into the decision-making process." The standards and the route to decision are agreed *first*. That is the same sequence Chapter 1 found for boundary objects — the common ground before the screen — arriving from a different literature.

### Is recognition a test?

The brief's failure condition — the scene does not recognise its place — has a methodological name. In qualitative research it is *member checking*: taking the account back to the people it is about and asking whether they recognise it. Yvonna Lincoln and Egon Guba called it "the most crucial technique for establishing credibility", giving participants "the opportunity to correct errors" — a line this chapter has only through a secondary source and which is on the verification list. `[Further research]` Linda Birt and colleagues then asked whether it is "a tool to enhance trustworthiness or merely a nod to validation", and found it is often the latter: a line in a methods section, a yes/no sign-off; their alternative, *synthesised member checking*, gives participants "the opportunity to engage with, and add to, interview and interpreted data" — a dialogue that can change the account, not a confirmation of it.†

So the answer the sources support is precise. Recognition is a legitimate test of *credibility* — whether the account is trustworthy to the people it describes — and not of *accuracy* — whether the numbers are right, which is tested by the methods of Chapters 4 to 7. And it is a test only when the recognising can change the thing recognised. A scene shown a finished dashboard and asked "do you see yourself?" is being offered a nod. A scene whose answer alters what the next release shows is being offered member checking. The brief's failure condition is therefore a design *sequence*, not a design *feature*: the check happens before the release, and the route from "no" to a change is part of the dashboard. `[Validate with users]` `[Prototype flag]`

## 10 · What holds true regardless

The candidate canonical truths this chapter contributes, each with its kind and firmness. Later chapters test them; the closing chapter confirms or demotes them.

1. **There is no reader in general.** Each reader makes a different decision, is trained or required to accept different evidence, and keeps a different clock; the same display is graded by all of them at once, and the first screen is always in somebody's grammar. *Reading of the whole chapter. Fixed as a stance; the design consequence — name each reader, decision, evidence rule and clock, and choose openly whose grammar comes first — is Fixed.*
2. **To the official reader a dashboard is monitoring, not evaluation, and on the evidence ladders in common use it stands alone on the first or second rung.** It can show that a metric moved; it cannot by itself show that the intervention moved it; the climb needs a comparison it can host but not supply. *Standard (Magenta Book 2026; Maryland SMS; Nesta; EEF). Fixed.*
3. **The benefit-cost ratio is not the decision; the Treasury's own order is pounds, then numbers, then words; and who benefits is now a required question.** *Standard (Green Book 2026; Green Book Review 2025). Fixed.*
4. **Wellbeing becomes pounds by one official route, and that route is graded by causal design.** A marginal change in a place's average life satisfaction is the rung marked "less confidence". *Standard (Wellbeing Guidance 2021). Fixed.*
5. **What readers say moves them is access, timeliness, relevance and relationship, not rigour — and the field knows this mostly from self-report.** *Evidence (Oliver et al.; 145 studies). Fixed as a statement of the record.*
6. **At local level, local beats rigorous, and an elected member's evidence is their ward.** *Evidence (Kneale; Kelly; Woodall). Fixed as a finding to design for.*
7. **Readers triage by the credibility of the source and do not sort research by method.** *Evidence (Kenny et al., n = 157). Fixed.*
8. **Charts persuade the undecided through the information they carry, not by looking scientific; in one study they did worse than a table with readers already opposed.** *Evidence (Pandey; Dragicevic & Jansen†; Markant†). The first clause Fixed as record; the reversal Adaptable — a hypothesis to test, not yet a rule.*
9. **Numbers move belief; stories move intention; both weakly. The identifiable victim is not safe; what survives is that people do not scale with magnitude and do respond to a fraction of a group they can picture.** *Evidence (Zebregs; Lee & Feeley†; Maier et al.; Jenni & Loewenstein). Fixed as a statement of the record.*
10. **Rankings act on the ranked, not the reader.** *Evidence (Espeland & Sauder; Marshall†; Cochrane 2018). Fixed.*
11. **The persuasive lever in a map is the denominator; the map itself did not reliably teach.** *Evidence (Engel et al.; Thorpe et al. — two large US studies of static maps). Fixed as record; as a rule, Adaptable until tested on interactive maps.*
12. **Quantified uncertainty is cheap and vague uncertainty expensive, at scale; authors omit it anyway, for reasons their own model calls editorial.** *Evidence (Kerr et al., n = 10,519; Hullman). Fixed — strengthens Chapter 1's truth 8.*
13. **A display's effect on behaviour is real: modest for the individual reader, large in aggregate, and never the whole decision.** Two per cent; five to seven points; d = 0.09 — and the clearest field result credits clear statistics at the time of choice, not the format. *Evidence (Allcott; Hastings & Weinstein; van der Linden et al.). Fixed as a statement of the record.*
14. **A place indicator built from secondary data is a description of a place and not evidence of an intervention — the concept is fuzzy, the data are proxies at the wrong geography, the indicator rewards the already-successful — so the construct is defined before the proxy is chosen.** *Reading (Markusen; Jackson et al.), with ArtPlace's abandonment as corroboration. Fixed.*
15. **Every valuation headline carries caveats that the next release drops; the same survey can value the same good thirty-fold apart by method; the dashboard is the last place in the chain the caveats can still be shown.** *Reading (Fields in Trust; Sport England; Fujiwara). Fixed as a design responsibility.*
16. **Property value is the contested indicator: success to the investor, displacement to the resident.** *Reading (Markusen; What Works Growth). Fixed as a design risk.*
17. **Funders require more evidence than they use; their ladders start with a convincing account; the conversation is the reporting event.** *Evidence (NPC 2012, 2013; CEP) and Practice (TNLCF; Esmée; Nesta). Fixed as a statement of the record.*
18. **Impact is measured at the level that can see the system; single organisations measure outputs and outcomes.** *Reading of Ebrahim & Rangan. Adaptable — a strong default for a sector of small organisations.*
19. **Coalitions learn on their own terms; evidence moves secondary beliefs; cross-coalition agreement needs figures the other side's analysts can check.** *Reading (Sabatier via Cairney). Fixed as a stance; Chapter 1's data-release rule is its condition.*
20. **Lived experience is admitted in principle and re-filed as a supplement in practice; its standing is earned when standards and the route to decision are agreed first.** *Evidence (Hill O'Connor & Bennett — three cases; Mahajan et al.) and Reading (Smith-Merry†). Fixed as a risk to design against.*
21. **Recognition is a test of credibility, not accuracy, and only when it is a dialogue that can change the account.** *Reading (Lincoln & Guba, secondary; Birt et al.†). Fixed as sequence.*

## 11 · Where the choice is yours

**Which reader comes first.** The table in section 1 has eight rows and a first screen has one. Whose grammar does the dashboard speak natively, with the others reached through layers and translations? The chapter's own sources say this cannot be dodged: the choice of what to count is made in somebody's grammar (Parkhurst), and the other readers read through it (Sabatier; Kahan). The evidence says the official reader's grammar (monitoring, social value, the ladder) is the one that will be used to discount the others, and the scene's is the one that will be used to judge whether the thing is real. That is a trade, and it is a Nightlife Culture decision; what is Fixed is that it is made and shown, not hidden under a claim of neutrality. *Adaptable in the choice; Fixed in declaring it.*

**Monetise, quantify or describe.** The Treasury's order permits all three. Route one wins rooms and loses credibility among people who check; route two wins the methods appendix and loses the press release. Doing both and labelling which is which is this chapter's default. *Adaptable; the labelling is Fixed.*

**How much to frame for the hostile reader.** One study says the table; another says the numerate hostile reader will read it their way regardless. Whether a dashboard offers a plain-table view as a first-class citizen, and for whom, is a design choice with a thin evidential basis that the prototype can thicken. *Adaptable.*

**Where lived experience sits.** In a panel beside the numbers (the re-filing), woven through the account as its frame (the harder design), or as the route by which the account can be changed (member checking as a feature). *Adaptable in form; the dialogue that can change the account is Fixed.*

**Which number to show the investor.** Property value, footfall and business births move the inward-investment reader and read as displacement to the resident. Whether to show them, and with what framing and company, is a choice to be made openly. *Adaptable; showing the choice is Fixed.*

**Whether to keep the problem documented between windows.** Kingdon's "special problem well-documented" implies a dashboard that is maintained when nobody is asking, which is a cost. Whether that is the dashboard's job or a report's is open. *Adaptable.*

## 12 · What this means for a nightlife dashboard

*An illustration, not a specification.*

The Vibe Map's brief names three groups who will test it — policymakers and politicians; advocates and trade bodies; funders and investors — with the people who make the night as co-developers. Read through this chapter, those are not three audiences but at least seven graders, and the first thing the illustration does is refuse to collapse them — while admitting that the first screen will be in one grader's grammar, and that choosing which is the studio's decision to make in the open.

The officer will read any panel showing a measure moving over time as *monitoring*, and will be right. The honest form of a nightlife dashboard, to that reader, is a well-documented account of what happens in a place's night time culture — the Magenta Book's "How are our success metrics changing over time?" — that links, where an evaluation exists, to the evaluation, and never implies a counterfactual it does not have. That is not a weakness to be designed around; it is the only claim the official grammar lets a dashboard make, and made cleanly it is already more than most sectors offer. Where the sector wants to say *we caused this*, the dashboard is the place to host the comparison, not to assert it. `[Prototype flag]`

The councillor will look for the ward. A map that cannot show the member's own patch at the scale they recognise will be discounted in favour of what the ward told them last Thursday, and the evidence says that is a rational thing for a councillor to do. The place reader will look for the seventeen indicators they already have and find none of them; the dashboard's job with that reader is translation — not into their currency, which the wellbeing valuations show is a route with a price, but into their *habit*, as Gehl did: counts of people, as ordinary as traffic counts, at the geography they plan at. The developer will look for anything that moves gross development value or costs them money, and the dashboard must decide, openly, whether it is willing to show property value and in what company.

The funder will look for the account first — Nesta's Level 1, the narrative that opens the place-based framework — and will know perfectly well that the numbers behind it are Level 2. A sector of small organisations cannot each prove impact; the chapter's best argument says the place can. A nightlife dashboard is, in Ebrahim and Rangan's terms, a candidate for the shared evidence base that measures at the level that can see the system — which is the strongest reason it should exist and the strongest constraint on what it claims.

The advocate will take numbers from it into rooms; the dashboard must therefore release them with their caveats and their data, so that the other coalition's analyst can check them and the UK Statistics Authority never has reason to write a letter. And the scene will ask whether it recognises itself, which is the one test on this list that cannot be passed after the fact: the recognising has to happen before release, and the route from "that's not us" to a changed next release has to be part of the thing. The brief's months of convening are, in this chapter's terms as in Chapter 1's, the method.

Three warnings travel with the illustration. The handsome chart may read as advocacy to exactly the reader the sector most needs to move; a plain-table view is a persuasion strategy with one study behind it, and the Vibe Map's first readers are the place to find out whether it holds. A "voices" panel is the re-filing of lived experience the Scottish cases describe, and the brief's recognition test asks for more. And the single killer number — the night time economy is worth £X — moves the thing it states a lot and the policy a little, and arrives in the council chamber with its caveats already gone; the dashboard that produced it is the last place they could have been kept.

## What proves a break works

If you must break a Fixed rule above — imply an effect without a comparison, show a monetised headline without its caveats, present a ranking as advice, show property value without saying whose reading it serves — the test is the same: **find the reader who grades by the opposite rule and show them.** Give the impact claim to a What Works analyst and ask what level it is. Give the £-headline to the person who wrote the report it came from and ask if they recognise their caveats. Give the property-value panel to a resident of the street and ask what it means to them. Give the "voices" panel to the people quoted in it and ask whether they could change it. If the break survives that, log it in the four-line record Chapter 16 describes (what rule, why, for whom, what proved it). If it does not, it was a reader you forgot.

## Looking back

Against Chapter 1 and Chapter 0, and the archived version 1.

- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's truth 5 (the proxy moves before the goal): the Magenta Book's own definition of monitoring — "Did we do what we said we would do? How are our success metrics changing over time?" — is the proxy's question, and the Treasury places the goal's question ("What was the impact, on who, and why?") in a different activity. Nesta's Level 2 says the same in a funder's voice.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's truth 7 (trust attaches to the producer): Parliament ranks source credibility first; civil servants "rely on personal relationships"; ministers "set the tone … and who is trusted".
- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's truth 8 (numeric uncertainty costs no trust) with Kerr's twelve-country sample and adds the producer's side (Hullman). **Opens a thread:** Markant's finding that "visual uncertainty representations may amplify" the resistance of strong priors — for the hostile reader, shown uncertainty may be read as weakness. `[Counterbalance → Ch 3]` where uncertainty display is a canonical truth.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's truth 11 (rankings are screens, not verdicts) and truth 6 (reactivity): the ranking acts on the ranked (Espeland & Sauder) and barely on the reader (Cochrane). Hastings & Weinstein, properly read, is not a counter-example; the authors attribute the effect to clear statistics at the time of choice, not to the sort.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's truth 15 (a boundary object is a social achievement) from two new directions: Sabatier's cross-coalition forums where both sides accept the data, and Mahajan's co-agreed standards and route to decision.
- **Strengthens** Chapter 1's truth 16 (a dashboard that is not a data release is a wall): the Evidence Transparency Framework and the 74%; and section 8's finding that checkability by the other coalition is the condition of being believed.
- **Counterbalances** Chapter 1's section 11, where the club flyer appears as the thing a dashboard might learn speed from: Pandey's reversal says that for the reader already opposed, the handsome persuasive object does worse than the plain table. The flyer persuades the persuadable. `[Counterbalance → Ch 1 §11; Ch 14]`
- **Counterbalances** Chapter 1's truth 2 as it was phrased ("a display built for nobody serves nobody"): the harder finding is that a display built for *everybody* is graded by contradictory rules, and that there is no neutral layer under the grammars — the choice of panels is itself issue bias. So "name the reader" must become "name the readers and their rules, and say whose rules the first screen keeps", which is what this chapter's first rule does.
- **Opens a thread** for Chapter 1's truth 10 (a place-level figure can be information about the place): Markusen's third failure mode — indicators reward the already-successful — is a reactivity of place indicators that Chapter 1 described for institutions and not for places.
- **Chapter 0.** The case for a fifth kind, *Practice*, is now overwhelming: this chapter leans on the Treasury books (Standard), the research (Evidence), its own synthesis (Reading), and a dozen practitioner frameworks — Gehl, PPS, TOMs, HACT, IRIS+, the five dimensions, Nesta's standards, EEF's padlocks — that are none of those. It uses the kind in its rules table before Chapter 0 has been revised to carry it; the revision is next.
- **Archived version 1.** Version 1's leverage-points answer (given in conversation in October, not in a chapter) treated the officer who writes the submission as the customer and a defensible number as the need. This chapter sharpens both: the officer needs a monitoring claim they can label truthfully and an evaluation they can link to; a defensible number is one that survives the other coalition's analyst. It **weakens** version 1's implicit assumption that a strong value headline would move the economic-development reader: route one has a credibility price, and the growth evidence centre itself told that reader to stop expecting growth effects.

## Design system (Style Guide v1.5) — conflict log

**Agrees.** "Strong design must never make weak evidence look stronger than it is" is, in this chapter, the Dragicevic & Jansen finding and the Hullman finding made into a rule. The guide's comparability gate is the denominator rule of section 5. Provenance on every chart is the "checkable source first" rule of section 4. The guide's instruction never to show RAG tags publicly is consistent with the headline-to-caveat finding: a traffic light is a caveat-free headline.

**Conflicts.** One possible. The guide's register — joy first, then value, evidence, action — is a persuasion order, and section 5 reports one study in which the reader already opposed was better served by the plain table than by the handsome object. This is not a conflict in the design system's own domain (the public, the scene, the sympathetic reader) but it is a conflict for the officer who arrives hostile. The resolution this chapter proposes is not to change the register but to make a plain view a first-class route. Logged for Carly; the design system may want a line on it. `[Counterbalance → design system]`

**Silent on.** The monitoring/evaluation label; the evidence-ladder level; the display of valuation caveats; the recognition sequence; the plain-table route. All candidates for the design system's dashboard section when it exists.
