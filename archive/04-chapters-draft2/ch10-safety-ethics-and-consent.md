## Before you start

This is the tenth chapter of a research book written for Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio led by Carly Heath, about how dashboards work and why. The book exists to support the Vibe Map — a planned public, non-commercial, interactive account of the people, activity, value and support systems that sustain a place's nightlife culture, built first for the West of England. You do not need to have read Chapters 1 to 9 to read this one. Several things from the earlier chapters recur, among them: the working definition — *a dashboard is a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader*; Chapter 4's six kinds of nothing, one of which is a cell withheld for confidentiality and one of which — "community-held" — the book carried forward to this chapter because no official vocabulary has it; Chapter 6's rule that the identifying threshold is set by the scene's own knowability, above any national rule; Chapter 8's finding that every live source is a dependency on someone else's terms; and Chapter 9's measure record, whose last line — *what this changed* — outlives any consent that was given at the start, and whose floor of fifty answers pulled against the brief's rule that a dashboard fails "if its an aggregation of 'the biggest'". Where this chapter refers to Carly, it means the studio's founder; where it refers to the Vibe Map, it is illustrating, not specifying. The studio's rulebook for design decisions is the Decision Book; its visual rules are in a design system (Style Guide v1.5).

Two conventions, explained fully in Chapter 0. Every rule carries a **kind** — *Standard*, *Evidence*, *Reading*, *Practice* or *Nightlife Culture* — and a **tier**: *Fixed*, *Adaptable* or *Decorative*; "Fixed as a stance" marks a rule the studio adopts whose source is a reading or a practice, and "Fixed as a procedure" fixes the steps and not the parameter. This chapter adds two sub-kinds Chapter 9 proposed: *Standard (law)* for the text of a statute or regulation, and *Standard (regulator)* for a regulator's guidance, which is not law unless it is a statutory code and is said to be. Flags in square brackets mark loose ends: `[Further research]`, `[Prototype flag]`, `[Validate with users]`, `[Revisit → Ch n]`, `[Revisit at end]`, `[Rich reference]` and `[Counterbalance → Ch n]`. A dagger (†) marks a source read at the authors' abstract or summary only; "secondary" marks a quotation reached through another named document; "(read once)" marks a quotation returned on a single reading and not confirmed by a second. The *symmetry test* (Chapter 0) means that every standard this chapter applies to others' methods it applies, in section 18, to the studio's own.

Because this chapter is about law as well as method, it says plainly what it is and is not. It reports what statutes, regulators, standards bodies and studies say, with the wording and the date, and it does not give legal advice; nothing in it is a substitute for the studio's own counsel on its own facts. The law it reports moved under the chapter's feet: the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 was brought fully into force for data protection on 5 February 2026 and reorganised the research provisions the brief expected to find; the office of Information Commissioner was abolished and its functions transferred to an Information Commission on 30 September 2026, six days before this chapter's sources were read; and most of the regulator's guidance pages carry a notice that they are under review. The chapter therefore cites every statutory text as read on legislation.gov.uk on 6 October 2026, with its in-force date, and every regulator page with the date shown on it. Where the brief's expected map was out of date, the chapter says so rather than quietly correcting it, because the gap between what a careful reader expected and what the law now says is itself a finding about how fast this ground moves.

Because the chapter uses words that have one meaning in statute and another in ethics, and a third in the studio's own vocabulary, it fixes each to one sense. *Consent* is used only in its statutory sense — a "freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous indication of the data subject's wishes" — and only of a person; where the ethics literature speaks of what a community can give, the chapter uses the literature's own words, *agreement* and *consultation*, and section 11 says why the three are kept apart. *Disclosure* means statistical disclosure — learning who someone is (*identity disclosure*), learning something about someone already identified (*attribute disclosure*), or learning something about a group (*group disclosure*) — and never means telling people things; for that the chapter says *publish*, *state* or *tell*. A *release model* is the regulator's phrase for how far data goes: to the public, to defined groups, or nowhere outside the organisation. A cell is *withheld* when a disclosure rule removes it from a view; a layer is *held* when the studio and the people it describes decide that it exists, is kept and is not shown. There are two *floors*, and the chapter qualifies the word every time: the *disclosure floor* is the minimum count below which a cell is withheld, which Chapter 6 called the identifying threshold; the *precision floor* is Chapter 9's fifty answers, which is a rule about reliability and not about identification. A *row* is a microdata record; the word *record* is kept for the documents this book leaves beneath a dashboard. A *view* is a panel in one filter state; a *query* is the request that produces it. The *motivated intruder* is the regulator's legal test of whether information is identifiable; the *friendly intruder* is the Office for National Statistics' test procedure, a person asked to try. A *group* is people who share an attribute, including a group assembled by an algorithm; a *community* is people with relationships, a voice and some capacity to decide; a *scene* is the nightlife word for a community, used where the chapter speaks of the studio's own case — its positions, its rules, its illustration — and not in the general argument. *Harm* has the kinds Citron and Solove list and three objects — persons, groups and places. *Public* is qualified wherever it occurs — "in public" against "being public", "public release", "public interest", "the public good", "publicly available" — because the arguments in this chapter turn on which is meant. *Anonymous information* is a statutory status; *anonymisation* is the process that aims at it; *effective anonymisation* is the regulator's term for the measures that reach the legal threshold; *pseudonymised* data is personal data still. *Perturbation* is the general word for a deliberate small change to a published count; *noise* appears only inside quotations about differential privacy. *Moderation* is Grimmelmann's "governance mechanisms that structure participation in a community to facilitate cooperation and prevent abuse". The *brief* is Carly's written commission for this book; the *house framework* is the studio's own Vibe Setting Framework, unpublished and confidential, which this book cites and never reproduces. A few of the book's own working words recur: the *face* of a panel is its visible surface; a layer is *lit* when it is shown at its own resolution; the *money view* is the house's name for the panels that put a value on a scene; the *small room* is the brief's case of a place too small to pass a floor; the *table twin* is Chapter 8's rule that every map has a table saying the same thing; a *cell key* is the census method that makes the same cell perturb the same way in every table; a *legitimate interests assessment* (LIA) is the regulator's record for the three-part test; *RAS purposes* is the statute's name for research, archiving and statistics. Where "the fetch tool's reader" is mentioned, it means the text-only reader this book's research used to open pages.

## The question

**How does a public dashboard tell the story of the communities in its data without exposing or exploiting them — what do the disclosure methods do and cost; what does UK law require of a private platform; what do consent, withdrawal and voice mean when the unit is a community and not a person; what is the harm a map or a figure can do to a place; how are hostile contributors met; what do the justice frames add — and where are the lines that are not crossed?** The book does not decide this for any scene. It sets out the methods, the law as it stood on the date it was read, the precedents for holding what is known, and the harms that have been measured, so that the prototype can draw its lines knowing what each costs and whom each protects.

## The 60-second version

> The brief's phrase has two verbs. *Exposing* is a problem of disclosure, and its methods are old and well measured; *exploiting* is a problem of consent, benefit and voice, and the law has an answer for persons and none for communities. A dashboard sits exactly where the two meet, because what it publishes is aggregate — which the law treats as outside data protection once it is "aggregate data that is not personal data" — and what it is about is a community, which data-protection law does not address and which equality law and fairness reach only in part.
>
> On disclosure, four things hold. Whether data is anonymous "is not a property of the data" but of the data in its environment, and a public release is the environment in which "you lose control of the data"; the regulator's test is a *motivated intruder* who "is reasonably competent", has "access to appropriate resources" and may be moved by "curiosity (eg a local person's desire to find out who has been involved in an incident shown on a crime map)", and "Identifiability risk can arise where one person or group knows a great deal about another person" — which is Chapter 6's reasoning that a community's own regulars could name the venues behind a count of three. Aggregation protects a person only as far as the aggregate does: a table whose cells are all large can still tell an intruder what everyone in a cell has in common ("so Alice concludes that Bob has cancer"), and the strongest formal guarantee, differential privacy, promises only that "It is the conclusions reached in the study that affect the smoker, not his presence or absence in the data set" — it does not protect the group. A filter is a table builder: the Office for National Statistics says of online tools that "any disclosure control applied to online data will have to be stricter than is applied to outputs which the provider is able to assess individually for risk", suggests "A minimum cell count equal to five", registration, a log "audited for potentially suspicious activity" and, "If possible", "a limit on the number of similar tables which can be requested, this is to avoid disclosure by differencing"; the census's own table builder caps the variables, perturbs the same cell the same way in every table, and "will not provide details of which rule or rules have caused the areas to fail". And the price of protection lands on the small: suppression "disproportionately impact[s] small population groups and small areas", and the US census's formal method "systematically undercounts the population in mixed-race and mixed-partisan precincts" while "it does not prevent accurate predictions of an individual's race and ethnicity".
>
> On the law, as read on 6 October 2026 and reported, not advised: the research provisions the brief expected under Article 89 and section 19 were omitted on 5 February 2026 and now sit in Chapter 8A of the UK GDPR, which excludes processing "likely to cause substantial damage or substantial distress" and processing "for the purposes of measures or decisions with respect to a particular data subject", and admits research "whether carried out as a commercial or non-commercial activity". The regulator says of research that "If you would not be able to fully action a withdrawal of consent … then you should not use consent as your lawful basis", and that "There is no exemption to this for scientific research"; a published figure cannot be un-published, and the house takes that sentence as its reason not to publish on consent; the legitimate-interests route carries a three-part test and "extra responsibility"; a DPIA is required for "Combining, comparing or matching personal data obtained from multiple sources", and — "when combined with any other criterion" from the European list — for "the novel application of existing technologies" and for tracking "geolocation or behaviour"; and the statute says that "Where appropriate, the controller shall seek the views of data subjects or their representatives". Inference of a special-category characteristic is special-category processing on the regulator's intention test "regardless of how confident you are that the inference is correct", while what onlookers "could (rightly or wrongly) infer" is a fairness question. A free, non-commercial site is inside the Equality Act — services "to the public or a section of the public (for payment or not)" — and inside the Online Safety Act if users can post what other users see, with no size exemption: "The Act covers services of all sizes … through to services run by micro businesses".
>
> On consent at scene level, the research-ethics literature is consistent on one point: "In no case should a collective community agreement or the consent of a community leader or other authority substitute for an individual's informed consent", and "Community consultation should not be mistaken for community consent". A community can be consulted and can govern; it cannot consent for its members, and "disease-based communities and many social groups typically lack a political structure, which makes community consent inappropriate". The models for a community holding its own data — the CARE principles, OCAP, Māori data sovereignty — offer "a normative foundation" that their authors extend to "minority groups, distinctive communities, or other collectives", but rest their authority, as the scholars of the movement put it, on "inherent rights to self-determination as sovereign entities", and the owner of OCAP says its principles are "specifically First Nations—not Indigenous"; a scene borrows the questions and the structure, never the authority. Withdrawal does not reach the archive — "it would not be possible to remove data from research that had already taken place", "You cannot remove sensitive data from other users' clones of your repository" — so practice is a declared cut-off, graded withdrawal with the irreversible part named, and a tombstone that keeps the identifier and states the reason.
>
> On harm, the regulator's own harm lists are framed on individuals and name no harm to a place; the statistical system's do: geospatial data "has the potential to unfairly disadvantage or blight a geographic area or community", "harm can also include things like stigmatisation", and "potential risk should not necessarily be used as a rationale for avoiding local or small area data altogether". The historic lending maps "led to reduced home ownership rates, house values, and rents and increased racial segregation in later decades"; fine-grained venue data "is a leading indicator for housing price changes"; a crime-map publisher "may be powerless to prevent secondary uses of the data recorded on it once it has gone into the public domain"; and an app that hid one field still let an adversary locate a user "up to the victim's building". "Known but not shown" has clean precedents — sensitive species "Shown at grid-cell level (400 km2 accuracy)", a list "agreed by the UK's statutory bodies and relevant experts" so that partners "do not have to make their own assessments", a presumption of openness with a named harm and a public-interest test — and the platforms and the list state what is held, for the category, and why.
>
> So the canonical truths. Anonymity is a property of the data in its environment, and a public release is the environment in which control is lost; the test is an intruder who knows the community, and the community's knowability sets the disclosure floor above any national rule. Aggregation protects persons and not groups; identity, attribute and group disclosure are three different things with three different remedies. A filter is a table builder, and an interactive view is protected as a query system — stricter than a static table, with fixed classifications, consistency across views, either a limit on overlapping views with a log or a consistent cell key, and a friendly intruder's walk before release. A lawful basis is chosen before collection, and the house does not use consent for what it will publish, because once a figure is published withdrawal cannot reach it; withdrawal is graded, with the irreversible part named and told beforehand. A community cannot consent and is never asked to; it is consulted, it governs and it agrees — three things with three entries in the record — and the models for holding are cited for their questions and never borrowed for their authority. "Known but not shown" is a declared status for a category, with a reason, a resolution, a decider and a review date, never a marker on a unit; the small room is shown as present in a coarser count, never as empty. The harm a dashboard can do runs through representation — labelling a place, making a community findable, turning a figure into a price — so it is assessed before launch as harm to persons, groups and places, with those affected consulted; and proportion is not exemption. Beneath any dashboard that shows people sits a *safeguards record*, and its first entry is written before the first row is collected.

## 1 · Two verbs, and the unit between them

The brief asks "how do we tell THAT story, without exposing or exploiting those communities in the data?" The sentence contains two verbs and one noun, and the chapter is organised around the fact that they belong to different literatures.

*Exposing* is the vocabulary of statistical disclosure control, a discipline older than the web, whose question is whether a published figure lets someone learn something about a person that they were not meant to learn. Its methods — floors, suppression, rounding, swapping, perturbation, and in the last two decades the formal guarantee called differential privacy — are well described, and their costs are measured. Sections 3 to 7 set them out in plain terms, with the costs, and section 7 adds the one thing a dashboard changes: a filter is a table builder, and a table builder is a different object from a table.

*Exploiting* is the vocabulary of research ethics and of the justice frames, whose question is who benefits, who decided, and whether the people in the data would recognise and accept what was done with it. Its methods — consent, consultation, governance, withdrawal — were built for persons taking part in studies, and the chapter's second finding is that they do not transfer cleanly to a community that did not take part in anything but was described. Sections 11 to 14 set out what the literature says a community can and cannot give, what the models for a community holding its own data actually claim, what withdrawal can and cannot reach, and what the precedents are for knowing something and not showing it.

Between the two verbs sits the noun, and the noun is the problem. UK law protects *persons*: personal data is "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person", and the statute's new definition of statistical purposes requires that "the information that results from the processing is aggregate data that is not personal data" — so a dashboard that publishes only aggregates is, on the regulator's own test, aiming at a status the law does not regulate. The ethics literature protects *participants*: people who gave something and can take it back. A dashboard about a scene is about neither. It is about a community of people most of whom gave nothing, were asked nothing, and will appear in it as a count, a share, a shape on a map or an absence; the harm it can do to them is a harm to a group or to a place, which the regulator's own harm lists do not name and the statistical system's do. The chapter's third job, in sections 15 to 17, is to say what that harm is, where it has been measured, and which of the frames gives it a name.

The brief's failure register supplies the test. A dashboard "fails if people dont trust it"; it fails, for nightlife, "if they do not recognise their place within the front-end interface"; and "it fails if its an aggregation of 'the biggest'". The first is this chapter's subject. The second and third pull against it, because the surest way to protect the small is to not show them, and the brief says that is a failure too. Section 14 is where the chapter finds, in other fields, the form that resolves the pull: a layer that is known, held and declared — present on the face, not shown in detail, with its reason stated — which is neither exposure nor erasure.

## 2 · Why people believe what they believe

Eight beliefs sit under most conversations about privacy and dashboards. Each has a reason to exist; each is wrong in a way the evidence can state.

### Belief one: aggregates are anonymous

The intuition is that a count of forty is about nobody. It is true that a count is not a row; it is false that a count discloses nothing. A count of one in a cell is a person; a count of forty in a cell all of whom share a characteristic discloses that characteristic about every one of them to anyone who knows they are in the cell; and two counts that differ by one, in overlapping tables, disclose the one. The Office for National Statistics' own policy states the first — "A cell with a count of one is a potential disclosure risk as it allows for possible immediate identification" — and the discipline's founding critique states the second: k-anonymity "can create groups that leak information due to lack of diversity in the sensitive attribute". The belief persists because it is true for most cells most of the time, and disclosure control exists for the rest.

### Belief two: anonymised once, anonymous forever

The intuition is that anonymisation is a state the data reaches and keeps. The practitioners' framework the UK uses says the opposite in its first pages: "whether data are or are not anonymised is not a property of the data, but determined by the relationship between the data and the context(s) in which they are held", and "Functional anonymisation does not assume that anonymisation can be zero-risk or irreversible". The regulator agrees: "Information may 'move' along the spectrum of identifiability to the point that data protection law starts to apply to it, or stops applying to it", and "You should periodically review the decisions you take when anonymising personal data and the assessments that underpin them". What changes is the environment — what else is published, who is motivated, what tools exist — and the regulator's own worked example is an authority answering yearly information requests for a programme's statistics, safe for three years and refused in the fourth because "the number of participants in the programme had decreased compared to previous years", "a high-profile event involving the participants increased the likelihood that there were people motivated to re-identify them", and more was by then in the public domain. The belief persists because anonymisation feels like an operation performed on a file, and the file does not change.

### Belief three: it is already public

The intuition is that information people posted, or that is on an open map or a listings site, carries no obligation. The regulator's statement is flat: "Organisations must have a lawful reason for collecting and using people's data, even when it is publicly available", and on the duty to tell people, "There is no automatic exception from the right to be informed just because the personal data is in the public domain". The scholarship states the distinction the law assumes: "There is a considerable difference between being in public (i.e. sitting in a park) and being public (i.e. actively courting attention)", and "Data may be public (or semi-public) but this does not simplistically equate with full permission being given for all uses". The sharpest version is that "Publicly available data can be put to a wide range of secondary uses, including being combined with other data sets, that can pose serious risks to individuals and communities". The belief persists because the cost of collecting public data is near zero and the act of collecting it is invisible to the people collected.

### Belief four: consent fixes it

The intuition is that if people agree, the rest follows. Three things cut against it. In law, consent is only one of seven bases, and the regulator says it "won't always be the most appropriate or easiest", is unsuitable "if there is a clear imbalance of power", and — of research — should not be used where withdrawal cannot be honoured: "If you would not be able to fully action a withdrawal of consent … then you should not use consent as your lawful basis". In ethics, consent is a person's act, and "In no case should a collective community agreement or the consent of a community leader or other authority substitute for an individual's informed consent". And in practice, a published aggregate cannot be withdrawn from the people who have read it, so consent to be published is consent to something that cannot be undone — the situation the regulator's research sentence describes. The belief persists because consent is the one word in this field everyone has heard, and because it moves the responsibility to the person who gave it.

### Belief five: small numbers — just hide them

The intuition is that suppressing cells under a floor is costless. It is not. Suppression has to be secondary as well as primary — "In order to ensure that the value cannot be discovered by differencing from the row or column total, additional cells will have to be suppressed" — so one small cell takes others with it; it falls on the people who are few — "suppression rules disproportionately impact small population groups and small areas"; and the alternative, perturbation, protects by making every small count doubtful rather than absent, which the UK census chose and declared: "Where there is a cell count of one in a published output, there is a high chance that it could be a swapped record, a perturbed count or an imputed record". Hiding is one choice among several, each with a cost that lands somewhere, and the brief's "aggregation of 'the biggest'" is the name for where the cost of hiding lands.

### Belief six: differential privacy solves it

The intuition is that there is now a mathematical guarantee and the problem is closed. The guarantee is real and narrow. Its authors describe it as "a promise, made by a data holder, or curator, to a data subject" that "You will not be affected, adversely or otherwise, by allowing your data to be used in any study or analysis", and in the same pages say what it does not promise: "A medical database may teach us that smoking causes cancer, affecting an insurance company's view of a smoker's long-term medical costs … It is the conclusions reached in the study that affect the smoker, not his presence or absence in the data set". It protects participation, not groups; its guarantee deteriorates with group size — "Any (ε, 0)-differentially private mechanism M is (kε, 0)-differentially private for groups of size k", and, in the authors' footnote, "As the group gets larger, the privacy guarantee deteriorates, and this is what we want"; and it has a price, measured in the one national deployment: the US census system "systematically undercounts the population in mixed-race and mixed-partisan precincts", and the Bureau itself tells users to "aggregate small populations and geographies to improve accuracy". The belief persists because the word *privacy* in the name promises more than the definition does.

### Belief seven: the community can consent — ask its leaders

The intuition is that a scene has representatives, and their yes is the scene's yes. The ethics literature has considered this for forty years in medicine and public health and declines it: "For community consent to be valid, there must be a legitimate political system in place, with representatives properly empowered to make such decisions on behalf of the community", and most social groups "typically lack a political structure". The models that do assert collective authority over data — the CARE principles, OCAP, Māori data sovereignty — rest, in the words of the scholarly collection behind them, on "inherent rights to self-determination as sovereign entities predating European settlers", which is not a claim a place-based cultural community can make. Gatekeepers "may also have other interests and may hold preconceived, conventional, and biased ideas". A community can be consulted and can govern a layer it holds; it cannot give the consent the law means on behalf of people who were not asked. The belief persists because asking leaders is easier than asking everyone, and because it is sometimes what the leaders want.

### Belief eight: if it harms no individual, it harms nobody

The intuition is the law's own: harm attaches to persons. The regulator's DPIA guidance lists harms to individuals — "inability to exercise rights", "discrimination", "re-identification of pseudonymised data" — and its 2022 taxonomy adds a societal tier, but neither names harm to a place or a community by stigma. The statistical system's ethics guidance does: "The use of geospatial data also has the potential to unfairly disadvantage or blight a geographic area or community", and "harm can also include things like stigmatisation". The historic case is a set of maps that identified nobody and "led to reduced home ownership rates, house values, and rents and increased racial segregation in later decades". The scholarship has a name for the object the law lacks — group privacy, "a third interest to balance alongside individual privacy and social, commercial and epistemic benefits" — and a reason it is needed: data technologies "formulate types, not tokens". The belief persists because the remedies all run through individuals, and a harm with no claimant is easy to leave uncounted.

## 3 · What exposing means: identity, attribute and group

Disclosure control begins by distinguishing what can be learned. Three things can be, and they need three different protections.

### Identity: who someone is

The founding case is simple enough to retell. In the mid-1990s a US state released hospital records with names removed but with date of birth, sex and postal code kept. A researcher bought the voter roll for the city — "For twenty dollars I purchased the voter registration list for Cambridge Massachusetts" — and found the governor: "six people had his particular birth date; only three of them were men; and, he was the only one in his 5-digit ZIP code". From this came *k-anonymity*: "A release provides k-anonymity protection if the information for each person contained in the release cannot be distinguished from at least k-1 individuals whose information also appears in the release". The attributes an intruder can match on — date of birth, sex, postcode — are *quasi-identifiers*, and the method coarsens them (a year for a date, a district for a postcode) until every combination appears at least k times.

The number most often quoted with it should be handled as what it is. The claim that "87% (216 million of 248 million) of the population in the United States had reported characteristics that likely made them unique" on postcode, sex and date of birth comes from a working paper, not a reviewed article, and its author says it rests on "experiments I conducted using 1990 U.S. Census summary data" — an estimate of likely uniqueness from summary tables, not a count of people found. It is still the most repeated number in the field. The method itself names its limits in its first paper: it "also examines re-identification attacks that can be realized on releases that adhere to k-anonymity unless accompanying policies are respected" — among them releasing a second table of the same rows, cut differently.

### Attribute: something about someone already placed

The second thing an intruder can learn is not who, but what. If a table has been coarsened until every combination of quasi-identifiers appears five times, but all five people in one combination share the same sensitive value, then anyone who knows a person is in that combination learns the value. The paper that showed this put it in two sentences: "First, an attacker can discover the values of sensitive attributes when there is little diversity in those sensitive attributes", and "Second, attackers often have background knowledge, and we show that k-anonymity does not guarantee privacy against attackers using background knowledge". Its examples are a neighbour — "so Alice concludes that Bob has cancer" — and a known fact about a nationality — "Therefore Alice concludes with near certainty that Umeko has a viral infection". The remedy it proposed, *ℓ-diversity*, requires each group to contain "at least ℓ 'well-represented' values for the sensitive attribute"; and the paper after that showed ℓ-diversity "is neither necessary nor sufficient to prevent attribute disclosure", because a group can be diverse and still skewed ("When the overall distribution is skewed, satisfying ℓ-diversity does not prevent attribute disclosure") or diverse in values that mean the same thing ("semantically similar"), and proposed *t-closeness*, under which the distribution of the sensitive value in each group must stay within a distance t of its distribution in the whole table. Its authors add the price in their own words: "requiring that P and Q to be close would also limit the amount of useful information that is released", and the limit: "t-closeness protects against attribute disclosure, but does not deal with identity disclosure".

The ladder from k to ℓ to t is worth knowing for one reason: each rung fixes the previous attack by removing more information, and none of them addresses a group that is uniform on the sensitive value because that is what the group *is*, nor an intruder who asks many questions. Those two gaps are where the next two sections go.

### Group: something about everyone in a cell

The third thing an intruder can learn is not about a person at all. A table that identifies nobody and whose every cell is large can still say that a district is poor, that a venue's regulars are mostly of one ethnicity, that a scene's members are young, or that an area is where a kind of activity happens. The regulator's position is that this is not a disclosure of personal data unless it attaches to a person: "You must have a degree of certainty that information is about one person and not another. The mere possibility of making an educated guess about a person's identity does not present a data protection risk. Even if a guess based on anonymous information is correct, this does not mean that a disclosure of personal data has happened." The same regulator routes the residue elsewhere: "Even if you're not drawing an inference or treating people differently, there may still be an obvious risk that other people could (rightly or wrongly) infer something from the data that might be considered sensitive or private. If this is the case (especially if this relates to the special categories of data), you should think carefully about how you ensure fairness".

The scholarship has given this residue a name. The editors of the one collection on the subject write that data technologies "are directed at the group level and are used to formulate types, not tokens", that "they work to scale, and enable their users to target the collective as much as the individual", and that "our legal, philosophical and analytic attention to the individual may need to be adjusted, and possibly extended" (abstract only †). The open article in the same literature adds the group that did not exist before the analysis: "Analytics allows for a new type of algorithmically assembled group to be formed that does not necessarily align with classes or attributes already protected by privacy and anti-discrimination law" — an *ad hoc group*, "a group whose membership is assembled for a third party interest according to perceived links between members" — and proposes "Group privacy … as a third interest to balance alongside individual privacy and social, commercial and epistemic benefits", noting that "An identity can be imposed on members unaware that they belong to the group". A dashboard that clusters venues into scenes, or colours an area by the character of what happens there, is assembling exactly such a group, and the people in it have not been asked whether the grouping is theirs.

Three disclosures, then, and three remedies that do not substitute for each other: identity is met by coarsening and floors; attribute by diversity within groups, which costs information; group by nothing in the disclosure-control toolkit at all — it is met, if it is met, by fairness, by the equality law in section 10, by consultation in section 11, by governance in section 12 and by the harm assessment in section 15.

## 4 · How re-identification actually works

The intuitions that fail in section 2 fail because re-identification is easier than they assume, and the evidence for that is a short list of cases that anyone building a dashboard with location or behaviour should know by heart.

### Location and behaviour are identifiers

Fifteen months of mobile-phone location data for one and a half million people: "four spatio-temporal points are enough to uniquely identify 95% of the individuals", and coarsening helps less than expected, because "the uniqueness of mobility traces decays approximately as the 1/10 power of their resolution. Hence, even coarse datasets provide little anonymity". Three months of card transactions for 1.1 million people: "four spatiotemporal points are enough to uniquely reidentify 90% of individuals", and "knowing the price of a transaction increases the risk of reidentification by 22%, on average" (abstract †). A model fitted to US demographic data: "we find that 99.98% of Americans would be correctly re-identified in any dataset using 15 demographic attributes" — a model estimate, as the sentence's first words say ("Using our model"), with the figure in the body given for one state — and the authors' conclusion that the results "seriously challenge the technical and legal adequacy of the de-identification release-and-forget model". Film ratings: "With 8 movie ratings (of which 2 may be completely wrong) and dates that may have a 14-day error, 99% of records be uniquely identified in the dataset" (the grammar is the paper's), and the demonstration that "an adversary who knows only a little bit about an individual subscriber can easily identify this subscriber's record". A national health sample: "successful re-identification of patients in an Australian de-identified open health dataset", from "a 10% sample dataset", with the authors' own statement of the trade — "Decreasing the precision of the unit-record level data, or perturbing it statistically, makes re-identification gradually harder at a substantial cost to utility" (abstract †).

The lesson for a dashboard is not that these datasets were careless. It is that a few points of where someone was and when, or a few things they did, are as identifying as a name; and a nightlife dashboard is, by its subject, about where people were, when, and what they did.

### Pseudonyms are not anonymity

A city released a year of taxi trips with the licence numbers hashed. The hash was unsalted, "there are only about 3M possible taxi licence numbers", and "computing the 22M hashes took less than 2 minutes"; the author's conclusion is the general one — "simply using hash functions is an ineffective way to anonymize data". The law reaches the same place by a different route: pseudonymised data "remains personal data and within the scope of the UK GDPR", and "Pseudonymised data is personal data in the hands of someone who holds the additional information".

### Aggregates can be reversed

The least intuitive case is the one that moved a national statistical office. A statistical release is a set of sums; publish enough sums about the same rows and the rows can be recovered. The theoretical result is from 2003: "in order to achieve privacy one has to add perturbation of magnitude Ω(√n)", and "smaller perturbation always results in a strong violation of privacy" — if answers to enough aggregate queries are each off by less than roughly the square root of the population, an adversary can rebuild almost the whole database. The US Census Bureau applied it to itself. For 2010 it had "published approximately 7.7 billion linearly independent statistics" about 308,745,538 people, and "the underlying microdata is recovered merely by finding a set of microdata that is consistent with the published statistical tabulations". Its own researchers "were able to accurately determine location (census block), age (+/- one year), sex, race (63 categories), and ethnicity for 219 million individuals", and "the Census Bureau was able to confirm accurate reidentifications for 52 million people" — the percentages often quoted with these figures are not in the article the Bureau's officer wrote; the arithmetic on the 2010 total gives roughly seven in ten and roughly one in six. A later and stricter simulation: "Ninety-seven million person records (every resident in 70% of all census blocks) are exactly reconstructed with provable certainty using only public information" (abstract †), and an attacker "can reidentify with 95% accuracy population unique individuals who are perfectly reconstructed and not in the modal race and ethnicity category in their census block (3.4 million persons)". The people at risk in that sentence are minorities within their own block. The Bureau's summary of why: "Overall, 44% of the entire US population has a unique combination of sex and single year of age at the block level."

A dashboard is not a census. But a dashboard with filters publishes many sums about the same rows, on request, and section 7 is about what follows.

### Maps and public traces

A fitness company published a global heat map built from "700 million activities" and "1.4 trillion latitude/longitude points" (the figure of a billion activities usually repeated is not on the company's page); its post says the map "only contains public activities and respects all privacy settings" (read once). What the map showed — the running routes of soldiers around bases that were not otherwise mapped — reached the public through journalism this chapter's tools could not open; the official aftermath can be quoted: a US Deputy Secretary of Defense memo of 3 August 2018 that "DoD personnel are prohibited from using geolocation features and functionality on both non-government and government-issued devices, applications, and services while in locations designated as operational areas". The memo does not name the company. The lesson is the one the regulator draws for crime maps: the aggregate respected each person's settings and still disclosed, because the disclosure was of the group and the place. And a search-engine company's release of "twenty million search queries for over 650,000 users over a three-month period", from which a newspaper identified one user by her queries, is known to this chapter only through a tertiary source (an encyclopaedia article citing the newspaper); it is recorded here as the case everyone cites, and nothing further is claimed from it.

### The intruder who knows the place

Every one of these cases needed an intruder with something — a voter roll, a few known points, computing time, a reason. The regulator's test builds that intruder in: "You must consider all practical steps and means that are reasonably likely to be used by someone motivated to identify people whose personal data was used to derive anonymous information. This is known as the motivated intruder test." The intruder "is reasonably competent; has access to appropriate resources (eg the internet, libraries, public documents); and uses investigative techniques (eg making enquiries with people who may have additional knowledge about a person …)"; may be "investigative journalists, estranged partners, stalkers, industrial spies or researchers attempting to demonstrate anonymisation weaknesses"; and may be moved by "curiosity (eg a local person's desire to find out who has been involved in an incident shown on a crime map)". Then the sentence that matters most for a small community: "Identifiability risk can arise where one person or group knows a great deal about another person."

That is Chapter 6's reasoning in the regulator's words. The national small-number rules assume an intruder without local knowledge; a community's own people are intruders with a great deal of it, and the chapter's first truth is that the disclosure floor is set by the community's knowability, above the national rule, because the motivated intruder the regulator describes is, for a scene, a regular. The regulator's list of intruders — journalists, estranged partners, stalkers, spies, researchers — does not name the one a nightlife dashboard's readers will think of first: a public body with power over the venues shown. The chapter treats that reader as an intruder with resources and a reason, and section 18 says why.

## 5 · The methods that protect a table, and what each costs

Disclosure control for published tables is a small toolkit, and the UK's official statements of it are short enough to quote. What follows is the toolkit with each tool's price attached, because the price is the finding: every protection takes something from somebody, and the design choice is whose.

### Floors and suppression

The oldest tool is a floor. The Office for National Statistics' policy for its most sensitive tables says: "Consider a 0, 1 or 2 in any table cell as having potential disclosure risk" (read once); "A cell with a count of one is a potential disclosure risk as it allows for possible immediate identification"; "Similar consideration needs to be given to a count of two"; and then the sentence that shows how a floor is actually set — "The producer of the tables will need to make a judgement call for other low counts, depending on the sensitivity of the table as a whole". The floor is a judgement scaled to sensitivity, not a derived constant, and the national schemes Chapter 6 recorded (two, three, five, seven) are different judgements for different sensitivities. The office's standard protects "the confidentiality of individual and business information", and most of a nightlife dashboard's small cells are businesses — a venue is a business, and a count of one venue in a category names it; the chapter's floors apply to venues as to persons, and section 18 records what the law does and does not say about a nameable business. The same policy adds that a rate can be disclosive when its parts are not published: "A rate or other statistic may be disclosive if it is possible to infer that the underlying counts contain small numbers, even if the latter are not published".

Below the floor, the cell is suppressed, and suppression has a second step most dashboards forget: "In order to ensure that the value cannot be discovered by differencing from the row or column total, additional cells will have to be suppressed (secondary suppression)". The cost is twofold. The obvious cost is the extra cells. The less obvious one is stated by the US Census Bureau's officers in a sentence that should be printed beside every floor: "suppression rules disproportionately impact small population groups and small areas because of the nonignorable missing data problem" — the small are the ones who vanish, and their vanishing is not random, so what remains is biased towards the big. That is the brief's "aggregation of 'the biggest'" described as a statistical mechanism. A dashboard that protects the small by suppressing them has protected them from exposure and delivered them to erasure, and Chapter 4's rule that a suppressed cell is marked and never blanked is the minimum that keeps the erasure visible.

### Rounding and bands

The second tool replaces exact counts with multiples. The national policy lists "rounding to a specific base to avoid very small numbers (usually three or five)"; the European handbook on the subject: "Each method is flexible in terms of the choice of the base for rounding, although common choices are 3 and 5", and rounding to three removes every one and two, "which prevents cases of perceived disclosure as well as actual disclosure" (read once). Controlled rounding keeps tables additive (read once). Bands — "fewer than five", "5 to 9" — are rounding with uneven bases. The cost is precision, spread evenly, and the benefit is that nothing is absent; a banded small count says *present, and few*, which is the form the brief's rule wants for the small room and which section 14 returns to.

### Swapping and perturbation: the census's choice

The UK census does not suppress cells within the tables it publishes; its table builder refuses whole tables instead (section 7). It declares what it does: "We protected the confidentiality of individuals' data in three ways: swapping records between areas, applying a cell key method to each table, and applying disclosure rules in deciding which tables could be published" (read once). Swapping targets the rare — "Households and their individuals that are unique or rare on one or more of those characteristics are highlighted as 'risky records', and all these households would be swapped" — within a district or, rarely, a neighbouring one; "the geographies were changed for between 7% and 10% of households". Perturbation then touches the tables: "a typical dataset would have around 14% of cell counts perturbed by a small amount, and small counts were more likely to have been perturbed than large counts", including zeros ("There is also some perturbation of cells where the counts are zero"). The consequence is the sentence that defines the method's philosophy: "Where there is a cell count of one in a published output, there is a high chance that it could be a swapped record, a perturbed count or an imputed record, or a combination of these". The census protects by doubt, not absence: every small count is published and none can be trusted to be exact.

Its cost is also declared, and declared modestly: "Overall, the differences should be small and so should not change the conclusions of any analysis or research"; "The use of perturbation causes small changes to cells but does not intrinsically impact the interpretation of the data"; and the advice to users — "To minimise the effect of perturbation, we recommend where possible using totals from tables with fewer cells, at higher geographies". No quantified small-area error is given on the pages read. And some of the method is deliberately withheld from the public: "Several features of the SDC methodology are intended to be kept private"; "we intend to keep the perturbation rate private for those small cell counts"; "Knowledge of the exact swapping rate may increase confidence in apparent attribute disclosures". The office's own standard requires it to "Protect the confidentiality of individual and business information when producing statistics" and to "apply appropriate disclosure control methods before release", and requires nothing numeric.

### Fixed classifications

The quietest tool is a rule about what may be asked. "it is good practice, where possible, to have a set of standard classifications to use to release data", because differencing — section 7 — needs two tables with slightly different cuts, and if the cuts are fixed the second table cannot be made. The census builder applies it as a cap: "up to four variables can be selected at Output Area (OA) or Lower layer Super Output Area (LSOA) level", "up to five variables are allowed at MSOA level and above". The cost is flexibility, which is exactly what an interactive dashboard sells.

### Synthetic data

The newest tool manufactures rows. "Synthetic data are microdata records created to improve data utility while preventing disclosure of confidential respondent information" (the office attributes the definition to the US Census Bureau); it is made "by statistically modelling original data and then using those models to generate new data values that reproduce the original data's statistical properties" (read once). The office's pilot describes six levels from structure-only to replica, the last carrying "extremely high disclosure risk, likely to be only available in secure research facility" (read once), and its rule is absolute: "it is not acceptable to release a synthetic dataset that could lead to a real or perceived risk of disclosure". Its joint work with the Turing Institute on a linked census-and-deaths dataset chose a formal guarantee for the generator — "the generator must adhere to the definition of DP, not the synthetic data itself"; "Ultimately, we chose to use ε = 1" (read once) — and said of that guarantee what section 6 says at more length: "While DP offers strong claims of protection against a litany of attacks, it does not provide an absolute guarantee of privacy" (read once). For a dashboard, synthetic data is the method for a demonstration, a test environment or a public download, and not for the face.

### Two floors, and what each is for

Chapter 9 left a floor of fifty answers for a felt measure and a tension with the small room. This section separates the floor from the one it is confused with. The *precision floor* — fifty answers, a coefficient of variation band — is a reliability rule: below it a mean is too uncertain to publish as a figure. The *disclosure floor* — two, three, five or seven in the national schemes Chapter 6 recorded — is an identification rule: below it a count may let someone be identified. They are different numbers for different reasons, they apply to different objects (a mean; a count), and a dashboard states both. A room with twelve answers is above the disclosure floor for a count of answers and far below the precision floor for a mean of them: it can be shown as *twelve people answered*, and not as *the room scores 7.2*. The brief's small room, then, may be lost to precision before it is lost to disclosure — a room with three answers is under both floors — and the remedy for the precision loss is in Chapter 9's record and section 14's held layer, not here.

## 6 · Formal privacy: the promise, the price and the quarrel

Differential privacy is the one method in this field with a proof attached, and the one whose public deployment produced a measured fight. Both are instructive, and both are stated here in the authors' words.

### The definition and the promise

"A randomized function K gives ε-differential privacy if for all data sets D1 and D2 differing on at most one element" the probability of any output changes by at most a factor tied to ε. That is the definition; "Differential privacy is a definition, not an algorithm". What it promises is stated as a promise: "Differential privacy describes a promise, made by a data holder, or curator, to a data subject: 'You will not be affected, adversely or otherwise, by allowing your data to be used in any study or analysis, no matter what other studies, data sets, or information sources, are available.'" The original paper says the same in terms of removal — "even if the participant removed her data from the data set, no outputs (and thus consequences of outputs) would become significantly more or less likely" — and the reason it was needed: an older ideal, that nothing should be learnable about a person from a release that could not be learned without it, "cannot be achieved", and "a variant of the result threatens the privacy even of someone not in the database".

### What it does not promise

The authors are exact about the limit, and the passage deserves quoting whole because the limit is the one a dashboard about a community needs: "A medical database may teach us that smoking causes cancer, affecting an insurance company's view of a smoker's long-term medical costs. Has the smoker been harmed by the analysis? … It is the conclusions reached in the study that affect the smoker, not his presence or absence in the data set." And from the original: "Note that a bad disclosure can still occur, but our guarantee assures the individual that it will not be the presence of her data that causes it". The guarantee is about participation. It is silent about what the study reveals about the group the person belongs to, and the mathematics says why: "Any (ε, 0)-differentially private mechanism M is (kε, 0)-differentially private for groups of size k" — the protection weakens in proportion to the size of the group, and a group of fifty regulars has fifty times less of it than a person.

Two further sentences carry the method's operating logic. The reason it exists is the reconstruction result of section 4, in the authors' phrase: "overly accurate answers to too many questions will destroy privacy in a spectacular way". And the thing it uniquely offers is an account: "the quantification of loss also permits the analysis and control of cumulative privacy loss over multiple computations" (read once) — the *privacy-loss budget*, ε, which is spent by every query answered and which, once spent, is gone. A dashboard with filters answers many queries; the budget is the first formal statement of what that costs.

### The national deployment and its price

The US Census Bureau adopted differential privacy for the 2020 census because "historical methods cannot completely defend against the threats posed by today's technology" — the reconstruction of its own 2010 tables in section 4. Its algorithm "creates noisy versions of key queries on the data, referred to as measurements", holds some totals exact as "statistics that the Census Bureau has determined, as matter of policy, to exclude from the privacy-loss accounting" (read once), and warns that "For well-populated geographies this answer will be useful; however, for sparsely populated geographies it will not be reliable" (read once). Its budget was set by a committee and published: "ε=19.61, which includes ε=17.14 for the persons file and ε=2.47 for the housing unit data", a figure its own release called "exponentially higher than the ε= 12.2 budget used in the April 2021 demonstration data" (read once) after accuracy objections — that is, the office bought back accuracy by spending more privacy, and said so.

The accuracy evidence is the quarrel. The strongest study found that "the DAS systematically undercounts the population in mixed-race and mixed-partisan precincts, yielding unpredictable racial and partisan biases", that "mixed White/non-White precincts lose the most population relative to more homogeneous precincts" (read once), and — the sentence that joins this section to section 3 — "it does not prevent accurate predictions of an individual's race and ethnicity". On the final, larger budget the same authors "observe the same pattern of bias as before, albeit around half the magnitude". A second study found the method "will more strongly affect mortality rate estimates for non-Hispanic blacks and Hispanics than estimates for non-Hispanic whites" and produce "significant changes in estimated mortality rates for less populous areas" (abstract †; it tested a proposed algorithm, not the final one). The demographers' objection was constitutional as much as technical — "Differential privacy goes far beyond what is necessary to keep data safe under census law and precedent"; "Reconstructing microdata from tabular data does not by itself allow identification of respondents" — and the historians of the episode located the fight somewhere else again: "the root of the controversy is epistemic in nature"; it "exposed the statistical illusions and limitations of census data, weakening stakeholders' trust in the data and in the Census Bureau itself"; "Data users did not want the government to intentionally introduce uncertainty for the sake of confidentiality; they wanted access to the bureau's ground truth" (read once). The Bureau's own advice to users stands as the cost in one line: "We encourage data users to aggregate small populations and geographies to improve accuracy and diminish implausible results".

### What a small dashboard takes from it

Three things, none of them the algorithm. First, the frame: a published aggregate is an answer to a query, every answer spends something, and the thing spent does not come back — which is why section 7 treats a filter as a query system and not a picture. Second, the honesty: the national office that adopted the strongest method published its parameters, named the committee that chose them, admitted the small-area cost and told users to aggregate; a dashboard that perturbs or suppresses owes the same account of its method and its cost at its own scale — whether it also publishes the parameter is section 18's question. Third, the limit: formal privacy protects a person's participation and not a group's description, so the mixed-race precinct was both undercounted and still predictable — the group paid the accuracy cost and received no privacy benefit. For a dashboard whose subject is communities, that is the finding: the strongest tool in the box is aimed at a different target.

## 7 · The query-time problem: a filter is a table builder

Everything in sections 5 and 6 was written for tables a statistician could look at before release. A dashboard with a filter is not that. It is a machine that makes tables on request, and the discipline has a separate and sterner body of advice for such machines, which this section gathers because it is the one place where a dashboard's design and disclosure control meet directly.

### Differencing

The attack an interactive tool enables is subtraction. "Differencing involves an intruder using two or more overlapping tables and subtraction to gather additional information about the differences between them"; "A disclosure by differencing can occur when this comparison of two or more tables enables a small cell (0, 1, or perhaps 2, but most specifically 1) to be calculated"; "If these tables are compared they can reveal a new, previously unpublished table for the differenced area". The consequence for anyone protecting a set of views is the rule the Handbook states: "when linked tables are produced from the same dataset it is not sufficient to consider the protection for each table separately. If a cell requires protection in one table then it will require protection in all tables, otherwise the protection in the first table could be undone" (read once). A dashboard whose "all venues" view is safe and whose "venues open after 2 a.m." view is safe can still disclose the venue that differs between them; and a map whose area boundary can be dragged by the reader is a differencing machine by design.

### The official advice for online tools

The most direct UK statement sits, unexpectedly, in the statistics office's policy for birth and death tables, in a section headed "Explorable datasets and online tools". Users of such tools are "able to generate multiple tables and potentially to attempt to breach confidentiality by specifying very detailed or overlapping outputs" (read once). Therefore: "it is likely that any disclosure control applied to online data will have to be stricter than is applied to outputs which the provider is able to assess individually for risk". The floor rises: "A minimum cell count equal to five is suggested", because "A higher minimum frequency will be necessary to ensure comparisons with previously published tables do not lead to individuals and their characteristics being identified". And the tool itself should "require user registration with a validated email address, name and contact details", "keep a log of data requests by user which is audited for potentially suspicious activity", "require users to read and acknowledge a statement on their legal obligations", and, "If possible, place a limit on the number of similar tables which can be requested, this is to avoid disclosure by differencing". The section closes with the sentence a dashboard's service record (Chapter 8) should carry: "The use of online data access tools is still developing, and appropriate controls will have to be kept under review".

The same office's own census table builder does not say any of that. Its pages describe automated checks — "These disclosure checks are automated rule-based checks run by the system, which decide if there is a low enough disclosure risk to allow the release of a dataset" (read once); "The rules in the table builder are necessarily more cautious since they are automated" — the variable caps of section 5, and a defence against differencing built into the perturbation itself: "Where the same cell, or same combination of respondents, appears in different datasets, both instances will have the same cell value and cell key, and so receive the same perturbation", so that "repeated requests of the same dataset will have the same perturbation applied consistently". Its design paper names the threat exactly: "The production of tables from a table builder could allow one to build an extremely large bank of tables"; "One could have access to large and extremely sparse tables which could allow identification of individuals and disclosure of information"; the cell-key method "offers protection against disclosure by differencing, and in instances where a number of tables can be constructed and could otherwise be linked together to reconstruct records from the microdata" (read once). No sentence on either page tracks a user's cumulative queries; the defence is that every answer to the same question is the same answer, so asking twice yields nothing, and that the rules refuse tables that would "give the perception of disclosure" (read once). And the refusal is unexplained by policy: "Where a user is unable to access a dataset for some geographic areas because of the disclosure rules in the table builder, we will not provide details of which rule or rules have caused the areas to fail".

Two official bodies, then, and two models: registration, logging and query limits in one; consistent perturbation, variable caps and unpublished rules in the other. They are not contradictory — the second is what you build when you cannot register the public — and a dashboard chooses between them knowing that the first costs openness and the second costs the reader an explanation. Section 18 takes up what that does to the house's rule that a withheld cell says why.

### A field test of the problem

The clearest demonstration that hiding one field does not stop query-time inference comes from an unlikely literature. Location-based dating apps show each user the distance to others; "Trilateration is one of the well-known threat models to the user's location privacy in location-based apps", and the model "bases on the publicly shown distance from a targeted victim to the adversary to pinpoint the victim's location" (read once). The apps responded by letting users hide the distance or by perturbing it — and "The effectiveness of such approaches however is still questionable", because the researchers "show how the adversary can still figure out the location of a targeted victim even when the 'show distance' function is disabled", locating users on default settings "up to the victim's building". The paper records that the threat "is said to be first reported to Grindr in 2014" and that the app "has adopted a function in which the user can opt to hide the distance since August 2014". Read as technology only, the case is a complete statement of section 7's problem: a single query is safe; many queries from chosen positions reconstruct the hidden thing; and suppressing the obvious field moves the attack to the next one. A dashboard whose reader can move a boundary, change a time window or toggle a category is offering chosen positions.

### Intruder testing

The procedure the UK uses to find out whether its protections hold is to ask someone to break them. "This involves using individuals described as 'friendly intruders' to try and see if they are able to re-identify anyone in the dataset." They are given a bounded time — "(around a half day is normal)" — "they do not need to be specialist hackers with the capability of employing advanced data exploration techniques", and "Each identification claim is given a percentage of confidence". The standard of success is not zero: "The aim is not to release a dataset with zero risk so a good result would be if there were a small number of correct claims with low confidence" (read once). The guidance is written mainly for microdata, the census's 2021 intruder-test results were not found, and no published protocol for intruder-testing an interactive view was reached `[Further research]`. But the form transfers: before a dashboard with filters is released, someone who knows the scene and has half a day is asked to find a person or a venue, and writes down what they found and how sure they are.

### The framework that holds it together

The UK's practitioners' framework for anonymisation puts the pieces of this section in one structure. Its premise is the one that opened section 2 — anonymity "is not a property of the data" — and it names the thing that matters instead: "all data are held in some sort of context, which we call the data environment", whose features are other data, agents, governance processes and infrastructure (read once); "a data situation is the aggregate set of relationships between some data and the set of their environments". Its ten components are, in order: describe the presenting problem; sketch the data flow and determine your responsibilities; map the properties of the environment(s); describe and map the data; engage with stakeholders; evaluate the data situation; select and implement the processes you will use to assess and control disclosure risk; maintain stakeholders' trust; plan what to do if things go wrong; monitor the evolving data situation. Three of the ten are about people (engage, maintain trust, plan for failure) and one is about time (monitor), and that is the point of quoting the list: the disciplinary answer to "is this anonymous?" is a process with stakeholders in it and a review date on it, not a number.

The regulator's release model closes the section. "The assessment will be linked to your 'release model' (ie public release, release to defined groups, or if anonymising data sets for further internal use only)"; "With public release, you should have a very robust approach to anonymisation. This is because when you release information publicly, you lose control of the data. For example, it is almost impossible for you to retract the data if it later becomes clear that it relates to people that are identifiable." A public dashboard is public release. Everything it shows is shown to the motivated intruder of section 4, and it cannot be taken back; the stricter standard for online tools is not a counsel of caution but a description of the environment.

## 8 · The law, part one: what personal data is, and when it stops being

This section and the next report UK data-protection law as it stood when read, on 6 October 2026, with the texts' own words and their in-force dates. They are a report and not advice. Where the regulator is quoted, the page is cited by the name it still carried — the ICO's — although the office of Information Commissioner was abolished and its functions transferred to the Information Commission on 30 September 2026 by commencement regulations made on 10 September; the statute now reads "the Commission", and the regulator's own pages dated 30 September 2026 had not yet changed.

### The definition, and what is in it

"'personal data' means any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person ('data subject'); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier or to one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural or social identity of that natural person" (Article 4(1)). Two of the named identifiers are the ones a nightlife dashboard runs on: "location data" is in the statute's own list, and so is "cultural or social identity".

Identifiability is tested by what is likely, not what is possible. Recital 26: "To determine whether a natural person is identifiable, account should be taken of all the means reasonably likely to be used, such as singling out, either by the controller or by another person to identify the natural person directly or indirectly", with regard to "all objective factors, such as the costs of and the amount of time required for identification, taking into consideration the available technology at the time of the processing and technological developments". And the law's own statement of where it stops: "The principles of data protection should therefore not apply to anonymous information, namely information which does not relate to an identified or identifiable natural person or to personal data rendered anonymous in such a manner that the data subject is not or no longer identifiable. This Regulation does not therefore concern the processing of such anonymous information, including for statistical or research purposes."

### Effective anonymisation, in the regulator's words

The regulator's guidance on anonymisation was published on 28 March 2025; it "is not a statutory code" and, since the 2025 Act, "is under review and may be subject to change" (both read once); the pages carry dates in 2025. Its operating sentences are these. "Anonymisation is the process of turning personal data into anonymous information so that a person is no longer identifiable." "Anonymisation is about reducing the likelihood of a person being identified or identifiable to a sufficiently remote level." "You do not need to take into account any purely hypothetical or theoretical chance of identifiability, rather, what is reasonably likely relative to the circumstances." "Identifiability exists on a spectrum" — "with the binary outcomes at either end, with a blurred band in between". Two indicators: "singling out; and linkability" (read once), where "Distinguishing one record from others in the table is not sufficient by itself to make the person the record relates to identifiable" and "Linkability is sometimes known as the mosaic or jigsaw effect". Then the motivated intruder test of section 4, the release model of section 7, and the one numeric anchor on the page, which is not the regulator's own: "The NHS's standard for publishing health and social care data requires that, for strong anonymisation, k is set to five". The guidance names k-anonymity's limits in the terms of section 3 — "susceptible to certain types of inference attacks (eg homogeneity and background knowledge attacks)" (read once) — and closes the method with the sentence a dashboard should carry on its safeguards record: "Using these methods does not guarantee you will achieve anonymisation: successful anonymisation depends on contextual factors".

Pseudonymisation does not reach this status. "Pseudonymised personal data is in scope of data protection law"; "It is not a way of transforming personal data to the extent the law no longer applies"; and the data may change status in transit — "If you share pseudonymised data (but not the additional information) with another organisation, it may be anonymous information in their hands" (read once) — without changing it for the holder.

### Inference: two tests that pull in different directions

The regulator's two passages on inference, from different guidance, must be read together because a dashboard about a community lives between them. For anonymisation: "An inference refers to the potential to infer, guess or predict details about someone who can already be identified directly or indirectly"; "Whether an inference is personal data depends on whether it relates to an identified or identifiable person"; and the educated-guess passage of section 3 — a correct guess from anonymous information "does not mean that a disclosure of personal data has happened". For special category data: "Whether or not inferred data counts as special category data and triggers Article 9 depends on whether: your processing intends to make an inference linked to one of the special categories of data; or you intend to treat someone differently on the basis of inferred information linked to one of the special categories of data"; "If this is the case, then you are processing special category data regardless of how confident you are that the inference is correct"; "If you carry out any form of profiling which infers things like ethnicity, beliefs, politics, health status (condition or risks), sexual orientation or sex life, you will be processing special category data and must identify an Article 9 condition for processing". The brief expected a certainty test; the current page has an intention test, and the phrase "with a reasonable degree of certainty" is not on it. The regulator's own example is a user posting that they are at a place of worship: "By itself, the user's statement is unlikely to be considered special category data… However, if the social media platform chooses to infer something about the user's religious beliefs, or decides to treat them differently on this basis… then the processing will involve special category data" (read once).

On the regulator's wording, what separates the two is whether the inference is the controller's intention and whether it attaches to an identifiable person. On that reading, a dashboard that colours a venue by the inferred ethnicity, faith, sexuality or health of its regulars is making the inference; a dashboard that shows which venues are busy, from which an onlooker draws that conclusion, is in the fairness passage. The chapter reads; counsel decides. Section 18 tests the studio's own vocabulary — "who is in the room" — against this line.

### The new definition that joins the two questions

On 5 February 2026 the statute gained a definition it did not have. "References in this Regulation to the processing of personal data for statistical purposes are references to processing for statistical surveys or for the production of statistical results where— (a) the information that results from the processing is aggregate data that is not personal data, and (b) the controller does not use the personal data processed, or the information that results from the processing, in support of measures or decisions with respect to a particular data subject" (Article 4(5)). The research definition beside it is wide: "any research that can reasonably be described as scientific, whether publicly or privately funded and whether carried out as a commercial or non-commercial activity" (Article 4(2)), including "technological development or demonstration, fundamental research or applied research" (4(3)(a)). The effect, for this chapter, is that the anonymisation question and the statistics question have become the same question: a dashboard's outputs are statistical, in the statute's sense, only if they are anonymous information, in the statute's sense, and only if the dashboard is not used to act on a particular person. The second limb is where Chapter 1's definition — a display that turns data into a decision — comes under the law's eye, and section 9 returns to it.

## 9 · The law, part two: a basis for a private platform, and its edges

The statute treats whoever decides the purposes and means of processing personal data as a controller, and a private studio collecting any personal data to build a dashboard — contributed answers, scraped listings with names in them, platform data about identifiable accounts, its own site's analytics — would, on its face, be one, needing a lawful basis for each purpose before it starts. This section reports the bases and the edges in the texts' words. It does not say which the studio should choose.

### Seven bases, and the three in play

Article 6(1) now lists seven. Three bear on a dashboard. Consent: "the data subject has given consent to the processing of his or her personal data for one or more specific purposes" (6(1)(a)). A *recognised legitimate interest*: "processing is necessary for the purposes of a recognised legitimate interest" (6(1)(ea), in force in full from 5 February 2026), which the regulator describes as "five conditions containing pre-approved purposes that are in the public interest" — headed, in the Annex's own words, "Disclosure for purposes of processing described in Article 6(1)(e)", national security, emergencies, crime and safeguarding — for which "you don't have to assess whether a person's rights, freedoms or interests outweigh the recognised legitimate interest", and which "you can only use … for the pre-approved purposes listed in its five conditions". None of the five is research, statistics or analytics. And *legitimate interests*: "processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular where the data subject is a child" (6(1)(f)).

The regulator's description of the last is the one most private platforms will read: "Legitimate interests is the most flexible lawful basis for processing personal information. But it won't always be the most appropriate." It fits "where you use people's information: in ways they would reasonably expect; in ways that have a minimal impact on their privacy; or where there's a compelling justification for what you want to do"; it carries "extra responsibility for considering and protecting people's rights and interests"; and it has a procedure — "For this three-part test, you must: identify a legitimate interest; show that the use of personal information is necessary to achieve it; and balance it against the interests, rights and freedoms of the person whose information you want to use" — with a record, the legitimate interests assessment, "to help you demonstrate compliance if needed" (read once).

### Consent, and the withdrawal test

The statute's definition is "freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous indication of the data subject's wishes" (point (11) of Article 4(1)), and its rule on withdrawal is in the first and last of Article 7(3)'s four sentences: "The data subject shall have the right to withdraw his or her consent at any time", and "It shall be as easy to withdraw as to give consent". The regulator's guidance draws three consequences, all on pages dated August 2026. Consent is a weak basis under power: "Consent will not usually be appropriate if there is a clear imbalance of power between you and the individual", because "Those who depend on your services, or fear adverse consequences, might feel they have no choice but to agree". Consent is unfair if it is not real: "If you would still process the personal data on a different lawful basis even if consent were refused or withdrawn, then seeking consent from the individual is misleading and inherently unfair". And consent fails where withdrawal cannot be honoured — the passage this chapter leans on most: "If you would not be able to fully action a withdrawal of consent – for example because deleting data would undermine the research and full anonymisation is not possible – then you should not use consent as your lawful basis (or condition for processing special category data). Consent is only valid if the individual is able to withdraw it at any time." "There is no exemption to this for scientific research." The same page adds that "there is no such thing as 'evolving' consent".

Section 13 is about what withdrawal can reach. Two readings of this guidance are open, and the chapter states both because the choice between them is the studio's and not the regulator's. The sentence sits under the regulator's heading on consent for scientific research, and its own example is one where "full anonymisation is not possible"; the research bodies in section 13 run on consent with a declared cut-off, and the statute says withdrawal "shall not affect the lawfulness of processing based on consent before its withdrawal". On that reading consent with a stated cut-off is a workable basis for contributions. The other reading is the chapter's: a dashboard that publishes figures built from contributions cannot fully action a withdrawal once a figure is on the face and has been read, and the regulator's sentence, written for research, describes that situation. The house rule in section 19 takes the second reading as a stance and says so; it is not the regulator's instruction. The 2025 Act added a second consideration. Article 8A(4): "Where the controller collected the personal data based on Article 6(1)(a) (data subject's consent), processing for a new purpose is only processing in a manner compatible with the original purpose if— (a) it falls within paragraph 3(a) or (c), or (b) it falls within paragraph 3(d) or (e) and the controller cannot reasonably be expected to obtain the data subject's consent" — and paragraph 3(b), the research-and-statistics route, is not in that list. On its face, data collected on consent cannot later be re-used for statistics by the compatibility route; what starts on consent stays on consent. On its face, that is; the reading is the chapter's. The new Article 5(3) adds, for the avoidance of doubt, that "processing is not lawful by virtue only of being processing in a manner that is compatible with the purposes for which the personal data was collected".

### The research provisions, as they now are

The brief expected to find the research safeguards in Article 89 and section 19 of the 2018 Act. Both were omitted on 5 February 2026 — the legislation pages now show only the F-notes — and replaced by a new Chapter 8A of the Regulation, Articles 84A to 84D, whose purposes are "scientific research or historical research", "archiving in the public interest" and "statistical purposes", together "RAS purposes" (read once). Article 84B: "Personal data may only be processed for RAS purposes if— (a) the processing consists of the collection of the personal data (whether from the data subject or otherwise), (b) the processing is carried out in order to convert the personal data into information which can be processed in a manner which does not permit the identification of a data subject, or (c) without the processing, the RAS purposes cannot be fulfilled", and "Processing of personal data for RAS purposes must be carried out subject to appropriate safeguards for the rights and freedoms of the data subject". Article 84C states the two exclusions the brief expected from the former section 19: "The requirement is not satisfied if the processing is likely to cause substantial damage or substantial distress to a data subject to whom the personal data relates" (84C(2)); "The requirement is not satisfied if the processing is carried out for the purposes of measures or decisions with respect to a particular data subject to whom the personal data relates, except where the purposes for which the processing is carried out include the purposes of approved medical research" (84C(3)); and "The requirement is only satisfied if the safeguards include technical and organisational measures for the purpose of ensuring respect for the principle of data minimisation (see Article 5(1)(c)), such as, for example, pseudonymisation" (84C(4)).

The regulator's guidance on these provisions had not caught up when read: its safeguards page, dated October 2025, still says "Article 89 of the UK GDPR says that use of the research provisions is dependent on you having appropriate safeguards in place" (read once), and the landing page says the guidance is under review. Its substance is still informative. "Scientific or historical research has a broad meaning"; "It can also include research carried out in commercial settings, and technological development, innovation and demonstration"; "We developed a set of indicative criteria for each of the three types of research" — among them "Formulating hypotheses, isolating variables, designing experiments", "Critical exposure to scrutiny, including peer review", "Publication of findings", "Ethics guidance", "Committee approval" (all read once) — and "Although you do not need to meet all of these criteria, we would expect you to meet more than one" (read once). "Processing for statistical purposes is processing where the main objective is to generate statistics", and "not all processing that creates statistical results counts as processing for statistical purposes". The exclusions in the regulator's plainer words: you may not use the provisions "if the processing is likely to cause someone substantial damage or substantial distress", where "substantial damage would include both material and non-material harms, such as: financial loss; economic or social disadvantage; physical harm; damage to reputation; loss of confidentiality; or deprivation of rights" (read once except "damage to reputation") and "Substantial distress would include upset, emotional or mental pain. It goes beyond annoyance, irritation, or strong dislike"; nor "if you are carrying out the processing for the purposes of measures or decisions with respect to particular people", which the regulator glosses as using the data "to make specific decisions about the people involved, or to inform the services you provide to them" (closest wording returned). "Where possible, you should carry out your research using anonymous information."

What none of the sources says is whether a dashboard is research or statistics. The statute is neutral as to who the controller is and defines statistical purposes by output and use, not by actor; the regulator's indicative criteria are about method and scrutiny; and no page addresses dashboards. Section 18 records this as a question the studio's counsel answers and the book cannot: a dashboard whose stated purpose is a decision for a named reader is, on its face, close to "measures or decisions", and whether those are "with respect to a particular data subject" depends on what the decision is about. The special-category version of the research condition in the 2018 Act, Schedule 1 paragraph 4, carries one more limb the ordinary provisions do not — the processing "is in the public interest".

### Special category data

Article 9(1) prohibits processing "revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person, data concerning health or data concerning a natural person's sex life or sexual orientation" unless a condition in 9(2) is met — among them explicit consent (9(2)(a)), data "manifestly made public by the data subject" (9(2)(e)), and research, archiving and statistics "carried out in accordance with Article 84B" (9(2)(j), substituted 5 February 2026). Section 8's intention test (the chapter's section 8, not the Regulation's Article 8A) decides when a dashboard's inference is caught. The practical consequence is stated by the regulator's enforcement rather than its guidance: in a 2024 notice on a government location-tracking pilot it found the department "failed to sufficiently assess the privacy intrusion of the continuous collection of people's location information", and the Commissioner said that "Having access to a person's 24/7 movements is highly intrusive, as it is likely to reveal a lot of information about them, including the potential to infer sensitive information such as their religion, sexuality, or health status"; in a 2025 penalty of £2.31 million for a security failure affecting 155,592 UK residents, it named the combination — "post codes, race, ethnic origin, familial connections, and health data could potentially be exploited by malicious actors for financial gain, surveillance or discrimination". Both are read for the legal point only: continuous location reveals protected characteristics by inference, and postcode with ethnicity is a named discrimination vector.

### Telling people, when the data did not come from them

Most of a dashboard's rows will not come from the people they describe. Article 14 then applies: the controller "shall provide the data subject with the following information" (read once) "within a reasonable period after obtaining the personal data, but at the latest within one month". The research-flavoured exemption the brief expected, 14(5)(b), was omitted on 5 February 2026; what remains is general — "providing the information is impossible or would involve a disproportionate effort" (14(5)(e)), or would be "likely to render impossible or seriously impair the achievement of the objectives of the processing" (14(5)(f), read once) — with a new statutory test, "the number of data subjects, the age of the personal data and any appropriate safeguards applied to the processing" (14(6)), and a new statutory duty: "A controller relying on paragraph 5(e) or (f) must take appropriate measures to protect the data subject's rights, freedoms and legitimate interests, including by making the information available publicly" (14(7)). The regulator's older guidance, still on the page: "There is no automatic exception from the right to be informed just because the personal data is in the public domain"; it "should be treated as the exception, not the rule"; "The more significant the effect, the less likely you will be able to rely on this exception"; "If you determine that providing privacy information to individuals does involve a disproportionate effort, you must still publish the privacy information (eg on your website), and you should carry out a DPIA"; "Situations in which it is impossible to provide privacy information to individuals are few and far between"; and, from the companion page, "Tell people where you obtained their information from. If it was from a publicly accessible source you must say this" (read once). A deputy commissioner's public statement on scraping, from 2023, is the plainest: "Organisations must have a lawful reason for collecting and using people's data, even when it is publicly available". And the regulator's AI consultation response: "Web scraping is a large-scale processing activity that often occurs without people being aware of it. This sort of invisible processing poses particular risks to people's rights and freedoms."

Chapter 8 read platform terms as the dependency's conditions. In the statute they are transparency questions as well: data taken from a platform about identifiable accounts is data not obtained from the subject, and the statute owes the subject Article 14's notice or a published reason why not.

### Objection and erasure

Where the basis is legitimate interests, the person may object: "The data subject shall have the right to object, on grounds relating to his or her particular situation, at any time to processing of personal data concerning him or her which is based on point (e), (ea) or (f) of Article 6(1)", after which "The controller shall no longer process the personal data unless the controller demonstrates compelling legitimate grounds for the processing which override the interests, rights and freedoms of the data subject" (Article 21(1)); the regulator adds "You have one calendar month to respond to an objection". For research and statistics the right is narrower — it holds "unless the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out for reasons of public interest" (21(6)) — and "the right to object is more restricted". Erasure applies where "the data subject withdraws consent on which the processing is based … and where there is no other legal ground for the processing" (17(1)(b)), and does not apply "for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes or statistical purposes in accordance with Article 84B in so far as the right referred to in paragraph 1 is likely to render impossible or seriously impair the achievement of the objectives of that processing" (17(3)(d)). And where the data was published: "Where the controller has made the personal data public and is obliged pursuant to paragraph 1 to erase the personal data, the controller … shall take reasonable steps, including technical measures, to inform controllers which are processing the personal data that the data subject has requested the erasure by such controllers of any links to, or copy or replication of, those personal data" (17(2), read once). The law knows that publication spreads, and asks for reasonable steps, not retrieval.

### By design, and the assessment

Article 25(1) requires "appropriate technical and organisational measures, such as pseudonymisation, which are designed to implement data-protection principles, such as data minimisation, in an effective manner", and 25(2) that "by default, only personal data which are necessary for each specific purpose of the processing are processed" and that "by default personal data are not made accessible without the individual's intervention to an indefinite number of natural persons" — a sentence written about defaults that describes a public dashboard's whole purpose. The principle behind it: data "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed" (5(1)(c)).

A data protection impact assessment is required where processing "is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons" (Article 35(1)), including "processing on a large scale of special categories of data" (35(3)(b)) and "a systematic monitoring of a publicly accessible area on a large scale" (35(3)(c)). The regulator's list of operations for which "the ICO requires you to complete a DPIA" includes several that match a public dashboard on their face: "Processing involving the use of new technologies, or the novel application of existing technologies (including AI)"; "Combining, comparing or matching personal data obtained from multiple sources"; "Processing of personal data that has not been obtained direct from the data subject in circumstances where the controller considers that compliance with Article 14 would prove impossible or involve disproportionate effort", with "Data aggregation/data aggregation platforms" and "Re-use of publicly available data" among its examples (read once); "Processing which involves tracking an individual's geolocation or behaviour, including but not limited to the online environment"; "Any profiling of individuals on a large scale"; and "Where the processing is of such a nature that a personal data breach could jeopardise the [physical] health or safety of individuals". Some of these apply only "when combined with any other criterion" from the European list (read once); the companion page says "If in any doubt, we would always recommend that you do a DPIA" (read once). What the assessment contains is statutory — "a systematic description of the envisaged processing operations and the purposes", "an assessment of the necessity and proportionality", "an assessment of the risks to the rights and freedoms of data subjects" (read once), and "the measures envisaged to address the risks" (35(7)) — and so is the sentence this chapter carries into sections 11 and 12: "Where appropriate, the controller shall seek the views of data subjects or their representatives on the intended processing, without prejudice to the protection of commercial or public interests or the security of processing operations" (35(9)). The regulator's step 3 is "consider consultation", and "You should seek and document the views of individuals (or their representatives) unless there is a good reason not to"; "If there is still a high risk, you need to consult the ICO before you can go ahead with the processing"; and "it is good practice to publish your DPIA" (read once). Prior consultation, where the assessment "indicates that the processing would result in a high risk in the absence of measures taken by the controller to mitigate the risk", is to the Commission, which has "up to eight weeks" (Article 36). And the regulator's own harm list for step 5 — "whether physical, emotional or material": "inability to exercise rights", "inability to access services or opportunities", "loss of control over the use of personal data", "discrimination", "identity theft or fraud", "financial loss", "reputational damage", "physical harm", "loss of confidentiality", "re-identification of pseudonymised data", "any other significant economic or social disadvantage" — is section 15's starting point, because it is framed on individuals and names no harm to a place.

### The site's own analytics

A dashboard measures its own readers, and that is governed separately. Regulation 6 of PECR, substituted 5 February 2026: "Subject to Schedule A1, a person must not store information, or gain access to information stored, in the terminal equipment of a subscriber or user" (read once). The new Schedule A1 adds an exception for statistics, with conditions: the "sole purpose of the storage or access is to enable the person— (i) to collect information for statistical purposes about how the service is used with a view to making improvements to the service", the information "is not shared with any other person except for the purpose of enabling that other person to assist with making improvements", the user "is provided with clear and comprehensive information about the purpose", and "is given a simple means of objecting, free of charge, to the storage or access and does not object". The regulator's guidance, finalised 29 April 2026 after the Act's provisions were in force, draws the line a dashboard-builder will feel: "The exception is essentially for analytics purposes"; "However, it is not a broad exception that covers all types of analytics technologies or ways you can use them"; "It is about how your service is used, not about who uses it"; "It is not for identifying, tracking or monitoring people or groups of people who use your service"; "You must ensure that the information resulting from the storage or access is aggregate statistical information that you cannot use to identify people"; consent is needed if you "make inferences or take decisions about people (or categories of people) based on information like their IP address" or for "tracking or profiling individual visitors or categories of visitors"; the example of segmenting visitors by "demographic, including age group and gender" to decide what to promote "cannot rely on the statistical purposes exception" (read once); and "The statistical purposes exception does not apply to collecting or monitoring information automatically emitted by terminal equipment, such as wifi probe requests" (read once). Chapter 8 said the dashboard is a service and needs its own use counted; this is the rule under which it counts.

### Children

Two tests, with different wording, from two regimes. The Children's Code — a statutory code under section 123 of the 2018 Act — "applies to 'information society services likely to be accessed by children' in the UK" and "is not restricted to services specifically directed at children"; it "also covers not-for-profit apps, games and educational sites, as long as those services can be considered as 'economic activity' in a more general sense", and the test is probability: "We consider that for a service to be 'likely' to be accessed, the possibility of this happening needs to be more probable than not", turning on "the nature and content of the service and whether that has particular appeal for children; and the way in which the service is accessed and any measures you put in place to prevent children gaining access" (read once), and revisited if "evidence later emerges that a significant number of children are in fact accessing your service". Its fifteen standards include geolocation and profiling: "Switch geolocation options off by default … Provide an obvious sign for children when location tracking is active. Options which make a child's location visible to others must default back to 'off' at the end of each session"; "Switch options which use profiling 'off' by default". Since 5 February 2026 the statute carries the duty directly: for services "likely to be accessed by children", "the controller must take into account the children's higher protection matters" (Article 25(1A)), and the regulator says "You should already satisfy this requirement if you conform to our Age appropriate design code". The Online Safety Act's test for its own child duties is different — "a significant number of children who are users of the service" or a service "of a kind likely to attract a significant number of users who are children", "based on evidence about who actually uses a service, rather than who the intended users of the service are" — and section 10 reports it. A dashboard about nightlife may not expect children; the first test asks whether that expectation is more probable than not, and the second asks for evidence of who actually comes.

### The scale of enforcement, as context

Article 83's maxima: "£17,500,000, or in the case of an undertaking, up to 4 % of the total worldwide annual turnover of the preceding financial year, whichever is higher" for the principles, consent conditions and rights; "£8,700,000, or … 2 %" for controller obligations including Articles 25 and 35. The two cases read above show the regulator's practice at opposite ends — a notice without a fine for an unassessed location pilot; a penalty of £2.31 million for a breach of genetic data — and are context, not prediction.

### The map that moved

Four places where the brief's statutory map, written in good faith from the law as it was widely described, was out of date on the day of reading: Article 89 and section 19 are gone, and the research safeguards are the Regulation's Chapter 8A; Article 14(5)(b) is gone, and the general exemption now carries a duty to publish; the research and statistics definitions and broad consent are Article 4(2)–(7); and the regulator is the Information Commission. The regulator's own pages were, on the same day, part updated (lawful bases, purpose limitation, cookies) and part not (research, consent, DPIA, transparency, special category), most carrying the notice "under review and may be subject to change". The chapter's rule from this is procedural and it is in the rules table: every statutory citation in a safeguards record carries the date it was read and the version it was read in, and the record is re-read against the current text on a stated schedule.

## 10 · The other boundaries: equality, online safety, and two lines from earlier chapters

Data protection is not the only law a public dashboard sits inside. Two others bind a private platform whatever its size, and both are reported here from the statute and the regulator, with the dates. Every Equality Act page read carries a notice of outstanding changes from a 2025 Act "yet to be applied" to other sections; every Online Safety Act page carries pending amendments from the Crime and Policing Act 2026, which received Royal Assent on 29 April 2026 and has already changed some duties.

### The Equality Act 2010 for a private service provider

The core prohibition does not depend on payment. Section 29(1): "A person (a 'service-provider') concerned with the provision of a service to the public or a section of the public (for payment or not) must not discriminate against a person requiring the service by not providing the person with the service"; and a service-provider "must not, in providing the service, discriminate against a person (B)— (a) as to the terms on which A provides the service to B; (b) by terminating the provision of the service to B; (c) by subjecting B to any other detriment" (29(2), read once). On the definitional words, a free, non-commercial dashboard offered to the public would be a service to the public. The protected characteristics are "age; disability; gender reassignment; marriage and civil partnership; pregnancy and maternity; race; religion or belief; sex; sexual orientation" (section 4, read once).

Three provisions bear on a dashboard's content. Indirect discrimination: a person discriminates "if A applies to B a provision, criterion or practice which is discriminatory in relation to a relevant protected characteristic of B's", which "puts, or would put, persons with whom B shares the characteristic at a particular disadvantage when compared with persons with whom B does not share it" and which "A cannot show it to be a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim" (section 19) — the clause under which a rule like "we do not show areas below a count" or "we rank by footfall" would be examined, if it disadvantaged a group. Harassment: "unwanted conduct related to a relevant protected characteristic" with "the purpose or effect of— (i) violating B's dignity, or (ii) creating an intimidating, hostile, degrading, humiliating or offensive environment for B" (section 26), judged with regard to "the perception of B" and "whether it is reasonable for the conduct to have that effect" — but, for services, with an exclusion: "neither of the following is a relevant protected characteristic— (a) religion or belief; (b) sexual orientation" (29(8)). Positive action is permitted, not required: where a person "reasonably thinks" that "persons who share a protected characteristic suffer a disadvantage connected to the characteristic" or that "participation in an activity by persons who share a protected characteristic is disproportionately low", "This Act does not prohibit P from taking any action which is a proportionate means of achieving the aim" of overcoming it (section 158).

The public sector equality duty does not reach a private studio's own service. "A public authority must, in the exercise of its functions, have due regard to the need to— (a) eliminate discrimination … (b) advance equality of opportunity … (c) foster good relations" (section 149(1)); "A person who is not a public authority but who exercises public functions must, in the exercise of those functions, have due regard to the matters mentioned in subsection (1)" (149(2)), where "A public function is a function that is a function of a public nature for the purposes of the Human Rights Act 1998" (31(4), read once). A studio building for a public body under contract would check whether it is exercising a public function in doing so; the statute does not answer that for it.

Accessibility is a duty under this Act before it is a technical standard. The reasonable-adjustments duty requires, where a practice "puts a disabled person at a substantial disadvantage", steps "as it is reasonable to have to take to avoid the disadvantage" (section 20(3)), including auxiliary aids (20(5)) and, "Where the first or third requirement relates to the provision of information", steps "for ensuring that in the circumstances concerned the information is provided in an accessible format" (20(6)). For services the duty is anticipatory — "the reference in section 20(3), (4) or (5) to a disabled person is to disabled persons generally" (Schedule 2, read once) — and bounded: nothing requires a step "which would fundamentally alter— (a) the nature of the service" (Schedule 2, read once). The Act names no technical standard. Government guidance says "All UK service providers have a legal obligation to make reasonable adjustments under the Equality Act 2010 or the Disability Discrimination Act 1995 (in Northern Ireland)"; the same guidance names "WCAG 2.2 AA" as the standard public bodies must meet, and the public-sector accessibility regulations apply to "a website or mobile application of a public sector body", defined as the State, local authorities, "bodies governed by public law" (established "for the specific purpose of meeting needs in the general interest, not having an industrial or commercial character" and having legal personality, and meeting one of three further tests, of which the first is "financed, for the most part, by the State, regional or local authorities"), and their associations, with non-governmental organisations excepted "unless they provide services that— (i) are essential to the public; or (ii) specifically address the needs of, or are meant for, persons with disabilities". Two exclusions in those regulations matter to a map-led dashboard: "online maps and mapping services, as long as essential information is provided in an accessible digital manner for maps intended for navigational use" (4(2)(d)), and "third-party content that is neither funded nor developed by, nor under the control of, the public sector body" (4(2)(e)). On the words read, a private studio is outside the regulations unless it meets the definition, and inside the Act regardless; the house's table twin for every map (Chapter 8) is its answer to the Act's accessible format, not a reliance on the regulations' exemption.

If the dashboard hosts what users write, the Act has its own knowledge-and-removal rule. Schedule 25: an information society service provider "does not contravene this Act only by doing anything in providing so much of an information society service as consists in the storage of information provided by a recipient of the service, if— (a) the service provider had no actual knowledge when the information was provided that its provision amounted to a contravention of this Act, or (b) on obtaining actual knowledge … the service provider expeditiously removed the information or disabled access to it". The Equality and Human Rights Commission's statutory code for services could not be opened by the chapter's tools (403 on both addresses) `[Further research]`.

### The Online Safety Act 2023 for a small interactive site

Whether a dashboard is inside the Act turns on one definition. "In this Act 'user-to-user service' means an internet service by means of which content that is generated directly on the service by a user of the service, or uploaded to or shared on the service by a user of the service, may be encountered by another user, or other users, of the service" (section 3(1)). "it does not matter if content is actually shared with another user or users as long as a service has a functionality that allows such sharing", and "it does not matter what proportion of content on a service is content described in that subsection" (3(2)). A service is regulated if it "has links with the United Kingdom" — "a significant number of United Kingdom users", or UK users as "one of the target markets" — and is not exempt (section 4). There is no user-number floor in the definition.

The exemption a dashboard would look to is Schedule 1 paragraph 4, "Limited functionality services": a user-to-user service is exempt if users can communicate "only in the following ways— (a) posting comments or reviews relating to provider content; (b) sharing such comments or reviews on a different internet service; (c) expressing a view on such comments or reviews, or on provider content, by means of— (i) applying a 'like' or 'dislike' button or other button of that nature, (ii) applying an emoji or symbol of any kind, (iii) engaging in yes/no voting, or (iv) rating or scoring the content (or the comments or reviews) in any way (including giving star or numerical ratings); (d) producing or displaying identifying content in connection with any of the activities described in paragraphs (a) to (c)". "provider content" is content "published on a service by the provider of the service or by a person acting on behalf of the provider" (55(7)). The regulator's gloss: the exemption applies where users can only post "comments or reviews on the service provider's own content (as distinct from another user's content)", as with "'below the line' comments or reviews on media articles". Reading the definitional words only: a dashboard on which readers can rate or react to panels the studio published is within the exemption's list; a dashboard on which members of the public can place their own pin, write their own text, or see each other's contributions is not obviously commenting on "provider content", and the Act's definition would then apply. Internal tools "available only to a closed group of people" are separately exempt (Schedule 1 paragraph 7, read once).

There is no small-site exemption. The regulator: "The Act covers services of all sizes, ranging from large, well-resourced services, through to services run by micro businesses". The government's explainer: "Providers' safety duties are proportionate to factors including the risk of harm to individuals, and the size and capacity of each provider", and "we aren't requiring small services with limited functionality to take the same actions as the largest corporations". Proportionality works through tiers, not exemption. For a service in scope the statutory duties are "to carry out a suitable and sufficient illegal content risk assessment" (section 9(2)) and keep it up to date; "to take or use proportionate measures relating to the design or operation of the service" (10(2), read once), and "where the provider is alerted by a person to the presence of any illegal content, or becomes aware of it in any other way, swiftly take down such content" (10(3)(b)) — no fixed period is stated for illegal content generally, though a new 10(3A), inserted on 29 June 2026 by the Crime and Policing Act 2026, sets an outer limit of "no later than 48 hours" for content reported as an intimate image; "to operate a service using systems and processes that allow users and affected persons to easily report content" (section 20, read once), where "affected person" includes "a member of a class or group of people with a certain characteristic targeted by the content" (read once); a complaints procedure "easy to access, easy to use (including by children) and transparent" (section 21, read once); and a written record of every risk assessment (section 23, read once). The regulator's pages, rewritten in February 2026: "You have three months to complete a risk assessment after launching a service that the Act applies to" (read once); "If your service is low or negligible risk for all kinds of priority illegal content, you are a 'low risk service;' and the minimum number of measures apply"; "If your service is medium or high risk for two or more kinds of illegal harm, we define it as a 'multi-risk' service"; a "large service" is one with "an average user base of 7 million or more per month in the UK" (the Codes themselves say "more than 7 million monthly active United Kingdom users"); "Some measures will apply to all services", which "include naming a individual accountable for online safety compliance and ensuring your terms of service (or publicly available statements) are clear and accessible" (read once), and "For small services without formal boards or oversight teams, this can simply mean reporting to a senior manager with responsibility for online safety". The measures the Codes apply to every user-to-user service include "Individual accountable for illegal content safety duties and reporting and complaints duties", "Having a content moderation function to review and assess suspected illegal content", "Having a content moderation function that allows for the swift take down of illegal content", "Having easy to find, easy to access and easy to use complaints systems and processes" — and a provider "can apply the measures that apply to your service set out in Ofcom's Codes of Practice or you can take alternative measures". The children's access assessment is a separate duty for every service in scope — by 16 April 2025 for existing services, and for a service that "came into scope of the Act after 16 January 2025", "three months to complete your assessment from the first day your service became available to users in the UK" — and a provider "is only entitled to conclude that it is not possible for children to access a service, or a part of it, if age verification or age estimation is used on the service with the result that children are not normally able to access the service". The penalty maximum is "whichever is the greater of— (a) £18 million, and (b) 10% of the person's qualifying worldwide revenue" (Schedule 13).

The design consequence is in section 16 and the rules, stated as the chapter's reading of the definitional words: a decision to let the public contribute to a dashboard looks like a decision to operate a regulated service with a named accountable person, a risk assessment within three months, a moderation function and a complaints route — at a small service's scale, but not nowhere.

### Two lines carried from earlier chapters

Chapter 9 found the emotion-inference boundary in the EU's AI Act, read as the reason a prohibition exists and not as UK law; it stands here as the outer edge of "signals read as feelings" and is not re-argued. Chapter 8 found that an open licence gives reuse and not supply, and that most open-data licences exclude personal data from what they license; the consequence here is that a dashboard's open-data inputs supply no lawful basis for anything personal in them, and Article 14's duty to tell people where their data came from applies to a publicly available source as to any other.

## 11 · Consent when the unit is a community

The brief asks how the dashboard avoids exploiting "those communities in the data", and the house's own earlier work spoke of consent at scene level. This section reports what the research-ethics literature says a community can give, because the answer is clear and it is not what the phrase suggests.

### The baseline is a person

The founding statement of research ethics names three principles — respect for persons, beneficence, justice — and its first is individual: "Respect for persons incorporates at least two ethical convictions: first, that individuals should be treated as autonomous agents, and second, that persons with diminished autonomy are entitled to protection". Groups appear in it as classes of subject who may be over-used — "Certain groups, such as racial minorities, the economically disadvantaged, the very sick, and the institutionalized may continually be sought as research subjects" — and its justice principle asks "Who ought to receive the benefits of research and bear its burdens?". It contains no collective-consent provision. The UK social-science funder's framework names groups as rights-holders in one of its six principles — "the rights and dignity of individuals and groups should be respected" — and gives no mechanism for the group's part. The internet-research guidelines say consent "may need to be sought not only from individuals, but also from communities and online system administrators" (read once), and in the same document that at scale "Such consent is manifestly impracticable in the case of Big Data projects, however, resulting in a serious ethical dilemma".

### Consultation is not consent

The clearest treatment is from public-health ethics, and its sentences are short. "Community consultation should not be mistaken for community consent, although the 2 are not mutually exclusive." "Consulting with a community includes eliciting feedback, criticism, and suggestions; it does not include asking for approval or permission." "For community consent to be valid, there must be a legitimate political system in place, with representatives properly empowered to make such decisions on behalf of the community." "disease-based communities and many social groups typically lack a political structure, which makes community consent inappropriate." And where it is appropriate, it is not a substitute: "Community consent may occur after community consultation and does not obviate the need for individual consent" (read once); "Rather, the community decides whether to permit investigators to solicit participation from community members". Consultation's goals are stated as four — to "Enhance protections for subjects and communities by identifying risks or hazards that were not previously appreciated", to enhance benefits, to "Confer ethical/political legitimacy by giving those parties with an interest or stake in the proposed research the opportunity to express their views and concerns", and because "Consulted communities may bear some degree of moral responsibility for the research project" — and "Community consultation must be tailored to the communities in which it is conducted".

International instruments say the same in the negative. UNESCO's declaration: "In appropriate cases of research carried out on a group of persons or a community, additional agreement of the legal representatives of the group or community concerned may be sought", and "In no case should a collective community agreement or the consent of a community leader or other authority substitute for an individual's informed consent" (Article 6(3), 2005). The international biomedical guidelines: "In no case, however, may the permission of a community leader or other authority substitute for individual informed consent", while "Such institutional procedures or cultural customs should be respected" (read once), and researchers "should engage potential participants and communities in a meaningful participatory process". The EU's own law on data cooperatives — not UK law, read for the principle — adds that cooperatives may seek "solutions to conflicting positions of individual members of a group on how data can be used where such data relates to several data subjects within that group", and that "it is important to acknowledge that the rights under Regulation (EU) 2016/679 are personal rights of the data subject and that data subjects cannot waive such rights".

The one UK practitioner text found that uses the phrase does so carefully: "Sometimes it is not only the consent of individual people that is required, but also collective consent (e.g. of a community, group or organisation)", followed by "the purpose and uses of the research may change and develop over time, so consent may need to be continually reviewed and renegotiated, especially as specific plans for publication and dissemination are developed" (read once), and "It may also be advisable to give organisations and places different names, as using real names may help identify people". The second-use harm case the literature returns to — a tribe's DNA samples "initially collected for genetic studies on type 2 diabetes, had been used in several other genetic studies" — "reached a settlement in April 2010 that included monetary compensation and return of DNA samples to the Havasupai but left no legal precedent for researchers" (abstract †), and the reported effect on researchers was "the perceived need to safeguard themselves by obtaining broad consent or shying away from research with indigenous communities altogether" (read once).

### Three words, kept apart

The literature licenses three things and forbids a fourth. A community can be *consulted*: told, asked, listened to, with its views recorded and its objections answered — which is also what Article 35(9) and the regulator's step 3 ask of a controller. A community can *govern*: decide, through a structure it recognises, what of its own it will share and under what terms — which is section 12's subject. A community can *agree*: give the "additional agreement" the instruments describe, which adds legitimacy and adds nothing to any individual's consent. What a community cannot do is *consent*, in the statutory sense, on behalf of the people in it, because the rights are personal and cannot be waived, and because most communities have no structure the literature would accept as empowered to do it.

For a dashboard the consequence is that "consent at scene level" is a phrase to retire and three entries to keep in the record: who was consulted and what changed; who governs the held layer and how they were constituted; what the scene agreed and what that agreement is understood to add. None of them is a lawful basis. The lawful basis is section 9's, and it is for the persons whose data is processed. The house's phrase is tested in section 18.

## 12 · Models of voice: holding, governing, and the care in borrowing

If a community cannot consent, the question becomes how it is heard and what it can hold. Several bodies of practice answer, at different depths, and the chapter's finding is that the deepest of them cannot be borrowed whole.

### CARE, and whom it is for

The CARE principles — Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics — were drafted at an Indigenous-led workshop in 2018 and published in 2020, and their authors say whom they are for: they "are people– and purpose-oriented, reflecting the crucial role of data in advancing innovation, governance, and self-determination among Indigenous Peoples"; they are "an assertion of the rights and interests of Indigenous Peoples in relation to data about them, their territories, and their ways of life"; "Indigenous Peoples retain their rights to self-determine their forms of collective governance and rules of membership". The grounding is political, in the UN declaration's "rights to self-determination as political entities" (read once). The paper's conclusion is what makes it citable beyond that ground, and it is more permissive than expected: the principles "have broader implications for other marginalized populations, highlighting the importance of local and group control for the quality and reproducibility of research and data"; they "address issues of relevance for many populations (e.g., privacy, future use, reuse, stewardship), to the extent that the Principles provide a normative foundation for setting standards, crafting policy, and developing research agendas in relation to data acquired about populations as collectives"; and "Those populations might be minority groups, distinctive communities, or other collectives wanting to maintain high levels of trust and accountability in the use of data about their communities".

### OCAP, and the owner's own caution

The First Nations principles — Ownership, Control, Access, Possession — are stated by the body that owns them, and owns them literally: "OCAP® is a registered trademark of the First Nations Information Governance Centre (FNIGC)". "Ownership refers to the relationship of First Nations to their cultural knowledge, data, and information." "Control affirms that First Nations, their communities, and representative bodies are within their rights to seek control over all aspects of research and information management processes that impact them." "Access refers to the fact that First Nations must have access to information and data about themselves and their communities regardless of where it is held." "Possession or stewardship is more concrete: it refers to the physical control of data." The caution is narrower than the chapter expected and therefore stronger: "OCAP® is an expression of First Nations jurisdiction over information about their communities and its community members" (read once), and "As such OCAP® operates as a set of specifically First Nations—not Indigenous—principles"; "OCAP® is not a doctrine or a prescription". The owner says the principles are not even pan-Indigenous.

### Māori data sovereignty, and the clauses a community can read

Te Mana Raraunga's six principles — Rangatiratanga (authority), Whakapapa (relationships), Whanaungatanga (obligations), Kotahitanga (collective benefit), Manaakitanga (reciprocity), Kaitiakitanga (guardianship) — define their subject as "the inherent rights and interests that Māori have in relation to the collection, ownership, and application of Māori data", where "Māori data refers to digital or digitisable information or knowledge that is about or from Māori people, our language, culture, resources or environments". Three of its clauses, read once, are the ones a dashboard about any community should read: "Individuals' rights (including privacy rights), risks and benefits in relation to data need to be balanced with those of the groups of which they are a part. In some contexts, collective Māori rights will prevail over those of individuals" (3.1); "Free, prior and informed consent (FPIC) shall underpin the collection and use of all data from or about Māori. Less defined types of consent shall be balanced by stronger governance arrangements" (5.2); "Māori shall decide which Māori data shall be controlled (tapu) or open (noa) access" (6.3). The scholarly collection behind the movement states its ground in a sentence that is also the reason it does not transfer: "Indigenous data sovereignty thus refers to the proper locus of authority over the management of data about indigenous peoples, their territories and ways of life", rooted "in their inherent rights to self-determination as sovereign entities predating European settlers"; "a demography of indigenous 'population' may be well suited to the provision of citizen rights but it does not provide for the expression of indigenous interests in inherent and proprietary rights as 'peoples'"; and "the manner of application of these principles and practices will necessarily vary between jurisdictions and between indigenous polities". A related initiative's labels — "TK Secret / Sacred", "TK Community Use Only", "TK Seasonal" — "allow communities to express local and specific conditions for sharing and engaging in future research and relationships", and are "an initiative for Indigenous communities and local organizations".

### What a community may take, and what it may not

The chapter's reading, stated as a reading. A scene may take from these bodies of practice their *questions* — who benefits, who has authority, who is responsible, what is sacred or seasonal or community-only, what open means — and their *structure*: a named body, a stated rule about which data is held and which is open, a form of agreement that is renewed, and a principle that weaker consent is balanced by stronger governance. It may not take their *authority*, which is the authority of peoples with inherent rights to self-determination, and it does not improve its claim by using their names. The right form of citation is the one the CARE authors themselves offer — a "normative foundation" for "distinctive communities" — with the owner's caution attached, and the house's own name for its layer. The participatory-mapping ethics literature, written for exactly the practice of a community putting itself on a map, supplies the questions in a ready form: "Who decides on who should participate?"; "and who is left out?"; "Who decides, and who should decide, on what to visualise and make public?"; "Who controls the use of information?"; "Who owns the output?"; "Who owns the map(s)?"; "Who owns the resulting data?"; "What is left with those who generated the information and shared their knowledge?"; "Who gains and who loses?"; "Who is empowered and who is disempowered?" — with its guideline "Avoid exposing people to danger" and its case of villagers whose 3D model "pointed to the hideouts of rebel groups, incurring immediate danger" (read once). That case is the community-mapping version of the heat map: what a community knows, drawn, becomes what an outsider can find.

### The spectrum, and legitimacy

Between consultation and sovereignty lies a graded practice. The Ada Lovelace Institute's framework defines participatory data stewardship as "The responsible use, collection and management of data in a participatory and rights-preserving way" and arranges it on a spectrum it credits to "Sherry Arnstein's 'ladder of citizen participation'": *inform* — "a one-way flow of information from those who use, gather, deploy and analyse data, to data subjects or 'beneficiaries'"; *consult* — "with individuals, groups, networks or communities, to enable people to voice their concerns, thoughts and perspectives"; *involve* — which "positions beneficiaries in an advisory role, which helps inform early-stage decision-making by data stewards and fiduciaries"; *collaborate* — "enabling people to negotiate and engage in trade-offs with powerholders and those governing data about specific aspects of decision-making"; *empower* — enabling them "to exercise full managerial power and agency, and take responsibility for exercising and actively managing decisions about data governance". Its redefinition of legitimacy is the one this book has been looking for since Chapter 5 split "recognition" into legitimacy and validity: "We redefine legitimacy in the context of data, therefore, as the broad base of public support that allows companies, designers, public servants and others to use data, and to design and develop data-governance frameworks". Chapter 9 placed a survey on the ladder; this chapter places the held layer: it is at *collaborate* or *empower*, or it is not held by the community at all.

The structures that hold data on others' behalf exist and are thin. A data trust "provides independent, fiduciary stewardship of data" (2018) — by 2019 the same body wrote "a legal structure that provides independent stewardship of data", and "one approach to data stewardship"; "Data trusts are not legal trusts but they take what we have learnt from them" (read once). A data cooperative, in the one running example reached, lets people "become members of the cooperative and thereby control the cooperative", is nonprofit, and lets them "withdraw their personal data at any time". A council's data charter "co-created with 2 resident panels" (read once) is a council's charter, not an agreement between a community and a public body, and no example of the latter was found `[Further research]`. The widely used ethics canvas has fifteen headings and none on consent or community.

### Who speaks

Every one of these structures has a door, and someone at it. "Gatekeepers control access to a particular community or institution and as a result researchers are reliant on their input"; "Nevertheless, gatekeepers may also have other interests and may hold preconceived, conventional, and biased ideas of what constitutes good research"; they "may present barriers to accessing research participants or they may provide unlimited access to the same participants, leading to multiple studies that prevent a diverse understanding of the community" (read once). The participation literature's own warning: "it is vital to pay closer attention to who is participating, in what and for whose benefit", and "Vagueness about what participation means may have helped the promise of public involvement gain purchase" (abstract †). The political theory behind inclusion: "Processes of debate and decision making often marginalize individuals and groups because the norms of political discussion are biased against some forms of expression" (publisher page). Chapter 9 found that one per cent contribute most and that effort is highest among those with most at stake; this section adds that the people who answer for a scene are the people who are at the door, and that a held layer governed by them is governed by the door. The slogan everyone reaches for — "nothing about us without us" — was, on the open text reached, popularised by "Disability rights and Disability justice activists … in the 1980s and 1990s", and does not appear in the design-justice principles that are usually said to carry it; the nearest is "We center the voices of those who are directly impacted by the outcomes of the design process".

So the record this section leaves has three names on it: who was consulted; who governs; who stood at the door. The third is the one most projects omit.

## 13 · Withdrawal against the archive

"Consent is only valid if the individual is able to withdraw it at any time", in the regulator's words; and a dashboard is an archive that publishes. This section reports what withdrawal can and cannot reach, from the bodies that have had to write it down.

### The law's reach

Article 7(3) gives the right to withdraw "at any time" and says it "shall be as easy to withdraw as to give consent", and that "The withdrawal of consent shall not affect the lawfulness of processing based on consent before its withdrawal" (read once). Article 17(2), for data made public, asks for "reasonable steps, including technical measures, to inform controllers which are processing the personal data" — not retrieval. The regulator's sentence from section 9 closes the loop for research: where withdrawal cannot be fully actioned, consent should not be the basis.

### What the research bodies actually promise

The UK funder states the conflict and the practice in one place: "In giving consent, participants have the right to withdraw this consent as well as the right not to answer particular questions"; "All research should indicate the point at which data will have been anonymised and amalgamated and in certain circumstances cannot then be excluded"; "Some projects give a date after which participants cannot withdraw consent or ask for data destruction"; "Participants need, as far as possible, to give specific consent if data are to be archived and shared"; consent should "be open to revision and withdrawal throughout the research lifecycle", and in participatory research "consent to participate is seen as an ongoing and open-ended process". A declared cut-off is the funder's own practice.

The national biobank grades it, and names the irreversible part. *No further contact*: "UK Biobank would no longer contact the participant directly but would have permission to retain and use information and samples provided previously". *No further access*: it "would no longer contact the participant or obtain further information from health records in the future, but still has permission to use the information and samples provided previously". *No further use*: "Any information and samples collected previously would no longer be available to researchers", and it "would destroy samples (although it may not be possible to trace all distributed sample remnants) and would only hold information for archival audit purposes". And then: "Such a withdrawal would prevent information about the participant from contributing to further research, but it would not be possible to remove data from research that had already taken place."

### What the archives actually do

A research repository: "If the uploaded research object must later be withdrawn, the reason for the withdrawal will be indicated on a tombstone page, which will henceforth be served in its place"; "Withdrawal is considered an exceptional action, which normally should be requested and fully justified by the original uploader"; "Data files are versioned. Records are not versioned"; "original content is never modified"; "The DOI and the URL of the original object are retained" (read once). The national archive: "As a general rule, information published on a website will be considered to be in the public domain and will be removed from that website only in exceptional circumstances"; "Material will be taken down temporarily on receipt of a request"; "The information will be regarded as having been removed temporarily and may be restored at a date decided by The National Archives"; the ground that succeeds is "sensitive personal information about someone who is still alive and continued public access would be unlawful or unfair to them" (read once). The web archive: "We do not make any guarantees beforehand about the outcome of a request."

### What the tools make true

Chapter 8 found that the prototype's repository commits every fetch. The version-control system's own book: "When you do actions in Git, nearly all of them only add data to the Git database"; "It is hard to get the system to do anything that is not undoable or to make it erase data in any way"; "Everything in Git is checksummed before it is stored and is then referred to by that checksum". The hosting platform's current page — the sentence usually quoted from it, that pushed data should be considered compromised, is no longer there — says: "You cannot remove sensitive data from other users' clones of your repository", and that "the commits with sensitive data may still be accessible elsewhere": "In any clones or forks of your repository", "Directly via their SHA-1 hashes in cached views on GitHub", "Through any pull requests that reference them".

### What follows for a dashboard

No fetched source claims a withdrawal right that reaches back through what has been published, analysed or copied. Practice is four things: a declared cut-off, after which contributions "cannot then be excluded"; graded withdrawal, with the irreversible part named in advance; removal from access with a tombstone that keeps the identifier and states the reason; and no guarantee about copies. A dashboard built on contributed data writes all four down before the first contribution — in the measure record's line 12, in Chapter 8's service record, and in this chapter's safeguards record — and tells the contributor, at the moment of contributing, which tier they can reach and what cannot be undone. Chapter 9's "what this changed" is the clearest instance: a line that records what a contribution altered in the world cannot be withdrawn without falsifying the record, and the form that keeps faith with the record is to say so.

## 14 · Known but not shown: the community-held layer

Chapter 4 named six kinds of nothing and found that one the house used — "community-held" — exists in no official vocabulary. This section finds the thing itself in other fields, under other names, with the form a dashboard can use.

### Precedents with data, reduced display and a stated reason

Birds. "Some bird species face risks from humans of capture, targeted killing, or significant targeted disturbance", and "Open-access data can be a risk for these birds that are targeted for exploitation". The platform's resolution: records of sensitive species are "Shown at grid-cell level (400 km2 accuracy) on eBird species maps", "Hidden from checklist views, except to observers on the checklist" (read once), "Hidden from all site-level output (e.g., Hotspot Explorer, bar charts for hotspots)"; still used — "These records are still seen by our eBird reviewers, who must check identifications for accuracy"; and decided with others — "eBird consults with our local partners worldwide in order to develop our list of Sensitive Species". The sister platform's method is the same with a different cell: "The public positional accuracy is increased to the diagonal of a 0.2 x 0.2 degree cell (~500km2 at the equator or about the same size as the Isle of Man)"; "Latitude and longitude are replaced with a random point within this cell"; a *private* setting under which "No geographic information is shared publicly"; and automatic obscuring for "taxa threatened by location disclosure".

The UK's own. "Environmentally sensitive information is any which, if released to the public, would result in an 'adverse effect' on a feature of the environment"; "These lists have been agreed by the UK's statutory bodies and relevant experts"; "Any records for species that are listed on the sensitive species lists (shown below) will be blurred to the resolution stated (i.e., a generalised OS grid reference will be assigned to the record)"; and the design point — "Data Partners do not have to make their own assessments on the sensitivity of their records" (read once): the list is held centrally, so that each contributor is not asked to judge. A separate route exists for "Enhanced access to sensitive species records" (read once).

Heritage. The treasure code of practice requires findspots recorded "to at least a 10 metre square (equivalent to an 8-digit National Grid Reference)" and says that "Neither the precise location of findspots nor details (e.g. addresses and telephone numbers) of the finder, occupier or landowner, should be made public at the inquest". The archaeological scheme's own publication policy could not be opened (403) `[Further research]`.

Statute. The environmental information regulations bind public authorities and not a studio, but their structure is the one: "A public authority shall apply a presumption in favour of disclosure" (12(2)) — the regulations' word for release, not this chapter's; it "may refuse to disclose information to the extent that its disclosure would adversely affect— … (g) the protection of the environment to which the information relates" (12(5)); and only where "in all the circumstances of the case, the public interest in maintaining the exception outweighs the public interest in disclosing the information" (12(1)(b)). A presumption of openness, a named harm, and a weighing.

Crime. The regulator's 2014 advice on crime maps: "Publishing exact household level mapping pins on crime maps will generally constitute the processing of personal data and is likely to breach the first data protection principle's requirement of fairness"; "The greater the number of individuals (number of properties X number of residents) in a mapping area, the lower the privacy risk"; "The privacy risk declines the less frequently the data is uploaded"; "The use of heat maps, blocks and zones can help to represent crime location and type in a way which reduces privacy risks". And a company's decision, in 2021, not to add a layer at all: "We recently decided not to add neighborhood crime data to Redfin.com" (read once), because "given the long history of redlining and racist housing covenants in the United States there's too great a risk of this inaccuracy reinforcing racial bias", and "most crimes in the U.S. go unreported". That is a decision not to publish, stated publicly with its reason; no verified case of a published map later withdrawn was reached.

### The form, and three different nothings

What the precedents share is a form with five parts: the thing exists and is used (reviewers see it; partners hold it); it is shown at a stated coarser resolution or not at all; the decision was made by a named process (a list agreed with statutory bodies; local partners; a public-interest weighing); the reason is stated in public; and the status is visible — a reader of the bird map knows the species is sensitive and shown coarsely, rather than absent. None of them hides the hiding; and none of them marks the hidden unit. The status is declared at the level of the *category* — a species list, a class of record, a kind of information — and the units in the category are coarsened until they cannot be placed, not flagged where they stand.

A dashboard can now distinguish three nothings — three of Chapter 4's six kinds, the ones a reader meets on a face — that Chapter 4 and Chapter 9 left tangled. A cell *withheld* under the disclosure floor is a nothing with a statistical reason: too few to show without identifying. A measure *unmeasured* under the precision floor is a nothing with a reliability reason: too few answers for the number to mean anything. A layer *held* is a nothing with a governance reason: data that could be shown under both floors, known, and not shown at its own resolution because the people it describes and the studio decided that showing it would expose them — to the intruder of section 4, to the speculation and findability of section 15. The three reasons can coincide in one room; the record says which apply. The first two are the statistician's; the third is the community's, and it is the one the brief's two failures meet in. "It fails if its an aggregation of 'the biggest'" and "it fails if they do not recognise their place" are both answered by a small room that is present in a coarser count — a band, a district total, a presence mark for its category — with the category's held status and its reason declared once, in public, with a review date; which is neither the biggest nor erased.

The held status is itself information, and the chapter applies its own disclosure rules to it. A marker on a single room saying *held at the request of the people in it* tells every onlooker that this room has something to protect, and a reason stated per room publishes the inference the holding was meant to prevent; the national office's reason for keeping its parameters private — knowledge of them "may increase confidence in apparent attribute disclosures" — applies with more force here. So the status is declared for the category and never for the unit — *rooms of this kind in this area are shown at district level, at the request of those who run them* — the reason is given for the category, and the units are coarsened until a reader cannot place them, which is what every precedent above does. The walk of section 7 then tests the held layer as it tests any other view: can the held units be recovered by subtracting the dashboard from the listings, ticketing pages and socials that already name them? If they can, holding has protected nothing and the layer is either lit or coarsened further. And the decision to hold is a condition the studio sets on itself at the community's request through a body it names; it is not a consent given by that body for anyone, and section 12's question — who stood at the door — is written beside it.

The UK statistical system's ethics guidance supplies the counterweight so that holding does not become hiding: "potential risk should not necessarily be used as a rationale for avoiding local or small area data altogether". A held layer is a decision taken, recorded and reviewed, not a default; and "Missing data is not the same as a zero value" is, on a dashboard, the rule that a held layer is drawn as held and never as empty. `[Revisit → Ch 11]` carries the display question: what a held category looks like on a face, beside a withheld cell and an unmeasured room, so that a reader can tell the three apart without being able to place the held units.

## 15 · Harm: to persons, to groups, to places

The law's harm is a person's. A dashboard's harm, when it comes, is usually a place's or a group's, and this section gathers what has been written down about that, from the typologies to the measured cases.

### The typologies, and the gap in them

The fullest legal typology lists physical, economic, reputational and psychological harms; autonomy harms (coercion, manipulation, failure to inform, "Thwarted Expectations", lack of control, chilling effects); "Discrimination Harms"; and "Relationship Harms" — and says why the law misses most of them: "The requirement of harm has significantly impeded the enforcement of privacy law"; "Countless privacy violations are not remedied or addressed on the grounds that there has been no cognizable harm"; "When privacy violations result in negative consequences, the effects are often small—frustration, aggravation, anxiety, inconvenience—and dispersed among a large number of people", yet "Over time, as people are individually inundated by a swarm of small harms, the overall societal impact is significant" (both read once). It is a US-law frame. The UK regulator's own taxonomy has two tiers: individual harms — "Financial harm", "Bodily harm", "Costs of avoiding/mitigating harm", "Discrimination", "Unwarranted intrusion", "Loss of control of personal data", "Lack of autonomy; manipulation and influence", "Psychological Harms", "Chilling effects", "Adverse effects on rights and freedoms" — and societal harms, including "Damage to media, democracy, information and public discourse", public health, the economy and the environment. Neither the taxonomy nor the DPIA list of section 9 names a harm to a place, a neighbourhood or a community by stigma; the chapter checked for the word and did not find it. An algorithmic-harm taxonomy from a review of 172 studies has five themes — "representational, allocative, quality-of-service, interpersonal harms, and social system/societal harms" (abstract †) — and the first of them, representational, is the one a map does.

The UK statistical system names what the regulator does not. Its geospatial ethics guidance: "The use of geospatial data also has the potential to unfairly disadvantage or blight a geographic area or community"; "there is always the possibility of an area or community being disadvantaged or 'redlined'"; "Area classifications (where areas are categorised in an attempt to capture the most common characteristics of their residents) is one topic where it is particularly easy to slip into assumptions"; "Where there is any potential for discrimination, the purpose for any data access and use should be clearly stated"; on location — "An individual (X, Y) location can point at a single address or business. A small area can pinpoint a small enough number of cases to be disclosive", and location "might have real implications for the personal safety of vulnerable people or groups"; and on presentation — "Red traditionally spells danger, while dark or dull colours can imply a negative association"; "Missing data is not the same as a zero value"; "Large areas, perhaps rural areas with relatively small populations, tend to dominate visually while physically small urban areas can get neglected". Its public-good guidance: public good asks "What impact will your work have on people, communities, organisations, and companies?"; "Remember, harm can also include things like stigmatisation"; and the balance — "Just because a project has risks associated with it, does not mean that it should not be done", provided one considers "whether any remaining risks can be justified to achieve the intended benefits". The statistics code, reissued as Edition 3.0 in November 2025, says "Everyone can apply or draw on the Code" and that its principles "can be used by anyone in any organisation who wants to ensure that the information they provide serves the public good"; the word "harm" does not appear on its principles page. And the official index that ranks every small area in England carries its own caveat against the ecological fallacy: "Within every area there will be individuals who are deprived and individuals who are not"; "The Index is not a suitable tool for targeting individuals"; the ranks "can tell you if one area is more deprived than another but not by how much"; the Indices are "designed to identify aspects of deprivation, not affluence". The fallacy itself was named in 1950 — "An individual correlation is a correlation in which the statistical object or thing described is indivisible"; "In an ecological correlation the statistical object is a group of persons" — and restated by the statistical system: "The ecological fallacy arises from erroneously assuming that relationships observed for groups necessarily hold for individuals". A dashboard that labels a place by its average and lets the label fall on the people in it has committed the fallacy on their behalf.

### Stigma is done through representation

The sociology of stigmatised places states the mechanism in terms a dashboard-builder should read as a description of their product. Territorial stigmatisation "is not a static condition or a neutral process, but a consequential and injurious form of action through collective representation fastened on place" (abstract †); it works on "the residents of disparaged districts, the surrounding denizens and commercial operators, street-level public bureaucracies, specialists in cultural production (such as journalists, scholars, and politicians), and state officials and policies"; it has become "autonomized, nationalized and democratized, equated with social disintegration, racialized through selective accentuation". Scholars are named among those who do it. The experimental counterpart: in some 500 Chicago block groups, "Observed disorder predicts perceived disorder, but racial and economic context matter more", and "As the concentration of minority groups and poverty increases, residents of all races perceive heightened disorder even after we account for an extensive array of personal characteristics and independently observed neighborhood conditions", "generating self-reinforcing processes that may help account for the perpetuation of urban racial inequality" (abstract †). Perception of a place tracks who lives there more than what is observed there; a dashboard that publishes what is observed does not control what is perceived.

### The maps that did measurable harm

The historic case is a set of lending-risk maps drawn in the 1930s for "over 200 cities" (read once), whose lowest grade was shaded red and whose grade descriptions included "infiltration of a lower grade population". The leading study's published abstract has no numbers: "The maps led to reduced home ownership rates, house values, and rents and increased racial segregation in later decades", and "the HOLC maps had meaningful and lasting effects on the development of urban neighborhoods through reduced credit access and subsequent disinvestment" (abstract †). Its working paper does: "the maps account for between 15 to 30 percent of the overall gap in share African American and home ownership over the 1950 to 1980 period and 40 percent of the gap in house values". The counter-position argues that the maps recorded a judgement rather than made one — "these maps were not the primary source used by the FHA to create their 'redlining' maps for insuring mortgages"; "racial bias in the construction of the HOLC maps can explain at most 4 to 20 percent of the observed concentration of black households in the lowest-rated zones"; "decades of disadvantage and discrimination had already pushed them into the core of economically distressed neighborhoods" (abstract †). The two figures are easily mis-set against each other: one is the maps' *effect* on later outcomes, the other is the share of the maps' *construction* explained by bias. The debate is whether a map causes harm or formalises an existing one; either way the map is the means by which the harm became durable, and that is the finding for a dashboard that grades places.

The UK's own public maps have a smaller record. The street-level crime maps on police.uk, published since December 2008, were reviewed in 2012: "many of the original objectives relating to improving engagement and empowerment have yet to be realized, poor cartographic discipline has led to misinterpretation and confusion" (abstract †). Their data has been priced: using the maps and land-registry transactions, "each case of anti-social behaviour per ten population in the same street leads to an approximately 0.6–0.8% drop in property prices, while a corresponding increase in violent crime decreases prices by roughly 0.6–1.6%", and "Crime outside of the respective street does not appear to matter" (abstract †) — an estimate of the price of street-level risk, not a test of whether publishing moved prices, and the chapter does not claim the second. The regulator foresaw the secondary market in 2014: "Crime-maps clearly have a range of secondary uses, for example to estate agents or insurance companies", and "the publisher of a crime-map may be powerless to prevent secondary uses of the data recorded on it once it has gone into the public domain. Personal data is not 'fair game', even if it is already publicly available" (read once).

### Coordinates for speculation

A dashboard about where culture is alive is, to a different reader, a map of where to buy. The evidence that fine-grained venue data works as that signal is direct: "gentrifying neighborhoods tend to have growing numbers of local groceries, cafes, restaurants, and bars", and "local economic activity, as measured by Yelp data, is a leading indicator for housing price changes and can help to predict which neighborhoods are gentrifying" (abstract †); and the same authors' companion paper on nowcasting local economic activity finds that the venue data "can explain 29.2 percent of the residual variance after accounting for lagged" official business counts. Short-let listings move the same prices: "a 1% increase in Airbnb listings leads to a 0.018% increase in rents and a 0.026% increase in house prices", which the authors translate as "about one-fifth of actual rent growth and about one-seventh of actual price growth" for the median area (abstract †). The chapter's reading: a panel that shows which small areas are gaining venues, footfall or felt liveliness is the leading indicator those studies measured, published free, in real time, by a studio whose stated aim is the opposite of displacement. Chapter 7's rule against ranking and this chapter's held layer are the two tools that address it; neither removes it. `[Validate with users]` whether the scenes see the money view as advocacy or as a listing.

### Visibility as a risk to a community

For some communities, being findable is the harm. The location-app case of section 7 is one instance; the data-sharing case behind it is another, read for its mechanism: an audit of ten apps found them "transmitting user data to at least 135 different third parties involved in advertising and/or behavioural profiling", and one "shared detailed user data with a large number of third parties", including "IP address, Advertising ID, GPS location, age, and gender" — data whose sharing, the complainant argued and the Norwegian authority later found, had no legal basis, "and must be considered as particularly sensitive as it concerns users' sexual orientation" (the complainant's account of the decision; the authority's own page could not be opened). The fine of 65 million kroner was upheld on appeal in October 2025, nearly six years after the report. Association itself discloses: "Public information about one's coworkers, friends, family, and acquaintances, as well as one's associations with them, implicitly reveals private information"; "Network data shifts the locus of information control away from individuals" (a 2009 student study of 4,080 profiles on one network, cited for the mechanism and not the accuracy). And being counted is not neutral: "Demographic data commonly imagines identity as fixed, singular, and discrete", and "the issue of who is or is not 'counted' profoundly affects visibility, access, and power in the digital realm". A dashboard that makes a scene legible to its members makes it legible to everyone, and the people with most to lose from legibility are often the people the brief most wants to recognise themselves. The harm assessment this chapter requires asks that question of each layer before it is lit.

## 16 · Hostile contributors

A dashboard that accepts contributions has readers who want to break it, and Chapter 9 recorded the open-vote cases. This section reports the discipline that has thought about it longest, the evidence on who it drives away, and the regulatory floor.

### The verbs and adverbs of moderation

"By 'moderation,' I mean the governance mechanisms that structure participation in a community to facilitate cooperation and prevent abuse." The study's grammar is the useful part: "There are four: excluding, pricing, organizing, and norm-setting"; "Pricing inhibits participation, both good and bad, by raising its costs"; "Organization shapes the flow of content from authors to readers" — by deletion, editing, annotation, synthesis, filtering, formatting (read once); and "Moderation's biggest challenge and most important mission is to create strong shared norms among participants". Each verb takes adverbs, given as four pairs of sub-headings: automatically or manually, transparently or secretly, ex ante or ex post, centrally or distributedly. A moderation design is a choice of verbs and adverbs, and a safeguards record names them.

A worked system shows the verbs applied. A forum platform's trust levels exist for "Sandboxing new users in your community so that they cannot accidentally hurt themselves, or other users while they are learning what to do" and "Granting experienced users more rights over time"; a new user cannot "Post more than 2 hyperlinks in a post", and the first promotion requires "Entering at least 5 topics", "Reading at least 30 posts" and ten minutes of reading (read once). In the grammar above, that is pricing and exclusion, applied automatically and ex ante, and it is the general form of every rule that says a contribution counts only after the contributor has done something costly first — Chapter 9's answer to the repeatable vote.

### Who is driven away

Participation has a cost that falls unevenly, and gating adds to it. Of 10,093 US adults surveyed in September 2020, "41% of Americans have personally experienced some form of online harassment", and among lesbian, gay or bisexual adults "Roughly seven-in-ten have encountered any harassment online and fully 51% have been targeted for more severe forms of online abuse"; harassed Black and Hispanic adults were far likelier than White to attribute it to race (read once). Of some 4,000 women in eight countries, "Nearly a quarter (23%) … said they had experienced online abuse or harassment at least once", "Over three quarters (76%) of women who said that they had experienced abuse or harassment on a social media platform made changes to the way they use the platforms", and "32% of women said they'd stopped posting content that expressed their opinion on certain issues" (the page's base for that figure appears to be women who had experienced abuse, and the chapter does not state it more precisely). Harassment changes who contributes, and it changes it in the direction of the people the brief least wants to lose. The measure record's line 5 — who answered and who did not — is where that cost is counted.

### Safety by design, and the floor

The regulator's framework in the field states three principles: "Service provider responsibility" — "The burden of safety should never fall solely upon the user"; "User empowerment and autonomy" — "The dignity of users is of central importance"; "Transparency and accountability" — "hallmarks of a robust approach to safety". Section 10's statutory floor is the UK form: a named accountable individual, a risk assessment within three months of launch, a reporting route for "affected persons" including "a member of a class or group of people with a certain characteristic targeted by the content", swift takedown, a complaints procedure, and a written record — and the Equality Act's own knowledge-and-removal rule for hosted content beside it. Chapter 5's audit — a scheduled, recorded check of what the system has been doing — is the practice that finds gaming after the fact; the verbs and adverbs are the design that prices it before.

## 17 · The frames: what each adds, and what none settles

Six frames were read for what they add to the lines a dashboard draws. Each adds a question. None draws the line.

*Data justice.* "fairness in the way people are made visible, represented and treated as a result of their production of digital data", on three pillars — "(in)visibility, (dis)engagement with technology and antidiscrimination" — where "The first pillar, visibility, deals both with privacy and representation", the second is "The freedom to control the terms of one's engagement with data markets", and the third has "two dimensions: the power to identify and challenge bias in data use, and the freedom not to be discriminated against". Its contribution to this chapter is the level: "many of the negative impacts of data occur on the group as much as the individual level", because "New data technologies tend to sort, profile and inform action based on group rather than individual characteristics and behaviour". The second school of the same name frames it against surveillance: resistance "needs to be (re)conceptualized on terms that can address the implications of this data-driven form of governance in relation to broader social justice agendas". What it adds: visibility is a two-sided good — privacy and representation are the same pillar — which is the brief's recognition failure and its exposure failure in one sentence.

*Data feminism.* Seven principles, in the authors' words: "Examine power. Data feminism begins by analyzing how power operates in the world." "Challenge power." "Elevate emotion and embodiment" — "the knowledge that comes from people as living, feeling bodies in the world". "Rethink binaries and hierarchies" — "challenge the gender binary, along with other systems of counting and classification that perpetuate oppression". "Embrace pluralism" — "with priority given to local, Indigenous, and experiential ways of knowing". "Consider context" — "data are not neutral or objective. They are the products of unequal social relations, and this context is essential for conducting accurate, ethical analysis." "Make labor visible." What it adds: the classification question — whose categories count the scene — which section 3's ad hoc group and section 15's "who is counted" both raise.

*Design justice.* Ten principles, among them: "We center the voices of those who are directly impacted by the outcomes of the design process"; "We prioritize design's impact on the community over the intentions of the designer"; "We see the role of the designer as a facilitator rather than an expert"; "We work towards sustainable, community-led and -controlled outcomes". What it adds: impact over intention — the test for a money view is what it does, not what the studio meant.

*Responsible data.* "the collective duty to account for unintended consequences of working with data by: 1. Prioritising people's rights to consent, privacy, security and ownership when using data in social change and advocacy efforts, and 2. Implementing values and practices of transparency and openness"; the NGO policy that preceded it names five rights — "the right to be counted and heard; the right to dignity and respect; the right to make an informed decision; the right to privacy; the right to not be put at risk". What it adds: *unintended consequences* as a duty, which is the measure record's line 12 and the safeguards record's last line.

*Contextual integrity.* "I posit two types of informational norms: norms of appropriateness, and norms of flow or distribution"; "Contextual integrity is maintained when both types of norms are upheld, and it is violated when either of the norms is violated"; "there are no arenas of life not governed by norms of information flow, no information or spheres of life for which 'anything goes'"; public information "is always tagged with that context and never 'up for grabs'" (fragment). What it adds: the test for "already public" — not whether the information was accessible, but whether moving it from a listing site, a ticketing platform or a social feed into a civic dashboard matches the norms under which it was given. The critical-data scholars state the same as a heading, "Just because it is accessible does not make it ethical" (the published wording; the draft had "doesn't"), and as the cautionary case of a released dataset of an entire college cohort in which "the source of the data was quickly identified, placing the privacy of the students at risk" (abstract †) despite "good-faith attempts … to hide the identity of the institution".

*The research-ethics baselines.* The social-science funder's framework "should be observed by ESRC-funded researchers and ROs, and may be useful to other research-related audiences"; it has no sentence for a body without an ethics committee, and its own bar is that "methods such as further analysis of data are not necessarily exempt from full review" (read once). The market-research code binds its members: "a participant's right to withdraw from a project at any stage is respected"; "the anonymity of participants is preserved unless participants have given their informed consent for their details to be revealed"; "A child is an individual under the age of 16"; vulnerable people are "individuals whose permanent or temporary personal circumstances and/or characteristics mean that they are less able to protect or represent their interests". The internet-research guidelines offer "'guidelines, not recipes'", a judgement that "is deeply relational and intersubjective", and one scaling rule — "the greater the vulnerability of our subjects, the greater our responsibility and obligation to protect them from likely harms". The national statistician's ethics committee asks that "The use of data has clear benefits for users and serves the public good", that "The views of the public are considered", that access "is transparent", and that "the issue of consent is considered appropriately". What they add: a studio that is not a university and not a member body is covered by none of them and can adopt any of them, and the adoption is a stance to be recorded.

None of the six says where the disclosure floor goes, who speaks for a scene, or when a layer is held. They are questions put to the people in the data, and the chapter's view is that this is what they are for.

## 18 · The studio's own positions, tested

The symmetry test: every standard this chapter applied to others' methods is now applied to the studio's own positions, as recorded in version 1 of this book, in the Decision Book's chapters on disclosure and uncertainty, and in the brief. Each is stated, tested, and left as *holds*, *holds with a change*, or *does not hold*.

*Threshold three for small cells.* Version 1 and the Decision Book withhold counts under three. The national policy lists "suppressing very small numbers (usually numbers less than three)" among its methods; its suggested minimum for online tools is five; the health service's standard the regulator cites is five; Chapter 6 set the floor by the scene's knowability, above any national rule. **Holds with a change:** three is the static-table value, and this chapter's finding is that a dashboard with filters is an online tool, for which the same office suggests five and a judgement "depending on the sensitivity of the table as a whole". The value is Adaptable; the procedure — a floor per view, set above the national rule where the scene can name the units, and stated — is Fixed. And the cost is now named: "suppression rules disproportionately impact small population groups", so every floor is paired with a banded or held form that keeps the small present.

*Secondary suppression.* **Holds**, and is restated for views: "If a cell requires protection in one table then it will require protection in all tables". The alternative the census uses — a consistent cell key, so that the same cell perturbs the same way in every view — is a legitimate substitute the Decision Book does not mention, and section 20 leaves the choice open.

*Fixed categories.* **Holds**, and gains its reason: "it is good practice, where possible, to have a set of standard classifications to use to release data", because differencing needs two cuts. A filter that lets the reader define a category, draw a boundary or choose a time window is the opposite of a fixed classification, and the rules now say that any such control is treated as a table builder and protected as one.

*Suppression at query time, with the reason.* Version 1 said a withheld cell says why. The census builder "will not provide details of which rule or rules have caused the areas to fail", and its design paper keeps parameters private because knowing them "may increase confidence in apparent attribute disclosures". **Holds with a change:** the *status* is always shown — a cell that is not shown is marked, never blank, and the kind of reason (disclosure floor; precision floor; held) is stated, because Chapter 4's six nothings require it — but the *rule and its parameters* need not be, and a dashboard may decide, as the national office does for its table builder (which says "because of the disclosure rules" and no more), that saying "fewer than five" tells an intruder which way to look. The reason is shown at the kind, not at the parameter, unless the parameter is itself published policy.

*The adversarial filter walk.* Version 1 proposed walking the filters as an adversary before release. **Holds, and is named**: it is intruder testing — "friendly intruders", "around a half day", "Each identification claim is given a percentage of confidence", and the standard that "a good result would be if there were a small number of correct claims with low confidence" — applied to an interactive view. Two changes: the walker is someone who knows the scene, because the motivated intruder is a regular; and the walk is not a substitute for the method, because the location-app case shows a system that passed casual inspection and failed systematic querying. The walk's claims, confidences and consequences are a line in the record.

*"Consent to be found."* Version 1 proposed that a scene consents to be findable. Section 11: a scene cannot consent; it can be consulted, govern and agree. Section 9: consent as a lawful basis fails where withdrawal cannot be fully actioned, and a published view cannot be withdrawn from those who read it; and data collected on consent cannot later take the statistics route (Article 8A(4)). **Does not hold as worded.** What the house meant survives in three parts with three names: individuals who contribute do so on a basis chosen for the fact of publication, told the withdrawal tiers; the scene is consulted and the consultation is recorded; and the layer that makes the scene findable is held or lit by a governance the scene recognises, with the decider named. The phrase to use is *agreed to be shown*, and the agreement is understood to add legitimacy and nothing to any person's rights.

*"The money view shows only consented scenes."* Same test, and two more. 84C(3) excludes from the research provisions processing "for the purposes of measures or decisions with respect to a particular data subject"; a money view built to move funding towards named places is, on its face, a view built for measures, and whether the subjects of those measures are "particular data subjects" is a question for counsel that the book records and does not answer. And section 15: the money view is the coordinate-for-speculation panel — "a leading indicator for housing price changes". **Does not hold as worded; holds as a procedure:** a money view is lit for a scene only after the scene's governance has agreed it, the harm assessment has considered the speculation reading, and the record says both; and the view is shown as Chapter 7 requires, against its own history, never as a ranking.

*Community-held as a kind of nothing.* **Holds with a change, and is now a status with precedents** (section 14): known, used, shown at a coarser resolution or not at all, decided by a named process, reason public, status visible for the category and never for the unit, tested by the walk, reviewed on a date. The change is that version 1 imagined the status on the room; the precedents and the chapter's own rules put it on the category, because a marked room is a disclosure. Chapter 4's carry-forward is discharged: the official vocabularies have no word for it, and conservation, heritage and environmental law have the thing.

*The floor of fifty against the small room.* Chapter 9 left this `[Revisit at end]`. This chapter separates the floors: fifty is a precision floor on a mean and has nothing to do with disclosure; the small room is lost to reliability, not to identification, and it is shown as *present, n answered, not scored*, or as *held*, never as absent — "potential risk should not necessarily be used as a rationale for avoiding local or small area data altogether". The tension is narrowed, not closed: the end of the book still decides whether a room under both floors is shown as unmeasured or held, and who decides which.

*The extraction finding.* Chapter 9 carried Chambers's "mix and balance of extraction and empowerment" as consequences evidence. This chapter gives it a frame — data justice's visibility pillar, CARE's collective benefit, responsible data's "unintended consequences" — and a line: the safeguards record's last, *what this changed*, which asks after launch who benefited from being made visible and who paid.

*Platform terms as transparency questions.* Chapter 8 read platform terms as the dependency's conditions. **Holds, and is widened**: data about identifiable accounts taken from a platform is data not obtained from the subject, owed Article 14's notice or a published reason, and "There is no automatic exception from the right to be informed just because the personal data is in the public domain".

*The studio's own analytics.* The house will want to know who reads the dashboard. PECR's statistics exception "is about how your service is used, not about who uses it"; segmenting readers by demographic "cannot rely on the statistical purposes exception"; wifi probe collection is outside it. **A new position, recorded:** the dashboard's own use is counted as aggregate use, without inferences about categories of reader, or it is counted on consent.

*The studio's own site as a regulated service.* If readers can place pins or write text that other readers see, the Online Safety Act's definition reads as applying, with no size exemption. **A new position, recorded:** contribution is a decision to run a regulated service — a named individual, a risk assessment within three months, a moderation function, a complaints route — at a small service's scale.

*Who is in the room.* The studio's history with a policing form that asked promoters to describe who would attend an event is Carly's knowledge, enters this chapter as frame, and is not researched here. It is the reason the chapter treats any panel that describes the people in a room — by ethnicity, faith, sexuality, health — as a panel whose inference is the controller's, on the regulator's intention test "regardless of how confident you are", and therefore as special-category processing needing a condition, a DPIA and the consultation of section 11; and it is the reason the chapter treats "what onlookers could (rightly or wrongly) infer" from a busy-venues map as a fairness question that the harm assessment must answer in writing.

*A nameable venue.* Most of a nightlife dashboard's small cells are businesses, and data-protection law protects natural persons; a sole trader or a venue named after its owner is a person in the statute's eyes, and a company is not. The statistics code protects "individual and business information" alike, and that is the standard the house adopts for its floors. What a studio owes a nameable business beyond confidentiality — in defamation, in commercial harm from a figure or a colour — was not researched for this chapter and is recorded for counsel `[Further research]`.

*The official reader as intruder.* Chapter 2 named the official reader as one the dashboard serves. This chapter adds that a licensing authority, a police force or a council can read the same panel as a list of premises to visit, and that nothing in the disclosure methods distinguishes that reader from any other. The house's history with a policing form (below) is why the harm assessment's "persons" and "groups" columns ask, for every panel, what a reader with power over the venues could do with it; and why a public-body partner that holds the held layer, the walk's claims or the assessment is asked, before it holds them, whether they can be requested from it under information law `[Further research]`.

*The book's own method.* This chapter's agents read public pages, and the frames of section 17 apply to them. The pages read were those of statutes, regulators, standards bodies, publishers and named organisations, and the content of no individual was fetched or quoted; the one case reached only through an encyclopaedia names a private person who was identified against her will in 2006, and the chapter records the case and does not repeat the identification. The chapter's rule for itself — quote the institution, not the person — is the one it asks of a dashboard.

*"A dashboard is not a research project."* The house has said the dashboard is a civic instrument and not a study. The statute defines research and statistics by method and output, not by actor; the regulator's indicative criteria are hypotheses, scrutiny, publication, ethics and committees; and the exclusions are substantial damage or distress, measures or decisions about particular persons, and — in the regulator's gloss — informing "the services you provide to them". Chapter 1 defined a dashboard as a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader. Whether that decision is a "measure or decision with respect to a particular data subject" is the question on which the dashboard's fit with the research and statistics provisions turns. **Recorded as open, for counsel**: the book cannot answer it and says so.

## 19 · What holds true regardless

Seven truths, each with its grounds; then the record beneath them. They are stated for any public dashboard whose subject is people; the nightlife case in section 21 is one application. Where a truth rests on law, it rests on the law as read on 6 October 2026, reported and not advised.

**1 · Anonymity is a property of the data in its environment, not of the data; a public release is the environment in which control is lost; and the test is an intruder who knows the community, so the disclosure floor is set by the community's knowability, above any national rule.** "not a property of the data"; "when you release information publicly, you lose control of the data"; the motivated intruder "is reasonably competent", may be moved by curiosity about a crime map, and "Identifiability risk can arise where one person or group knows a great deal about another person"; four points identify 95 per cent; the national rules assume an intruder without local knowledge. *(Sections 2, 4, 7, 8; Elliot, Mackey & O'Hara; ICO anonymisation guidance; de Montjoye et al.; Chapter 6.)*

**2 · Aggregation protects persons and not groups; identity, attribute and group disclosure are three different things with three different remedies, and a figure that identifies nobody can still harm everyone it describes.** k-anonymity "can create groups that leak information"; differential privacy protects presence, not conclusions — "It is the conclusions reached in the study that affect the smoker"; the regulator routes group inference to fairness; "types, not tokens"; the mixed-race precinct was undercounted and still predictable. *(Sections 3, 6, 8; Sweeney; Machanavajjhala et al.; Dwork & Roth; Kenny et al.; Taylor, Floridi & van der Sloot †; Mittelstadt; ICO.)*

**3 · A filter is a table builder, and an interactive view is protected as a query system: stricter than a static table, consistent across every view, with fixed classifications or a cap on what can be combined, and either a limit on overlapping views with a log or a consistent cell key that makes repetition useless, and a friendly intruder's walk before release; the status of every cell that is not shown — withheld, unmeasured or held — is stated, even where its rule is not.** "stricter than is applied to outputs which the provider is able to assess individually"; "A minimum cell count equal to five"; registration, a log "audited for potentially suspicious activity", "If possible, … a limit on the number of similar tables"; the same cell "receive[s] the same perturbation"; "will not provide details of which rule"; hiding one field did not stop trilateration. *(Sections 5, 7, 18; ONS births and deaths policy §4.4; ONS Census 2021 methodology; EAP168; Hoang et al.; ONS intruder testing.)*

**4 · A lawful basis is chosen before the first row is collected; the house does not use consent as the basis for what it will publish, because once a figure is published withdrawal cannot reach it; and whichever basis is used, withdrawal is graded, the irreversible part is named, and the person is told before they contribute.** "If you would not be able to fully action a withdrawal of consent … then you should not use consent as your lawful basis"; "no exemption to this for scientific research"; "It shall be as easy to withdraw as to give consent"; "it would not be possible to remove data from research that had already taken place"; the tombstone; "You cannot remove sensitive data from other users' clones". *(Sections 9, 13; UK GDPR Arts 6, 7, 8A(4), 17; ICO consent guidance; ESRC; UK Biobank; Zenodo; GitHub.)*

**5 · A community cannot consent and is never asked to; it is consulted, it governs, and it agrees — three things with three entries in the record — and the models for a community holding its own data are cited for their questions and their structure, never borrowed for their authority.** "Community consultation should not be mistaken for community consent"; "In no case should a collective community agreement … substitute for an individual's informed consent"; "a legitimate political system in place"; CARE as "a normative foundation" for "distinctive communities"; OCAP "specifically First Nations—not Indigenous"; "Less defined types of consent shall be balanced by stronger governance arrangements"; who stood at the door. *(Sections 11, 12; Dickert & Sugarman; UNESCO 2005; CIOMS 2016; Carroll et al.; FNIGC; Te Mana Raraunga; Kukutai & Taylor; McAreavey & Das; Ada Lovelace Institute.)*

**6 · "Known but not shown" is a declared status for a category — with a reason, a resolution, a decider and a review date — never a marker on a unit; the three nothings — withheld, unmeasured, held — are told apart on the face; and the small room is shown as present in a coarser count, never as empty.** "Shown at grid-cell level (400 km2 accuracy)"; "Hidden from all site-level output"; "still seen by our eBird reviewers"; lists "agreed by the UK's statutory bodies" so that partners "do not have to make their own assessments"; a presumption of openness, a named harm, a weighing; "Missing data is not the same as a zero value"; "should not necessarily be used as a rationale for avoiding local or small area data altogether". *(Sections 5, 14; eBird; iNaturalist; NBN Atlas; EIR 2004 reg 12; UKSA geospatial ethics; Chapters 4 and 9.)*

**7 · The harm a dashboard can do runs through representation — labelling a place, making a community findable, turning a figure into a price — and is assessed before launch as harm to persons, to groups and to places, with those affected consulted and the assessment published; and proportion is not exemption: a free, small, non-commercial site is inside data-protection and equality law, and inside online-safety law if the public can write what the public sees, and meets each at its scale.** Geospatial data "has the potential to unfairly disadvantage or blight a geographic area or community"; "harm can also include things like stigmatisation"; the maps "led to reduced home ownership rates, house values, and rents"; venue data "is a leading indicator for housing price changes"; "up to the victim's building"; "Where appropriate, the controller shall seek the views of data subjects or their representatives"; "for payment or not"; "services of all sizes … through to services run by micro businesses". *(Sections 9, 10, 15, 16; UKSA; Aaronson, Hartley & Mazumder; Glaeser, Kim & Luca †; Hoang et al.; UK GDPR Art 35; Equality Act s.29; Online Safety Act s.3; Ofcom.)*

### Beneath the dashboard: the safeguards record

Chapters 4 to 9 each left a record — the provenance set, the recipe card, the claim record, the comparison record, the service record, the measure record — and whether they are one document is for the end of the book `[Revisit at end]`. This chapter leaves a seventh, because none of the six says what was done to protect the people in the data, on what basis, with whose voice, and what it cost them. The *safeguards record* takes its name from the statute's own phrase for what research and statistics must carry — "appropriate safeguards for the rights and freedoms of the data subject" — and has fourteen lines. Its first line is written before the first row is collected; it is dated, it names a person, and it is re-read against the current law on a stated schedule.

| Line | What it states |
|---|---|
| 1 · What is processed, about whom | The unit (person, venue, area, scene); whether each layer is personal, pseudonymised or anonymous information, and on what test; whether special-category data is present or inferable, and whether any inference is the studio's |
| 2 · Lawful basis and its test | The basis per purpose; the legitimate interests assessment or the consent record; whether the research and statistics provisions are relied on, and the counsel's view of the fit; the date the statute was read |
| 3 · Release model and environment | Public, registered, or not shown, per layer; what other data about the same people and places is already public; who else holds what |
| 4 · Disclosure method per view | The disclosure floor per view and how it was set against the scene's knowability; suppression (primary and secondary), rounding, banding or perturbation; fixed classifications or the cap on combinations; consistency across views; the precision floor, separately |
| 5 · Query-time controls | What a reader can combine, move or redefine; the limit on overlapping views; registration for deeper views, if any; the log and who audits it; what is not shown and whether the kind or the rule is stated |
| 6 · The intruder's walk | Who walked, when, for how long, knowing what; each claim and its confidence; what changed before release; the next walk's date |
| 7 · Who was consulted | The DPIA's step 3 and Article 35(9): who, how, when; what they said; what changed; whether the assessment is published |
| 8 · Who governs, and who stood at the door | The scene's body for the held layer and how it was constituted; the gatekeepers through whom it was reached; the form and renewal of its agreement, and what that agreement is understood to add |
| 9 · The held layer | What is held, as a category and never as a marked unit; at what coarser resolution it is shown, if at all; who decided and by what process; the reason, in public, for the category; the walk's test that the held units cannot be recovered from public lists; the review date |
| 10 · Withdrawal | The tiers available to a contributor; the cut-off; what cannot be undone and why; how and when the contributor was told |
| 11 · Harm assessment | Harms to persons (the regulator's list), to groups (inference, discrimination, findability), to places (stigma, speculation, the ecological fallacy); the panels that carry each; the mitigations and the residue; published or not |
| 12 · Regimes in scope | Equality Act (service to the public; accessible format; hosted content); Online Safety Act status, risk tier and the named accountable individual; the children's test applied and its evidence; PECR for the site's own analytics; the emotion-inference line |
| 13 · Moderation and audit | The verbs and adverbs chosen; the gating rules; the reporting and complaints routes; the audit's schedule and last date; what gaming was found |
| 14 · Review, incident, and what this changed | The record's review date against the current law; the plan if things go wrong and the commitment to be transparent about a breach; after launch, who benefited from being made visible and who paid — the extraction question, answered with evidence |

**A filled example, invented for the method.** *1.* Venue-level counts and a felt measure per room; venue rows pseudonymised, published layers aggregate and assessed as anonymous information on the motivated-intruder test with a regular as the intruder; no special-category field collected; an onlooker's inference about a room's regulars noted as a fairness risk. *2.* Legitimate interests for the counted layers, with an assessment on file; the felt layer collected on a basis chosen for publication, not consent; research-provisions fit *for counsel — open*; statute read 6 October 2026. *3.* Public for area and venue layers; held for one scene's rooms at the scene's request; the ticketing platform and two listings sites already publish venue names and events. *4.* Floor of five per view, raised to eight for one district where the panel's walker named two venues at five; secondary suppression across linked views; banded under ten; classifications fixed; the precision floor of fifty stated separately on the felt panel. *5.* Readers may filter by district, month and category from fixed lists; no boundary drawing; no more than two filters combined at venue level; no registration; a request log, kept per session since there is no registration, reviewed monthly by a named person; cells not shown marked by kind. *6.* A promoter from the scene, three hours, with the public site and their own knowledge; four claims, two correct at low confidence, both venues in a category since banded; next walk at the next data release. *7.* Two sessions with the scene's own forum and one with the local authority's data lead; the money view deferred at the scene's request; assessment published without the walker's claims. *8.* The forum's organising group, self-constituted, reached through two promoters named here; agreement renewed yearly; understood as legitimacy, not consent. *9.* The rooms layer for one scene held as a category — *rooms of this kind in this district are shown at district level, at the request of those who run them*; no room marked; the walker could not recover the rooms from the listings sites; decided at session two; review in twelve months. *10.* Three tiers (no further contact; no further use; nothing published from you after date X); the cut-off is each quarterly release; what cannot be undone — figures already on the face and in the archive — told at the point of contribution. *11.* Persons: re-identification by a regular — mitigated by floor and walk, residue stated; groups: an onlooker's inference about who frequents a room — the rooms layer held; places: the money view read as a listing — deferred; stigma by colour — no red, no dark for low; published. *12.* Service to the public, free; table twin for every map; no hosted contributions at launch, so read as outside the Online Safety Act's definition — counsel's view on file; to be re-read if contribution is added; children's test: not more probable than not, evidence from site analytics at three months; analytics aggregate only; no emotion inference. *13.* No contributions at launch; the felt measure's answers gated by in-room recruitment; complaints by email to a named person; audit quarterly. *14.* Review in six months against the current text; incident plan on file; at twelve months, ask the scene what visibility did — *not yet*.

The example is a shape and not a plan; its numbers, its tiers and its scene are placeholders. Several of its lines say "open" or "not yet", and a record whose lines say so is one a sceptical reader, a regulator or a scene can trust more than one that does not.

## 20 · Where the choice is yours

*The lawful basis, and whether the research and statistics provisions are claimed at all.* The chapter reports seven bases and two exclusions; which fits the studio's facts is for the studio and its counsel. The chapter requires only that lines 1 and 2 be written before the first row, and that line 2 carry the date the law was read.

*The disclosure floor's value, per view.* Three is a static-table convention, five the office's suggestion for online tools, and the scene's knowability the reason to go higher. The procedure is Fixed; the number is the studio's, per view, and is stated.

*Suppression, rounding, banding or perturbation.* Each is a different kind of nothing in a number's clothing (Chapter 4). The census chose doubt over absence; most tables choose absence; bands keep the small present at the cost of precision. The chapter requires the choice to be made once per layer, declared, and consistent across views.

*Whether a cell that is not shown states its rule or only its kind.* The national office's table builder states the kind ("because of the disclosure rules") and not the rule; Chapter 4 requires the kind. Between the kind and the parameter is a judgement about what an intruder learns from "fewer than five", and it is the studio's.

*Registration for deeper views.* The office's advice is registration, a log and a limit; its own public builder registers nobody and caps variables instead. A public dashboard may keep its first screen open and ask for a name at the second; which views sit behind the door is a design decision the chapter leaves.

*The held layer's resolution, and who decides.* A grid cell, a district, a presence mark, or nothing; a scene's forum, a panel the studio convenes, a public body's list. Section 14 gives the form and section 12 the cautions; the choice of body and of resolution is the scene's and the studio's together.

*The withdrawal tiers and the cut-off.* Three tiers is the biobank's; a quarterly cut-off is the illustration's. The chapter requires that they be named before the first contribution and told at it.

*Whether to host contributions at all.* To let the public write what the public sees is to run a regulated service. The chapter does not say whether the dashboard should; it says what follows if it does.

*Whether to publish the assessment.* The regulator calls it good practice; the walker's claims are a reason to publish a redacted one. The studio decides what is public and what is on file.

*The moderation adverbs.* Automatic or manual, transparent or secret, before or after, central or distributed — Grimmelmann's grammar is complete and the sentence is the studio's.

*Which frame to adopt as a stance.* A studio outside the university and the member bodies is bound by none of section 17's codes and may adopt any; the adoption is recorded as a Nightlife Culture rule, Fixed as a stance, and says which.

## 21 · A nightlife illustration — method only

A scene's organisers say to the studio: *show us, don't expose us.* The chapter's method turns the sentence into a sequence, and the sequence into a record, and reaches no finding about any scene.

The studio writes line 1 before any data moves: what the layers are, what unit each is at, whether any describes people by a protected characteristic or lets an onlooker guess one, and whether the studio is the one making the inference. It writes line 2 with counsel and dates it. It asks what else about these rooms is already public — the listings, the ticketing pages, the socials — because the environment is where anonymity lives, and writes line 3.

It sets the disclosure floor per view by asking the scene's own people what count would let them name the room, and sets it above that; it fixes the classifications a reader may filter by, caps what may be combined at venue level, and decides, once, whether small cells are withheld, banded or perturbed, and whether the same cell will always show the same way. That is line 4. It decides what a reader can move, how many overlapping views they may request, whether the deeper views ask for a name, and whether a withheld cell says its kind or its rule; line 5.

It gives a promoter who knows the rooms the public site, a map and three hours, and asks them to find a person or a room. Each claim is written down with how sure they were. What they found becomes a change before launch. Line 6, with the next walk's date.

It convenes the scene — not a leader, a forum — and the authority's data lead, and asks the assessment's step 3 questions; it writes who came, who did not, who it was reached through, what was said, and what changed. The scene says which rooms it would rather were held, and the studio asks who in the scene can say that and how the saying is renewed. Lines 7 and 8. The held rooms are drawn as present and held, at a resolution the forum chose, with the reason in public and a review date; line 9.

Before anyone contributes to the felt measure, the contribution screen says what can be withdrawn, by when, and what cannot — a figure already on the face, a line in the archive. Line 10.

The studio writes the harm assessment in three columns — persons, groups, places — and for each panel asks: could a regular find someone; could an onlooker guess who frequents a room; could an investor read this as where to buy; does the colour say danger; does a small room read as nothing. The money view waits until the forum has read that column. Line 11, published.

It checks which regimes it is in: a service to the public, free, so the Equality Act and an accessible twin for every map; no hosted contributions at launch, so the online-safety definition is read and set aside with a note to re-read it if contribution is added; the children's test answered with evidence at three months; the site's own analytics aggregate and nothing about who. Line 12. It writes the moderation design even for the day contributions begin, and the audit's schedule; line 13. It writes the date the whole record is re-read against the law, the plan for the day something goes wrong, and the promise to say so in public if it does; and twelve months after launch it goes back to the forum and asks what being visible did — who came, who was priced, who stopped answering — and writes it down. Line 14.

None of that is a finding. It is the shape of a dashboard that could show a scene what it is and be trusted by the scene to have asked first.

## What proves a break works

The test for a departure from one of this chapter's rules has two halves, and both are conversations. **First, give the view and its record to someone who knows the scene and was not consulted, and ask them to find a person or a room in it in an hour.** If they cannot, or can only with low confidence about a unit the record already names as a residual risk, the disclosure half of the break has cost nothing. If they can, the floor, the classifications or the walk has failed, whatever the rule said. **Second, give the same view and the record to someone in the scene who was described by it and was not asked, and listen for three things.** Do they see, from the face, that the small rooms are present and not erased — and that the held ones were held at someone's request and not hidden by the studio? Do they see who was consulted and through whom, and would they have gone through the same door? And can they tell, from what the record says about harm, whether an investor reading the same view would learn where to buy? If all three, the voice half of the break has cost nothing — and this is a test of whether the face and the record are legible, not of whether the law was met, which the record and counsel carry.

For a departure from the floor, the test is the intruder's hour, with the floor's new value. For a departure from the held layer's resolution, it is the scene's own reading of the coarser map. For a departure from the withdrawal tiers, it is a contributor asked, before contributing, to say back what they could and could not take back. For a departure from "no ranking", it is the money view shown to the scene with the speculation column of the harm assessment beside it.

## Design system (Style Guide v1.5) and Decision Book — conflict log

**Agrees.** Suppression with secondary suppression (Decision Book Chapter 8) is this chapter's rule for static views. DSH-15 ("Gaps render as gaps in every filter state") and DSH-16 ("Carry uncertainty through filtering, or suppress below a base threshold") are the disclosure floor and the precision floor stated for every view; this chapter adds that the floor for an interactive view is higher than for a table. COL-17's text-coded status chips are the right furniture for *withheld*, *unmeasured* and *held*. BRF-04's "affected, influential, exposed" are the three names in the record's lines 7 and 8. The seven tests' "Dancefloor — retains cultural truth, avoids institutionalising the culture?" and "Partner — does the language make room for collaboration and recognise others' contribution?" are section 12 applied to a surface.

**Conflicts.** One direct. Version 1 and the Decision Book require a withheld cell to say *why*; the national office's practice, for a stated reason, is to say neither the rule nor the parameter, and this chapter narrows the house rule to the *kind* of reason — disclosure floor, precision floor, held — with the rule and parameter optional. The Decision Book's text should carry that narrowing, or state that the house publishes its parameters as policy and accepts the office's stated risk. Three gaps that will become conflicts if unaddressed. The Decision Book treats the identifying threshold as one number; an interactive dashboard needs one per view, higher than a table's, and a procedure for setting it against the scene's knowability. The Decision Book's disclosure chapter protects tables and has no rule for a filter as a query system — fixed classifications, a cap on combinations, a limit on overlapping views, a log, the walk — so the prototype would ship a table builder protected as a table. And neither the Decision Book nor the design system has the held layer as a status with a decider, a resolution, a reason and a review date, so a held room would be drawn as empty or not drawn. **Logged for the design system:** a held-status component, distinguishable from withheld and unmeasured, with its reason reachable; a three-nothings pattern for one face; a safeguards-record panel; a withdrawal notice at the point of contribution; a stigma check in the colour rules (no red or dark for low; no large empty areas dominating). **Logged for the Decision Book:** a chapter on safeguards — the record's fourteen lines as rules; the floor per view; the query-time controls; the walk; lawful basis before the first row; the DPIA's consultation step and publication; consult, govern, agree; the held status; withdrawal tiers; the three-column harm assessment; the Online Safety Act decision; the site's own analytics; the twelve-month question — or an extension of Chapters 8 and 15 that carries them.
