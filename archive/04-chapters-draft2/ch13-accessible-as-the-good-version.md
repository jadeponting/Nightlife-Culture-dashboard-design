# Chapter 13 — Accessible as the good version

*Dashboard Design · Part 3: The reading experience*
*Version 2, draft 2 · 6 October 2026 · research log, Entry 13*

---

## Before you start

This is the thirteenth chapter of a research book written for Nightlife Culture, a creative policy studio led by Carly Heath, about how dashboards work and why. The book exists to support the Vibe Map — a planned public, non-commercial, interactive account of the people, activity, value and support systems that sustain a place's nightlife culture, built first for the West of England. You do not need to have read the earlier chapters. Where this one leans on them it says so and gives the finding in a line, and it argues from its own sources.

The chapter's conventions are the book's. Every finding is marked by **kind** — *Standard* (a published rule from a standards body, a regulator, a design system or a method text, with the sub-kind named: Standard (W3C), Standard (law), Standard (EU law), Standard (regulator), Standard (official statistics), Standard (design system)), *Evidence* (a measured result, with what was measured and on whom), *Reading* (this book's interpretation of evidence or standards), *Practice* (what a named project does, read for method), or *Nightlife Culture* (the house's own position) — and by **tier**: *Fixed* (holds in any dashboard), *Adaptable* (holds unless a stated reason overrides it) or *Decorative* (open). "Fixed as a stance" means the house has decided it and the chapter gives the grounds; "Fixed as a procedure" means the rule is a thing to do. "Evidence (official statistics)" marks a figure from a national survey. A dagger (†) marks a figure or quotation taken from an abstract or a secondary account rather than from the paper's body; "secondary" marks a source read only through another; "(read once)" marks a quotation returned once by the fetch tool without a presence check. Findings in the body carry their kind in italics at the end of a paragraph; tiers are given in the rules table. Flags in square brackets — `[Further research]`, `[Prototype flag]`, `[Validate with users]`, `[Revisit → Ch n]`, `[Revisit at end]`, `[Rich reference]`, `[Counterbalance → Ch n]` — are defined in Chapter 0.

This chapter reports law. It reports what a statute, a regulation, a code or a piece of official guidance says, with its wording, its section and its date as read on 6 October 2026. It never says what the law requires of the Vibe Map or of the studio; it quotes the texts that bear on a private, non-commercial provider of a public information service and leaves their application to the studio's own adviser, and it says so wherever a reader might otherwise take a sentence as advice.

The chapter's working words. *WCAG* is the W3C's Web Content Accessibility Guidelines; version 2.2 is a W3C Recommendation, republished 12 December 2024, whose rules are *success criteria* at three *levels*, A, AA and AAA; a page *conforms* at a level when it meets every criterion at that level and below, whole, not in part. A *conforming alternate version* is the standard's name for a separate accessible version of the same content; the W3C calls it a fallback, and section 3 quotes it. A *text alternative* is text programmatically tied to something that is not text; for a chart the W3C asks for two parts, a *short description* that names it and a *long description* that carries its information; an *alt attribute* is the HTML field that usually holds the short one. *Assistive technology* is software or hardware a disabled person uses to read or operate a page; a *screen reader* is the kind that speaks or brailles what is on it. *ARIA* is the W3C's vocabulary of roles and properties that tells assistive technology what a thing on a page is; *COGA* is the W3C's note on content for people with cognitive and learning disabilities. A *navigable structure* is a chart exposed to a screen reader as a tree the reader steps through, rather than as one sentence or one flat table. *Sonification* is data rendered as non-speech sound; an *audio graph* is a platform's way of letting a screen reader play a chart. The *table twin* is Chapter 8's rule that every map, and Chapter 11's that every dense chart, has the same figures as a table reachable before any script. The *four-level model* is a published account of what a chart description can contain, from the chart's looks (level 1) through its statistics (2) and its trends (3) to its meaning in the world (4); section 5 gives it. The *Regulations* are the Public Sector Bodies (Websites and Mobile Applications) (No. 2) Accessibility Regulations 2018, S.I. 2018/952, as amended; *the Act* is the Equality Act 2010; the *anticipatory duty* is the name the Act's explanatory notes give to the duty to make reasonable adjustments for "disabled persons generally", in advance of any individual asking. The *three user groups* are the studio's testing groups, named in its design repository and not here. An *overlay* is a third-party script added to a site that claims to make it accessible by altering the page for the visitor. The *ELS* is the ONS's Explore Local Statistics service (Chapter 12). The *floor* is version 1's name for the set of accessibility requirements the house adopted from its design system and its Decision Book — keyboard operation including chart elements, visible focus, 200 and 400 per cent zoom, semantic structure, alt text generated from the current state, user override of colour, contrast and font, and observed testing — which this chapter maps to the criteria. *View*, *layout* and *route* are used as in Chapter 11: a view is one chart or panel with its data, specification and selection; a layout is the page as presented at one screen size; a route is one way a reader reaches a view's content. "Version 1" is the first draft of this book, archived; its Chapter 11 is this chapter's predecessor and is cited as a set of positions to test.

A note on sources. Four research agents worked with web search rationed to thirty calls each, and the fetch tool cuts long single-page specifications, so the W3C's normative text was read through its Understanding pages, which reproduce it, and the United Kingdom's law through legislation.gov.uk regulation by regulation. Three things could not be read at source and are marked: the body of the EHRC's new 2026 Code's chapter on the anticipatory duty; the CSS specification's definition of the reduced-motion preference; and the aria-live property's table. Everything quoted was checked by presence re-fetch except where "(read once)" says otherwise, and the verifier's pass is recorded in the log.

## The question

What does it take for the accessible version of a public dashboard to be the primary version and not a parallel one — which of the W3C's documents are hard lines and which are guidance, and what each says about an interactive display of data; what the United Kingdom's law says, in the statute's and the regulator's words, about a private, non-commercial provider of a public information service and about the public bodies that would embed or cite its work; what is measured about how blind, low-vision, cognitively disabled and otherwise disabled readers read charts and maps, and which non-visual routes have evidence behind them; what official statistics bodies do about their own dashboards and what they admit they cannot do; and whether the claim that the good version and the accessible version converge is measured anywhere, or is a stance?

---

## The 60-second version

**The standard already says it.** WCAG 2.2's own conformance text: "providing an alternate version is a fallback option for conformance to WCAG and the preferred method of conformance is to make all content directly accessible"; a page conforms "for full web page(s) only, and cannot be achieved if part of a web page is excluded"; and the Working Group, writing of organisations that keep "formats designed for hard copy as the 'primary' version", says it "feels these approaches should be deprecated" though "it does not feel they can be forbidden so long as accessible versions are readily available". Version 1's rule — no parallel accessible version — is the standard's stated preference before it is the house's rule; the house makes it a rule. The United States Department of Justice's note on its public-sector web rule says the same in plainer words, and the one practitioner page on text-only sites says they "accommodate users with some disabilities but neglect other types".

**No rule names a chart.** Not one WCAG criterion names one in its normative text (the Understanding pages do); ARIA has no chart role and the one module that reaches graphics says chart annotation is "Future work"; the W3C's two community groups in the area closed in 2023; the chart-roles proposal is a wiki last edited in 2015. A chart meets the standard by readings of general criteria, and the W3C has written those readings down: a short description that identifies the chart and a long description that gives "scales, values, relationships and trends", with the data "where possible and practical" in a table; graphical objects such as "each line in a graph" at 3:1 contrast unless labels carry the same information; a filter's result announced by a brief status message ("18 results returned") that is not the results themselves; auto-updating information with a mechanism to pause it; motion that can be disabled, which is AAA and is the house's line anyway because "The impact of animation on people with vestibular disorders can be quite severe". COGA, read in its body, is specific where WCAG is silent — "Reinforce numbers with non-numerical concepts"; "Look at the most common 1500 words or phrases"; a summary readable at "lower secondary education level" — and one of its user needs asks *for* graphs: "I need contextually-relevant graphs and pictures to supplement text."

**The law, reported.** The Regulations bind public sector bodies and presume conformity where a site "conforms to Level A and AA Success Criteria as set out in the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines … as amended from time to time" — no version number in the law; WCAG 2.2 AA is the government's stated standard and the monitoring team "has been monitoring for this standard since October 2024". They do not apply to "third-party content that is neither funded nor developed by, nor under the control of, the public sector body" — which is the sentence that re-bases version 1's "distribution argument": a public body that embeds the studio's free dashboard is exempt from the Regulations for it, must say so in its statement, and is in scope the moment it funds it. The text that bears on a private provider is the Equality Act's: a service "to the public or a section of the public (for payment or not)"; a duty whose reasonable steps "include steps for ensuring that in the circumstances concerned the information is provided in an accessible format"; owed to "disabled persons generally", which the explanatory notes call "an anticipatory duty". No EHRC text read names WCAG — not its websites guidance, nor the chapters read of the new statutory Code that came into force on 5 August 2026 and revoked the 2011 one; no reported judgment of a UK court on a website case was found; the public record is settlements of £3,000 and £5,000 and ninety-three EHRC letters. The European Accessibility Act "will not directly apply to the UK", reaches websites through listed services such as e-commerce "with a view to concluding a consumer contract", and exempts microenterprises providing services. None of this is advice.

**What is measured about disabled readers.** Screen-reader users "extract information 61.48% less accurately and spend 210.96% more time" on online charts than sighted users (36 against 36). What blind readers want from a description is the statistics and the trends: with 30 blind and 90 sighted readers, "blind readers rank content and Levels 2 and 3 as most useful" and "A majority of blind readers (63%, n=19) were emphatic that descriptions should not contain an author's subjective interpretations, contextual information, or editorializing about the visualized data"; in practice "only 50% of alt texts in our sample contain information about extrema or outliers" and "only 31% contain information about major trends or comparisons", and authors who start from machine-written alt text write worse alt text than from a blank box. The navigable-structure study's only significant quantitative result favoured the plain table on ease of use; in an audit of 87 live visualisations, tables were the only type of which a majority (7 of 13) were rated moderately to extremely accessible, and maps the lowest of the main types (1 of 15); and in the one experiment that tested the table as a map's substitute, blind participants answered geographic questions right 44 per cent of the time with an interactive text map and 23 per cent with the table — with lower workload on the table. Sonification: a twenty-minute training "can reduce performance error by 50%"; 63 per cent of 106 screen-reader users had never met it online and about half found it at least somewhat beneficial; in a ten-person comparison "task accuracy did not differ significantly across systems" between a description tree, an audio-tactile tablet and a refreshable tactile display, which was fastest (†). Asked their preferred way to data, 50 of 127 screen-reader users named tactile graphics, the most-named route; seven named the screen reader.

**What official bodies admit.** The Analysis Function: "Dashboards rarely meet full accessibility requirements", so "not publish a dashboard without the data being available in another form"; its own Q&A, "I am yet to find a fully accessible dashboard." Each official dashboard statement read that states a status — UKHSA, the ONS, Fingertips, Public Health Scotland, a London borough — says "partially compliant", and where a fallback is offered it is the data in another form; Public Health Scotland lists its R dashboards' failures criterion by criterion, beginning "Title of the graph is not communicated to screen reader users as it is an image of text." The ONS and the Analysis Function disagree about where a chart's description lives: the ONS mandates an alt attribute of "between 100 and 200 characters" in a fixed formula; the Analysis Function says body text, "never be in both the alt attribute and the body text", and never a caption. Across 1,203 public websites the government monitored, "Accessibility issues were found on nearly all"; ten were clean; the commonest failures were focus visibility, contrast, keyboard and reflow, and three of those four — keyboard, focus and reflow — are the ones the government says automated checkers are unlikely to find ("approximately 30-40%" of issues caught by them). Among a million home pages in February 2026, 95.9 per cent had detectable failures, and pages using ARIA had more errors than pages without.

**Convergence, measured narrowly.** Sixty-one non-disabled users performed better and rated a site better at high WCAG conformance than at low (†); fifty-five visually impaired and fifty-five matched non-disabled users showed the same direction (†); typefaces that read better for dyslexic readers read better for everyone (97), while the fonts designed for dyslexia made no difference in three studies; about six million of the 7.5 million who "had used" television subtitles in 2006 had no hearing impairment; the Service Standard says "Inclusive, accessible services are better for everyone." The same laboratory found that easy-to-read language cost non-disabled users reading time and liking (secondary). Nothing measures convergence for charts. So "accessible as the good version" is a stance with evidence for pages and a gap for charts, and the prototype is where the first measurement of that gap can be made.

Beneath the dashboard the chapter leaves a tenth record, which is also a public document: the *accessibility statement*, in the model's form, with a line per view for each route — description, structure, table and what it cannot carry, audio, motion and update controls — and the declared non-compliances, the test date, the technology tested with, and the contact. The pass test is three walks and a question: unplug the mouse; zoom to 400 per cent; read the page with a screen reader; then ask a blind reader not what the chart looks like but what the trend and the figures are — and, for a map, where — and compare the answer with a sighted reader's on those things.

## 1 · What "accessible as the good version" means

Version 1 made one claim in three forms: that the accessible version of this dashboard is not a parallel thing to maintain but the product itself, because the written account is the screen-reader route, the plain register is cognitive access, and a dashboard about sound has a native non-visual channel. This chapter tests the claim's three legs separately, because they stand on different ground.

**The first leg is a standard's.** The W3C's conformance model does not forbid a separate accessible version; it names it a fallback and says which way it prefers. That is section 3's first finding, and it means the house's rule against a parallel version is an adoption of the standard's own stance, with the standard's own reasons.

**The second leg is the law's.** The Regulations define a public sector body and exempt most non-governmental organisations; the Equality Act's duty to make reasonable adjustments is owed to a service's users "for payment or not" and to disabled people generally, in advance. Section 4 reports the texts and leaves their application to the studio's adviser. The claim that a public body "inherits" a non-compliant embed, which version 1 used to make accessibility a distribution decision, meets a regulation that exempts third-party content the body neither funds, develops nor controls; the argument survives in a different shape.

**The third leg is empirical, and it is the one the house will be asked about.** Does building for the disabled reader make the page better for everyone? Section 5 reads what is measured: two experiments from one laboratory on whole pages, one on typefaces, reviews on captions, and a counter-finding on easy-to-read text. Nothing on charts. The convergence claim is therefore a stance with partial evidence, and the chapter says so rather than letting version 1's "keep turning out to be the same build" stand as a finding.

What the three legs share is the thing the brief's failure register names twice — "people dont use it" and "people dont trust it" — read for the disabled reader: in a survey of 127 screen-reader users, 73 per cent did not agree that the data-driven media they met was typically accessible, and, asked about pandemic information, 94 per cent "agreed they have concerns about accessing accurate information in a timely manner" (Fan et al. 2023). A dashboard that some of its readers cannot read is not used by them and not trusted by them; how many that is depends on the dashboard, and the United Kingdom's count of disabled people is "16.8 million (25%)" in 2023 to 2024, of whom "more than 2 million" live with sight loss (Family Resources Survey; House of Commons Library quoting the NHS). *(Evidence (official statistics).)*

## 2 · What people believe about accessibility, and what the evidence says

Eight beliefs, common in the trade, each tested below.

**"WCAG is the law."** It is the standard a public body is presumed to conform by, named in the Regulations without a version number, and it appears in no EHRC text read; for anyone else the text that bears is the Equality Act's duty, which names no standard at all. *(Section 4.)*

**"A chart needs an alt text sentence."** The W3C asks for two parts, a short identifier and a long description with values, relationships and trends and, where practical, the data; the ONS asks for a 100–200 character alt attribute in a formula; the Analysis Function says body text, never the attribute as well. In the one measured sample, half of alt texts omit the extrema and seven in ten the trend. *(Sections 3, 5, 6.)*

**"A table makes a chart accessible."** For values, it is the type of which most examples were rated accessible in the one audit; for shape and geography it is a loss, measured: 23 per cent right on geographic questions against 44 with a text map. *(Section 5.)*

**"A public body cannot embed non-compliant content."** The Regulations exempt third-party content the body neither funds, develops nor controls; the body must say so in its statement; bodies handle it three ways in practice. *(Section 4.)*

**"No dashboard can be made accessible."** An Analysis Function answer has "yet to find a fully accessible dashboard", and each official statement read is "partially compliant" — of built products on particular tools. The chapter's reading is that the record is of tools and habits, not a proof about the form; no fully conforming public dashboard was found either, and the chapter's own filled example opens "partially compliant", so the claim the house can make is that it declares what fails and keeps the routes around it. *(Sections 5, 6.)*

**"Sonification solves it."** It gives point estimates with training and, its vendors say, overview; about half of surveyed screen-reader users found it at least somewhat beneficial; those users found it of limited value for maps; and in the one comparison accuracy did not differ by system. It is an extra, labelled. *(Section 5.)*

**"A text-only version helps."** The standard calls it a fallback; the practitioner page says it neglects other disabilities and goes stale; nobody has measured the staleness. *(Sections 3, 6.)*

**"An overlay fixes the site."** An overlay is a script bolted onto a site that claims to make it accessible. Sixty-seven per cent of practitioners rated them not effective, 72 per cent of practitioners with disabilities; a United States regulator settled with one vendor for $1 million in 2025 over claims that its widget made sites compliant. No UK regulator has spoken. *(Section 6.)*

## 3 · The W3C documents, classified for a data display

Chapter 3 sorted the W3C's documents into hard lines and guidance and listed the criteria that bite on a chart's perception. This section does the rest: the conformance model, the criteria that bite on an *interactive* display, the ARIA vocabulary that does and does not exist for charts, COGA's body text, and the status of what is coming.

### The conformance model, and the alternate version

WCAG 2.2 is a W3C Recommendation, republished 12 December 2024, and now an ISO standard too (ISO/IEC 40500:2025 is the October 2023 text). Its five conformance requirements decide what "accessible" means for a page. Conformance level: for AA, the page "satisfies all the Level A and Level AA success criteria, or a Level AA conforming alternate version is provided", with the note that "It is not recommended that Level AAA conformance be required as a general policy for entire sites because it is not possible to satisfy all Level AAA success criteria for some content". Full pages: "Conformance (and conformance level) is for full web page(s) only, and cannot be achieved if part of a web page is excluded" — and "A full page includes each variation of the page that is automatically presented by the page for various screen sizes", so the phone layout conforms or the page does not. Complete processes: every page in a sequence conforms. Accessibility-supported ways only: "Any information or functionality that is provided in a way that is not accessibility supported is also available in a way that is accessibility supported." Non-interference: four criteria "apply to all content" even where it does not conform — "1.4.2 - Audio Control, 2.1.2 - No Keyboard Trap, 2.3.1 - Three Flashes or Below Threshold, and 2.2.2 - Pause, Stop, Hide". *(Standard (W3C).)*

The conforming alternate version is defined: a version that conforms, "provides all of the same information and functionality in the same human language", "is as up to date as the non-conforming content", and can be reached by one of three routes, the first that "the conforming version can be reached from the non-conforming page via an accessibility-supported mechanism". And then the sentence that settles version 1's rule: "Note that providing an alternate version is a fallback option for conformance to WCAG and the preferred method of conformance is to make all content directly accessible." The Working Group's reasons for permitting one are new technologies not yet supported, content that cannot be changed for legal or historical reasons, legacy documents, and the case where "the best experience for users with certain types of disabilities is provided by tailoring a web page specifically to accommodate that disability"; and its view of organisations that keep "formats designed for hard copy as the 'primary' version" is that "the Working Group feels these approaches should be deprecated" though "it does not feel they can be forbidden so long as accessible versions are readily available". "Alternate versions should not be confused with supplementary content, which support the original page and enhance comprehension." *(Standard (W3C); the orchestrator re-fetched the fallback sentence.)* The phrase "last resort", which circulates, is not the W3C's.

The reading: a dashboard conforms as one page per layout or not at all; a table twin, a long description and a written account are *supplementary content* on the same page, which is what the house builds, not an alternate version; and a separate "accessible dashboard" would be the fallback the standard names and prefers not to see. The standard permits the fallback and gives reasons, one of them that a tailored page can serve some readers best; the house's rule is stricter than the standard's preference and is marked as the house's. The United States' Department of Justice says it for public bodies there, in its fact sheet on its rule: "Usually state and local governments should not have a main web page that is inaccessible and a separate accessible version of the same content, because people with disabilities should get equal access to that content on the same page" (ADA Title II rule fact sheet, 2024; United States law, reported for its wording).

Partial conformance exists for content "legitimately outside the author's control": "the author can make a claim of partial conformance", and "It is important to recognize that this is a statement of non-conformance". That is the W3C's version of the Regulations' third-party exemption (section 4), and it carries the same duty: "it is necessary to identify the non-conforming parts of the page for users". *(Standard (W3C).)*

### The criteria that bite on an interactive data display

Chapter 3 holds 1.1.1, 1.3.1, 1.4.1, 1.4.3, 1.4.10, 1.4.11 and 3.1.5; Chapter 11 holds 1.4.13, 2.1.1, 2.5.1, 2.5.7 and 2.5.8. The rest, in the standard's words with the Understanding pages' reasons, grouped by what they govern on a dashboard. All are Standard (W3C), normative text reproduced on the Understanding pages.

*The chart's text alternative, in full.* 1.1.1's Understanding page has a chart example in four sentences: "A bar chart compares how many widgets were sold in June, July, and August." "The short label says, 'Figure one - Sales in June, July and August.'" "The longer description identifies the type of chart, provides a high-level summary of the data, trends and implications comparable to those available from the chart." "Where possible and practical, the actual data is provided in a table." Technique G92, sufficient for 1.1.1: "The short alternative identifies the non-text content; the long alternative provides the information"; and its description's test of equivalence — "If the non-text content were removed from the page and substituted with the short and long descriptions, the page would still provide the same function and information." Its two worked examples are a prose-only description ordered by the chart's purpose ("The primary use of the chart is to show leaders, so the description is in sales order") and one that "includes the data table that was used to generate the line graph". The WAI tutorial on complex images, revised 8 April 2026, lists "graphs and charts" and "maps" among complex images, says of its chart example that "The long description provides detailed information, including scales, values, relationships and trends that are represented visually", and now gives three approaches, not four, and no longdesc: a text link adjacent to the image; the location described in the alt attribute; or the image and its long description wrapped together so that "The long description (presented as headings, text, and a table) is wrapped in the figcaption element". Its warning about the alternative: "The element referenced by aria-describedby is treated as one continuous paragraph of text", so headings and tables inside a description are lost that way. Its worked long description has three parts, Overview, Values and Presentation, and the Values part is a table. One tension is logged: the tutorial's third approach wraps the description in a figcaption, and the Analysis Function (section 6) warns that a description "should also never be in a caption as screen readers do not always pick up on these"; the house follows the Analysis Function and tests the figure element with the screen readers it names. The definition of *text alternative* itself has a chart for its example: "An image of a chart is described in text in the paragraph after the chart. The short text alternative for the chart indicates that a description follows."

*Graphics' contrast, and which parts need it.* 1.4.11's Understanding names charts: a graphical object includes "the important parts of a more complex diagram such as each line in a graph"; "Not every graphical object needs to contrast with its surroundings - only those that are required for a user to understand what the graphic is conveying"; and the contrast requirement does not apply where "A graphic with text embedded or overlaid conveys the same information, such as labels and values on a chart", or "The information is available in another form, such as in a table that follows the graph", or the presentation is essential — "Color gradients that represent a measurement, such as heat maps." The pie chart is the page's case study: labels present but slices not at 3:1 fails; "visible labels and values that convey equivalent information to the graphical objects" makes the criterion not applicable. For a line chart, "The graphical objects are the lines in the graph, including the background lines for the values, and the colored lines with shapes."

*Text as text, and the size of it.* 1.4.4 Resize Text (AA): "Except for captions and images of text, text can be resized without assistive technology up to 200 percent without loss of content or functionality", because "Images of text do not scale as well as text because they tend to pixelate". 1.4.12 Text Spacing (AA): no loss when line height is set to 1.5 times the font size, paragraph spacing to 2, letter spacing to 0.12 and word spacing to 0.16 — and the page's note that matters for charts, "For this SC, canvas implementations of text are considered to be images of text", while SVG is not mentioned. 1.4.5 Images of Text (AA) is section 7's subject and is quoted there. 1.3.2 Meaningful Sequence: "a correct reading sequence can be programmatically determined", because content read "in the wrong order" confuses, with the technique "Making the DOM order match the visual order". 1.3.3 Sensory Characteristics: instructions "do not rely solely on sensory characteristics of components such as shape, color, size, visual location, orientation, or sound" — "the darkest shape" is an instruction of that kind unless it is also said in words, which Chapter 11's rule already requires; and the page adds that shape and location "should not" be discouraged "as long as the information is also provided in other ways".

*Keyboard and focus.* 2.1.2 No Keyboard Trap: focus that can enter a component can leave it "using only a keyboard interface" — the cause named is "when custom components and widgets are not implemented with keyboard users in mind", which is a chart library's tooltip layer. 2.4.3 Focus Order: focus "in an order that preserves meaning and operability", with arrow-key movement inside a tree out of its scope. 2.4.7 Focus Visible (AA): "a mode of operation where the keyboard focus indicator is visible" — the commonest failure in the government's simplified tests across 1,203 sites (section 4). 2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (AA): the focused component "is not entirely hidden due to author-created content" — "sticky footers, sticky headers, and non-modal dialogs" are the named culprits, and a dashboard's pinned filter bar is one. 2.4.13 (AAA) sizes and contrasts the indicator — "at least as large as the area of a 2 CSS pixel thick perimeter" and "a contrast ratio of at least 3:1" — and the house takes it as its own line, as it takes 2.3.3 below, because an indicator that cannot be seen is the failure the public record names first. 2.5.2 Pointer Cancellation: the function completes on the up-event or can be undone, so a brush that fires on mouse-down fails.

*Motion, movement and updating.* 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide (A), which applies to all content regardless of conformance: for moving or scrolling information that "starts automatically", "lasts more than five seconds" and "is presented in parallel with other content", "a mechanism for the user to pause, stop, or hide it unless the movement, blinking, or scrolling is part of an activity where it is essential"; and — the clause a live dashboard meets — "For any auto-updating information that (1) starts automatically and (2) is presented in parallel with other content, there is a mechanism for the user to pause, stop, or hide it or to control the frequency of the update unless the auto-updating is part of an activity where it is essential." Five seconds "was chosen because it is long enough to get a user's attention, but not so long that a user cannot wait out the distraction". 2.3.1 Three Flashes (A): nothing "that flashes more than three times in any one second period". 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (AAA): "Motion animation triggered by interaction can be disabled, unless the animation is essential", with the reason — "The impact of animation on people with vestibular disorders can be quite severe"; "Triggered reactions include nausea, migraine headaches, and potentially needing bed rest to recover" — and the boundary: 2.2.2 is for animation the page starts, 2.3.3 for animation the reader's action starts. The sufficient technique is the CSS reduced-motion preference (C39), whose specification sentence could not be read at source this sprint; its intent, from the technique, is that "Media queries that selectively enable/disable CSS-driven animations based on operating system or user agent preferences allow users to avoid those triggers". Chapter 3's "one staged transition per reshape" and Chapter 11's rule that nothing crucial sits behind motion meet here: the house honours the reduced-motion preference, and treats 2.3.3 as its own line though the standard puts it at AAA. *(Standard (W3C); Reading for the house line.)*

*Predictability, and what a filter may do.* 3.2.1 On Focus: receiving focus "does not initiate a change of context". 3.2.2 On Input: "Changing the setting of any user interface component does not automatically cause a change of context unless the user has been advised of the behavior before using the component" — and the page's definition of a change of context is a major change "that, if made without user awareness, can disorient users who are not able to view the entire page simultaneously", while "A change of content is not always a change of context"; its closest example to a filter is a select that reveals fields: "Because only parts of the entry change and the overall structure remains the same, the basic context remains for the user." So a filter that re-draws a panel in place is a change of content; one that moves the reader to a new page on change is a change of context and is announced first. 3.3.2 Labels or Instructions: every option labelled "so that users know what they are actually selecting"; "Too much information or instruction can be just as harmful as too little."

*Name, role, value, and the announced result.* 4.1.2: for every user interface component "the name and role can be programmatically determined", states "can be programmatically set", and "notification of changes to these items is available to user agents, including assistive technologies" — the criterion "primarily for web authors who develop or script their own user interface components", which a custom chart is. 4.1.3 Status Messages (AA): "status messages can be programmatically determined through role or properties such that they can be presented to the user by assistive technologies without receiving focus". A status message is "change in content that is not a change of context, and that provides information to the user on the success or results of an action". The distinction a dashboard needs: "the list of results obtained from a search are not considered a status update and thus are not covered by this success criterion", but brief messages about them — "'Searching...', '18 results returned' or 'No results returned'" — are; and the caution: "there is a risk of making an application too 'chatty' for a screen reader user"; "User testing should be carried out to ensure the appropriate level of feedback is achieved"; "The purpose of this success criterion is not to force authors to generate new status messages." The technique is role=status, "an implicit aria-live value of polite", read "without the AT having to focus on the place where the text is displayed". *(Standard (W3C).)* The reading for a filter: when a selection changes a view, one brief status message says what changed and that the view's sentence has changed — "District changed; 3 of 20 shown" — and neither the view nor the sentence is read out unasked; the reader goes to the sentence.

### What ARIA has, and has not, for a chart

WAI-ARIA 1.2 is a Recommendation of 6 June 2023; 1.3 is a Working Draft of 4 June 2026. Its roles that a dashboard uses: *status* ("A type of live region whose content is advisory information for the user but is not important enough to justify an alert"); *alert*; *table* ("A section containing data arranged in rows and columns"); *grid* ("A composite widget containing a collection of one or more rows with one or more cells where some or all cells in the grid are focusable by using methods of two-dimensional navigation, such as directional arrow keys"); *figure*; *img*; *application*. No role is named chart or graph. The Graphics Module is a Recommendation of 2 October 2018, not a draft, with three roles — *graphics-document*, "A type of document in which the visual appearance or layout of content conveys meaning", which authors should use for "structured graphics such as charts, maps, diagrams"; *graphics-object*; *graphics-symbol* — and its own statement of what it lacks: "Future work will expand on this framework to enable more detailed annotation of data-rich graphics such as charts or maps." The proposed chart roles — graphics-datachart, graphics-axis, graphics-legend, graphics-dataunit — are a W3C wiki page "last edited on 11 September 2015". The Accessible Infographics Community Group closed on 7 April 2023 with no published report; the Data Visualization Community Group on 7 December 2023; the ARIA Working Group's current charter (to 1 January 2027) does not contain the words "chart" or "data visualization". There is no WAI tutorial, note or guidance document dedicated to charts or data visualisation; the complex-images tutorial's chart example is the nearest thing. *(Standard (W3C); the absence verified.)*

The Authoring Practices Guide, informative, supplies the discipline. "No ARIA is better than Bad ARIA"; "A role is a promise"; "Incorrect ARIA misrepresents visual experiences, with potentially devastating effects on their corresponding non-visual experiences"; "ARIA roles do not cause browsers to provide keyboard behaviors or styling"; "Testing assistive technology interoperability is essential before using code from this guide in production." On tables: "a WAI-ARIA table is a static tabular structure … it is not an interactive widget"; "authors are strongly encouraged to use a native HTML table element whenever possible"; the grid "is particularly useful if the tabular information is editable or interactive". On alerts: "it is crucial they do not affect keyboard focus"; "Frequent interruptions inhibit usability for people with visual and cognitive disabilities." On names: "Rule 2: Prefer Visible Text"; and a description given by aria-describedby is "reduced to text strings". *(Standard (W3C), informative.)* The WebAIM Million's finding sits beside this: in February 2026, "Home pages with ARIA present had significantly more errors (59.1 on average) than pages without ARIA (42 on average)" — an association across a million pages, not a cause, and the APG's first sentence is one reading of it.

### COGA, read in its body

*Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities* is a W3C Working Group Note of 29 April 2021, with no later version: "Following this guidance is not required for conformance to WCAG"; its patterns "address accessibility barriers that were not included in the normative WCAG 2.x". Chapter 3 flagged its numeric patterns for reading; they are specific enough to become house rules though not conformance ones.

*Provide Alternatives for Numerical Concepts.* The user need: "I need words rather than numbers and numerical concepts." "Not all people can understand numbers and numerical concepts"; "a user with dyscalculia may have difficulty processing temperature data when presented only in a numeric format"; "Other people have challenges with any calculations such as relative sizes or times." The pattern: "Reinforce numbers with non-numerical concepts, e.g., very cold, cold, cool, mild, warm, hot, very hot", with extra support listed for "sizes, quantity, distance, time, date, temperature, positive/negative, calculations, sequencing, percentages", and the thing to avoid: "Numerical content without extra support." *Do Not Rely on Users Calculations or Memorizing Information.* "Many users have an impaired working memory"; "They cannot remember many details at the same time"; "Each step in a process should contain the information necessary to allow a user to proceed"; avoid "Requiring the user remember a code or a category from a previous step." *Use Clear Words.* "Look at the most common 1500 words or phrases"; "Remove or explain uncommon acronyms, abbreviations, and jargon"; "Do not invent new words or give words new meanings in your application"; "If you must create new terms, make sure the user has access to an explanation within one click or event." *Provide Summary of Long Documents and Media.* "Provide a text summary that can be understood by people with lower secondary education level reading ability"; "A high level outline in a few sentences or bullet points is most effective"; "In pieces of content with less than 300 words, headings can act as a summary." *Make the Purpose of Your Page Clear.* "Page headings that tell the user where they are." *Make It Easy to Find Help and Give Feedback.* "Make it easy for the user to ask for help or report issues at any point in a process"; avoid "A single way to give feedback that not everyone can use." And the pattern on complex information, whose user need asks for graphs: "I need contextually-relevant graphs and pictures to supplement text." "The way information is presented, such as a graph, diagram, or table, may make it more complex for some users", and "a supporting description and guided interpretation will highlight the key features the user needs to understand"; the example to avoid is "A long text and data table of sales figures is shown without any explanation of the key features". *(Standard (W3C), informative Note.)*

The reading, and it is a strong one for this book: COGA is not against charts; it is against numbers and charts *without words*. Chapter 11's sentence on every view and its authored account, Chapter 7's worded comparison ("higher", "similar"), Chapter 9's bands (words for a felt measure's range), Chapter 12's labels, and the ELS's rule that turns a comparison into three words at one median absolute deviation are COGA's "non-numerical concepts" and "guided interpretation" already in the book — and they are on the page for every reader, which is where section 5 will place interpretation. The house's clear-words rule gains a measurable form — the 1,500 most common words, uncommon terms explained within one click — and the "how this argues" page gains a summary at lower-secondary reading level.

### What is coming, and what is not

WCAG 3.0 is a Working Draft of 10 September 2026 — "It is inappropriate to cite this document as other than a work in progress"; "WCAG 3 is not expected to be a completed W3C standard for a few more years"; "WCAG 3 will not supersede WCAG 2 and WCAG 2 will not be deprecated for several years after WCAG 3 is finalized"; "The best way to prepare for WCAG 3 in the future, is to meet WCAG 2.2 success criteria now." Its image guideline so far: "Text alternatives for non-decorative images convey the equivalent purpose to the image." EN 301 549, the European standard the United Kingdom's Regulations once named, is v3.2.1 (2021) in legal effect, which incorporates WCAG 2.1 AA for web; v4.1.1 was published in September 2026 aligned to WCAG 2.2 and, as the Commission's own centre said on 7 September 2026, "is not yet the legal reference standard". The United Kingdom's Regulations no longer name the EN at all (section 4). *(Standard (W3C); Standard (EU law).)*

## 4 · The United Kingdom's law, reported

Nothing in this section is advice. It is the text of the Regulations, the Act, the new Code and the government's guidance, as they read on legislation.gov.uk and gov.uk on 6 October 2026, with the regulation and section numbers as they stand. The brief's own numbering of the Regulations was out by one or more throughout (reg 2 is a revocation), and version 1's "the regulations likely don't bind this instrument" is replaced here by what the regulations say.

### The Regulations: who, what, and the exemption that matters

The Public Sector Bodies (Websites and Mobile Applications) (No. 2) Accessibility Regulations 2018, S.I. 2018/952, in force 23 September 2018, extent the United Kingdom, with "no known outstanding effects" shown. Regulation 3 defines the "accessibility requirement" as "the requirement to make a website or mobile application accessible by making it perceivable, operable, understandable and robust", and a "public sector body" as the State, regional or local authorities, "bodies governed by public law", and certain associations of them. A body governed by public law is one "established for the specific purpose of meeting needs in the general interest, not having an industrial or commercial character", with legal personality, and which is, among other routes, "financed, for the most part, by the State, regional or local authorities, or by other bodies governed by public law". That "financed, for the most part" limb is the only route in the definition's text by which a body outside the State enters it. Regulation 4(1) then exempts, among others, "non-governmental organisations, unless they provide services that— (i) are essential to the public; or (ii) specifically address the needs of, or are meant for, persons with disabilities". The government's gloss: charities and other NGOs are exempt "unless they are mostly financed by public funding, provide services that are essential to the public or aimed at disabled people". *(Standard (law); Standard (regulator).)*

Regulation 4(2) lists the content the Regulations "do not apply to", and two letters bear on this book. "(d) online maps and mapping services, as long as essential information is provided in an accessible digital manner for maps intended for navigational use" — the government's gloss, "maps - but you'll need to provide essential information in an accessible format like an address". The exemption's condition is written for maps "intended for navigational use"; what it asks of a thematic map is not stated, and the house provides the figures and the sentence regardless (sections 5 and 8). And "(e) third-party content that is neither funded nor developed by, nor under the control of, the public sector body". The government's gloss: "third party content that's under someone else's control if you did not pay for it or develop it yourself - for example, social media 'like' buttons", followed by "You'll need to explain in your accessibility statement that you've not made things like this accessible because they are exempt"; and the monitoring page: "If you have funded, developed or are in control of third party content, it is your responsibility to make sure that it is accessible." The wording comes from the European directive the Regulations once implemented, whose recital says embedded content "should be covered", that third-party content the body neither funds, develops nor controls "should be excluded", and that "Such content should, in principle, not be used if it hinders or decreases the functionality of the public service offered". *(Standard (law); Standard (regulator); the directive as origin.)*

Regulation 6: "Subject to regulation 7, public sector bodies must comply with the accessibility requirement." Regulation 7, disproportionate burden: a body need not comply "if doing so would impose a disproportionate burden", but "must perform an assessment" taking account of "the size, resources and nature of the public sector body" and "the estimated costs and benefits for the public sector body in relation to the estimated benefits for persons with disabilities, taking into account the frequency and duration of use", and must then explain in its statement what could not be complied with. The guidance adds what the regulation does not: "You cannot take things like lack of time or knowledge into account in your assessment - or argue that making things accessible is a disproportionate burden because you've not given it priority." Regulation 8: an accessibility statement "in accordance with the model accessibility statement", kept "under regular review", containing "an explanation of those parts of the content that are not accessible and the reasons why", "a description of any accessible alternatives provided", a contact route to report failures and request excluded information, and a link to the enforcement procedure. Regulation 9, as substituted on 26 October 2022: a site "will be presumed to be in conformity with the accessibility requirement to the extent that the website or mobile application conforms to Level A and AA Success Criteria as set out in the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines recommended by the World Wide Web Consortium, as amended from time to time" — no version number, no European standard; the 2022 amendment's explanatory note says it "substitutes for the European accessibility standard (EN 301 549) the international web accessibility standard". The government's statement of the version: "The technical standard for websites and mobile apps is level AA of the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) version 2.2", and its monitoring team "has been monitoring for this standard since October 2024". Regulations 11 and 12 connect the Regulations to equality law: the enforcement bodies are the Equality and Human Rights Commission and, in Northern Ireland, the Equality Commission; and "A failure by a public sector body to comply with the accessibility requirement is to be treated as a failure to make a reasonable adjustment" for the purposes of "sections 20, 21 and 29 of the Equality Act 2010". *(Standard (law); Standard (regulator).)*

### What the monitoring found

The government's report of 17 December 2024 covers January 2022 to September 2024, and its monitoring guidance page describes the method: "We have monitored 1,203 websites and 21 mobile apps during this monitoring period"; "Accessibility issues were found on nearly all tested websites and apps"; "We found no accessibility issues for 10 websites"; "We found 29,787 accessibility issues", of which 55.3 per cent were fixed during monitoring; "At the time of each detailed test, none of the 52 websites were fully compliant". The commonest failures in simplified tests: 2.4.7 Focus Visible 13.3 per cent of issues; 1.4.3 Contrast 13.0; then 2.1.1 Keyboard (10.8), 1.4.10 Reflow (9.2) and 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value (8.6); "The 5 most common criteria account for 55% of the total issues, and the 10 most common criteria account for 80%." The method, from the guidance page: "We test pages using an automated accessibility tool called Axe"; "Automated testing does not find all accessibility issues"; the manual tests, "tabbing through each page without a mouse", zooming, and simulating a phone, "can find issues with keyboard functionality, visibility of keyboard focus and reflow" that "are unlikely to be found using automated testing"; and from the report, "Automated checkers are believed to detect approximately 30-40% of accessibility issues". Disproportionate-burden claims at closure, by type of test: 17 per cent of simplified, 37 of detailed, 57 of mobile apps. Enforcement: "of the 93 public sector bodies in England and Wales that the EHRC has sent initial letters to since 1 January 2022, compliance was secured with 66, without the need for enforcement"; no fines are mentioned. *(Standard (regulator); Evidence (monitoring data).)* `[Rich reference]` The reading: of the four failures that dominate the public sector's own record, three — focus, keyboard, reflow — are the ones a keyboard walk and a zoom find in minutes and the government says a scanner is unlikely to find; the fourth, contrast, is the one a scanner finds best. Both are run.

### The Act: the duty that reaches a studio

The Equality Act 2010, as in force on 5 October 2026 with no textual amendment to the sections below. Section 6(1): "A person (P) has a disability if— (a) P has a physical or mental impairment, and (b) the impairment has a substantial and long-term adverse effect on P's ability to carry out normal day-to-day activities"; "substantial" means "more than minor or trivial". Section 29(1): "A person (a 'service-provider') concerned with the provision of a service to the public or a section of the public (for payment or not) must not discriminate against a person requiring the service by not providing the person with the service"; section 29(7): "A duty to make reasonable adjustments applies to— (a) a service-provider". Section 20(3), the first requirement: "where a provision, criterion or practice of A's puts a disabled person at a substantial disadvantage in relation to a relevant matter in comparison with persons who are not disabled, to take such steps as it is reasonable to have to take to avoid the disadvantage"; 20(5), the third, the auxiliary aid; and 20(6): "Where the first or third requirement relates to the provision of information, the steps which it is reasonable for A to have to take include steps for ensuring that in the circumstances concerned the information is provided in an accessible format." Section 21: "A failure to comply with the first, second or third requirement is a failure to comply with a duty to make reasonable adjustments"; "A discriminates against a disabled person if A fails to comply with that duty in relation to that person." Schedule 2, paragraph 2(2): "the reference in section 20(3), (4) or (5) to a disabled person is to disabled persons generally"; paragraph 2(7): nothing "requires A to take a step which would fundamentally alter— (a) the nature of the service". The word "anticipatory" is not in the Act; it is the explanatory notes': "As the duty is owed to disabled persons generally, it is an anticipatory duty which means service providers and people exercising public functions must anticipate the needs of disabled people and make appropriate reasonable adjustments." *(Standard (law); the notes are not law.)*

The regulator's Code changed while this book was being written. The 2011 Services Code was revoked on 5 August 2026 and the *Code of Practice for services, public functions and associations, 2026* came into force the same day; it "is a statutory Code"; "The Code does not impose legal obligations. Nor is it an authoritative statement of the law: only the courts and tribunals can provide such authority"; but "Courts and tribunals must consider any part of the Code that appears relevant". Its paragraph 3.6: the obligations apply "whether in the private, public or voluntary sectors"; "It does not matter if services are provided free of charge"; and "The obligation also applies to the provision of services on a website (section 29)." Its Chapter 11 on remote provision: "Some goods and services are provided remotely through mediums like websites or apps, for example, where they are delivering information, products or entertainment to the public." Its Chapter 7 on the anticipatory duty could not be read at source (the page refused the tool), and a secondary site's quotations of it — "The duty is anticipatory. This means that service providers … must proactively consider the barriers that disabled people could face"; "the duty to make anticipatory adjustments for a class of people, as well as the continuing duty to make adjustments in individual cases" — are carried as secondary and flagged `[Further research]`. The EHRC's own web guidance, last updated 2019: a provider "must think in advance about what people with a range of impairments might reasonably need", and "Where this is a reasonable adjustment (and, as with other written information, it is likely to be), a website must be accessible to all users". And the negative finding, verified for what was read: neither the 2026 Code's chapter on remote services, nor its first five chapters, nor the EHRC's websites guidance contains the words "WCAG" or "Web Content Accessibility Guidelines"; the Code's remaining chapters were not reached. *(Standard (regulator).)* Where WCAG appears is in the government's texts — the Regulations' presumption, the GOV.UK guidance, the Technology Code of Practice's "You must meet the WCAG 2.2 AA standard as a minimum" for suppliers — and not, in anything read, in the regulator's; what a court would take as the measure of "reasonable" is not a question this chapter answers.

No reported judgment of a United Kingdom court deciding a website-accessibility claim under the Act or its predecessor was found. ### The European Act, and the rest

Directive (EU) 2019/882, the European Accessibility Act, applies to listed services "provided to consumers after 28 June 2025" — electronic communications, audiovisual media access, passenger transport, "consumer banking services", "e-books and dedicated software", and "e-commerce services", defined as services "with a view to concluding a consumer contract". It excludes "online maps and mapping services, if essential information is provided in an accessible digital manner for maps intended for navigational use" and "third-party content that is neither funded, developed by, or under the control of, the economic operator concerned" — the same two carve-outs as the Regulations, almost word for word. "Microenterprises providing services shall be exempt." A website is not itself one of its categories; the text reaches websites through the services listed. The government's answer of 10 October 2024: "The European Accessibility Act (EAA) will not directly apply to the UK and, as such, there are no immediate plans within His Majesty's Government (HMG) to transpose this EU legislation"; "UK companies offering in-scope products or services in the EU will need to comply with its provisions". *(Standard (EU law); Standard (government statement).)* The Equality Act's Schedule 25 applies that Act to a provider's acts in another EEA state "as this Act would apply if the act in question were done by the person in Great Britain". BS 8878:2010, the British process standard, still shows "Current" on its publisher's page, with no supersession notice; ISO/IEC 30071-1:2019 is the international one, "confirmed" in 2024. The Procurement Act 2023's technical-specifications section contains no accessibility wording; the Cabinet Office routes it through the public sector equality duty; and the Technology Code of Practice tells suppliers to the public sector that "You must meet the WCAG 2.2 AA standard as a minimum". *(Standard (law); Standard (regulator).)*

### The distribution argument, re-based

Version 1 said a government user "cannot embed or cite a non-compliant product without inheriting the problem", and made meeting the standard "a distribution decision". The texts say something more exact. Under regulation 4(2)(e), a public body that embeds content it did not fund, develop or control is not bound by the Regulations for it, and must say so in its accessibility statement. Three real statements show the three handlings: the ONS lists a third party's pop-up polls as its own non-compliance and says of its embedded visualisations that "In all instances, the underlying data are accessible through other formats"; Manchester City Council lists a third-party census tool and says "We've provided accessible summaries of the census data"; Kent County Council says "We do not have control over and are not responsible for the accessibility of this content, but we endeavour to work with the third party to improve its accessibility." And the exemption's first word is "funded": the government's reading is that "If you have funded, developed or are in control of third party content, it is your responsibility to make sure that it is accessible." So the argument survives in two forms the texts describe and not in the one version 1 gave: as *funding* (content a public body has funded is, on the government's reading, that body's responsibility) and as *reputation* (a body that embeds a dashboard it must declare non-compliant in its statement, or disclaim, has a reason not to). What "funded" covers, and whether any of this touches the Vibe Map, is the studio's adviser's question. *(Reading of the texts; recorded, not advised.)*

## 5 · How disabled readers read data, measured

The accessible-visualisation literature is young, small in its samples, and almost entirely about blind and low-vision readers using screen readers; its own survey says so — 56 papers since 1999, "Most research in our collection did not consider various types of visual impairment", "Most studies do not go beyond basic statistical plots" (Kim et al. 2021), and the field "has not paid enough attention to the needs of people with disabilities", while for cognitive and learning disabilities its recommendations remain "largely heuristic" (Marriott et al. 2021). This section reads what it has measured, and marks the size of each result.

### The gap

Sharif and colleagues, with 36 screen-reader and 36 non-screen-reader users on bar, scatter and line charts built in three common libraries: "screen-reader users extract information 61.48% less accurately and spend 210.96% more time interacting with online data visualizations compared to non-screen-reader users" (2021). *(Evidence; online, unsupervised.)* Their interviews' plainest sentence: "Many visualizations are entirely indetectable to screen readers". Fan and colleagues audited 87 live visualisations and surveyed 127 screen-reader users: "82% (9/11) of Born Accessible visualizations were rated as very or extremely accessible, while only 14% (11/76) of Top Results visualizations achieved the same ratings"; by type, "21% (3/14) of line graphs, 18% (3/17) of bar graphs, and 7% (1/15) of maps were rated moderately to extremely accessible", and "tables (n = 13) were the only type of representation in which the majority of visualization were rated moderately to extremely accessible (54%, 7/13)"; "None of the audited maps in both groups directly conveyed complexity in the data". Asked how they prefer to access data, "the most frequently indicated method was through tactile graphics (n = 50), followed by Braille (n = 25), then audio (n = 15), screen reader (n = 7), and sighted assistance (n = 4)" (2023). *(Evidence; judgement sample; three auditors, two sighted and one blind.)* `[Rich reference]`

### What a description should hold

Lundgard and Satyanarayan built a four-level model of a chart description from 2,147 sentences — "Elemental and Encoded Properties" (chart type, encodings, axes), "Statistical Concepts and Relations" (extrema, outliers, correlations, comparisons), "Perceptual and Cognitive Phenomena" (trends, patterns, exceptions), "Contextual and Domain-Specific Insights" (meaning in the world) — and had "30 blind and 90 sighted readers" rank descriptions at each level. "While both groups generally prefer mid-level semantic content, they sharply diverge in their rankings of both the lowest and highest levels": "blind readers rank content and Levels 2 and 3 as most useful" and "semantic content at Levels 1 and 4 as least useful", while for sighted readers "Levels 3 and 4 are found to be most useful"; and "A majority of blind readers (63%, n=19) were emphatic that descriptions should not contain an author's subjective interpretations, contextual information, or editorializing about the visualized data (i.e., Level 4 content)". The paper's introduction draws the conclusion: descriptions "should aim to convey a chart's trends and statistics, rather than solely detailing its low-level design elements or high-level insights". *(Evidence; a usefulness ranking, not comprehension; curated descriptions.)* `[Rich reference]` What the finding is and is not: it is about the *description that stands in for the chart*, which blind readers want to carry what the chart carries and not the author's view of it; sighted readers, who have the chart, ranked the author's view highly. It says nothing against interpretation on the page — the authored account Chapter 11 puts beside every view, read by everyone — and the chapter places interpretation there. Jung and colleagues interviewed 22 people with visual impairments: descriptions should "reference the underlying data instead of visual elements"; readers "construct an image of visualizations in their head"; the length most wanted "ranges from 2 to 8 sentences"; "All participants favored having access to the underlying data"; use "plain language instead of visualization-specific language" (2022). *(Evidence; interviews.)*

What practice supplies is less. In 547 alt texts from research papers, "only 50% of alt texts in our sample contain information about extrema or outliers" and "only 31% contain information about major trends or comparisons" (Chintalapati, Bragg & Wang 2022); 40 per cent of 7,493 figures in three venues had any alt text at all (Jung et al.). And when twelve authors wrote alt text for slides, "authors crafted significantly lower quality alt text when starting from the automatic alt text compared to starting from a blank box" — 2.38 against 2.96 on the raters' scale (Mack et al. 2021). *(Evidence.)* The reading: a chart's description carries the chart — its type as the short part; its figures and its trend as the long part — in two to eight plain sentences, generated from the data by a template whose rule is published (Chapter 11's method; the ONS's checked local stories are the precedent) and read by a person against the data. Mack's finding is a caution about that reading step, not a bar on templates: it compared free writing with editing a machine's caption of an image, and found the editor anchored on the draft; so the person who reads a generated sentence reads it against the figures, and the templates are tested with readers, not only reviewed.

### Structure, navigation and the table

Zong and colleagues built screen-reader experiences along three dimensions — "structure, or how chart entities should be organized for a screen reader to traverse; navigation … and, description" — and had "13 blind and low vision" readers try three prototypes: a standard HTML table as the control, a faceted multi-view of bar charts, and a scatterplot with targeted navigation. Of tables, participants "overwhelmingly reported the same problems: they are ill-suited for processing large amounts of data and impose high cognitive load as users must remember previous lines". The only significant quantitative result ran the other way: "A Friedman test found a significant rating difference for the ease-of-use of the prototypes", and "multi-view was more difficult to use than table with statistical significance (p<0.01)" (2022). *(Evidence; n = 13; the cognitive-load claim qualitative.)* Version 1 wrote that hierarchical navigation "reduced cognitive load for blind and visually-impaired users versus flat tables"; the study's own numbers say the table was easier to use than the multi-view prototype, and the memory-load complaint is what participants said. The tooling exists — Olli "renders a chart as an accessible tree view following the HTML Accessible Rich Internet Applications (ARIA) standard" from Vega, Vega-Lite and Observable Plot specifications — and Chart Reader, built with ten screen-reader users over five months, adds non-speech audio and notes that "providing tabular representations is a lossy exchange, as raw data is devoid of the design decisions and communicative intent of the author" (Thompson et al. 2023). VoxLens, a plug-in giving voice query, summary and sonification, raised accuracy from 34 to 75 per cent and cut time from 84.6 to 54.1 seconds with 21 screen-reader users — against "prior work's data [69] (N=36) as a baseline", not a within-study control, and for "two-dimensional single-series data" only (Sharif et al. 2022). Siu and colleagues' audio data narratives, description interleaved with sonified segments, produced more insights than description followed by one sonification (15.4 against 12.4; 16 users; single-variable series only). *(Evidence; small n throughout.)*

The reading: a navigable structure is a route with a prototype behind it and no measured win over a table yet; a table is the type most often rated accessible in the one audit and the one official statements fall back on; and the two are not rivals — the table carries values, the structure carries shape, and the description carries the trend. Where the table fails is geography, next.

### Maps

Chapter 11 found the map the hardest first screen for sighted readers; it is harder here. In Fan's audit maps rated worst of the main types (7 per cent). The one experiment that tests the table as a map's substitute is a 2026 preprint by the developer of an interactive text map: "Twenty sighted and 20 blind and low-vision individuals (BLVIs) performed tasks designed to compare visual maps, ITMs, and tables"; blind participants showed "substantially higher accuracy when using the ITM (M = 0.44, SD = 0.50) compared to the table (M = 0.23, SD = 0.42)" on geographic questions, while "Blind participants reported lower cognitive workload when using the table (M = 27.40, SD = 8.74) compared to the ITM (M = 34.10, SD = 7.69)", numeric questions showed "less differentiation between conditions", and the authors conclude that "Tables should no longer be considered an acceptable alternative to a geographic map" — fifteen minutes' training, a two-minute timeout for blind participants only, and an older blind group (Biggs et al. 2026, preprint). An earlier evaluation of the same tool's choropleth "was evaluated by thirteen blind participants" (Biggs et al. 2022 †). The field's grand challenges paper says "Digital maps are inherently graphical and rely on gestures or mouse inputs for interactivity, which limit their use" and does not mention choropleths (Froehlich et al. 2019). *(Evidence; one preprint with a developer author; one abstract.)* The reading, stated at that size: a thematic map's table twin carries the figures and not the geography; the written sentence for "the darkest shape" (Chapter 11) carries some of the geography; a text route that answers "where" — which places border, which cluster, which is nearest — is the thing the one experiment found the table lacks, and whether to build one is a choice `[Prototype flag]`. 1.4.10 excepts a map from reflow and the Regulations except mapping services on a condition about navigational maps; neither says what a thematic map owes; the house provides the sentence, the figures and, where it builds one, the "where" route, and says in its statement which of them a view has.

### Audio

Sonification's own handbook: "a brief training period (around 20 min) can reduce performance error by 50% on a point estimation sonification task"; "research has generally found weak to non-existent correlations between musical experience and performance with auditory displays"; and "the field as a whole continues to exist in a virtual theoretical vacuum" (Walker & Nees 2011). Among 106 screen-reader users surveyed in 2025, "63% (N=67) of respondents had never encountered sonification in online data visualizations", about half found it at least somewhat beneficial, and it "has limited value in comprehending complex visualizations and exploring data granularly", maps among them (Sharif et al. 2025). In the one comparison across systems, "Ten blind participants completed data analysis tasks" with a description tree, an audio-tactile tablet and a refreshable tactile display, and "task accuracy did not differ significantly across systems, while completion time varied substantially", the tactile display fastest (Chundury et al. 2026 †). Apple's platform now builds audio graphs: "Use the audio graphs API to provide all the information that VoiceOver needs to construct an audible representation of the data in your charts and graphs"; "Typically, the audio graph represents the x-axis as time, and the y-axis as pitch." Highcharts ships a sonification module and claims, without a study, that "many blind or low vision users find sonification helpful for getting an overview of the data". *(Evidence; vendor documentation marked as such.)* Version 1's position — audio designed once as first-class display, data sonification a labelled probe — holds, with the evidence now attached: audio gives a trained listener point estimates, and by its vendors' account an overview; it is unfamiliar to most; it does not replace a table or a description; and the users who have met it found it of limited value for maps.

### Audit, populations and the page

Chartability, the audit checklist version 1 adopted, is "7 principles and 50 heuristics, 14 of which are considered critical" on its current site (the paper previewed ten); "those new to auditing may take anywhere between 2 and 8 hours to complete a full pass"; its evaluation was "19 total participants across 8 environment spaces" and measured auditors' confidence — "data practitioners with a novice level of accessibility skills were more confident and found auditing to be easier after using Chartability" — not disabled readers' outcomes; its critical items include "No table", "Reading level inappropriate" and "No title, summary, or caption". Data Navigator, its authors' toolkit for structure and input, has no user study: "a critical next step for future work is to conduct separate studies". *(Evidence; the instrument's limits stated by its authors.)*

Who reads, and with what. WebAIM's tenth screen-reader survey, 1,539 self-selected respondents in 2023–24: primary screen reader JAWS 40.5 per cent, NVDA 37.7, VoiceOver 9.7; "91.3% of respondents report using a screen reader on a mobile device"; to find information on a long page, 71.6 per cent "Navigate through the headings on the page". The WebAIM Million, February 2026: "95.9% of home pages had detected WCAG 2 failures", "an average of 56.1 errors per page", low-contrast text on 83.9 per cent, missing alt text on 53.1, and "96% of all errors detected fall into these six categories" — worse than February 2025 on failures, errors per page and contrast, better on missing alt text (55.5 to 53.1). Populations, with their sources' limits: "16.8 million (25%)" disabled people in the United Kingdom (Family Resources Survey 2023/24); "more than 2 million people are living with sight loss in the UK", "around 340,000" registered blind or partially sighted (House of Commons Library, quoting the NHS); colour vision deficiency "approximately 1 in 12 men (8%) and 1 in 200 women" (a charity, unsourced); dyslexia "Ten percent of the population are believed to be dyslexic" (a charity, "believed"); balance and dizziness problems in "About 15 percent of American adults" (United States; no UK figure found). *(Evidence; surveys and official statistics; the charity figures marked.)*

### Reading, numbers and type

The reading-age claims the trade repeats have thin footing. GOV.UK's content guidance has moved and no longer states a reading age; a county council still cites "a reading age of 9 to match GOV.UK standards"; the NHS manual says "We aim for a reading age of 9 to 11 years old" with no source on the page. What stands: "1 in 6 (18% / 6.6 million people) adults aged 16 to 65 in England have very poor literacy skills" (National Literacy Trust, from the Survey of Adult Skills 2023); the NHS's "more than 6 in 10 adults struggle with health content that includes numbers and statistics", introduced by "Research shows that" with no source named; and GOV.UK's "80% of people preferred sentences written in clear English", which traces, through a 2014 guest post, to a legal-writing survey of "376 responses" (†) — a preference, not a comprehension measure. COGA's "lower secondary education level" for a summary is the W3C's own figure. *(Evidence; Standard (design system); provenance of each claim stated.)* The house's register is cognitive access by COGA's patterns and the literacy figure, not by a reading age nobody can source.

On type, the measured results are clearer than the guidance. With 48 dyslexic readers and twelve fonts, "Sans serif, monospaced and roman font styles significantly improved the reading performance over serif, proportional and italic fonts", "Arial It. should be avoided since it significantly decreases readability", and "The fonts designed specifically for dyslexia, OpenDys and OpenDys It., did not lead to a better or worse readability" (Rello & Baeza-Yates 2013); extended to 97 readers, "Using fonts that are good for people with dyslexia improves accessibility for a large percentage of the population and are also beneficial for those without dyslexia" (2016). With 170 dyslexic children reading text, and then 102 with and 45 without dyslexia reading word lists, "Words written in Dyslexie font were not read faster or more accurately", and "the Dyslexie font neither benefits nor impedes the reading process of children with and without dyslexia" (Kuster et al. 2018 †); a single-subject design found "no improvement in reading rate or accuracy" and "None of the participants reported preferring to read material presented in that font" (Wery & Diliberto 2017 †). On size, an eye-tracking study of 104 readers recommends "at least 18-point font size for the text body of websites", replicated on 28 dyslexic readers (Rello, Pielot & Marcos 2016) — above the British Dyslexia Association's "12-14 point or equivalent (e.g. 1-1.2em / 16-19 px)". The BDA's other rules — sans serif, "1.5/150%" line spacing, "Left align text, without justification", "dark coloured text on a light (not white) background", "Avoid green and red/pink" — are unsourced on the page, and its own closing line is the convergence claim: "Adopting these principles for dyslexic readers has the advantage of making all written communication easier on the eye for everyone." *(Evidence; charity guidance marked.)* The reading for the design system: the body type and its size are an accessibility decision with measured bounds; a "dyslexia font" is not one of them.

### Convergence, where it is measured

The claim that an accessible page is a better page for everyone has two experiments behind it, both from one laboratory. "Sixty-one nondisabled participants used one of three Web sites differing in levels of accessibility (high, low, and very low)"; "A high level of Web accessibility led to better performance (i.e., task completion time and task completion rate) than low or very low accessibility", and "improved user ratings (i.e., perceived usability, aesthetics, workload, and trustworthiness)" (Schmutz, Sonderegger & Sauer 2016 †). Then "55 participants with visual impairments and 55 without impairments", randomly assigned to sites at three levels of WCAG 2.0 conformance: "Higher conformance to Web accessibility guidelines resulted in increased performance and more positive user ratings (e.g., perceived usability or aesthetics) for both user groups" (2017 †). The same group then found, for easy-to-read language on a disability-friendly site, effects on non-disabled users of "increased reading time, decreased text liking and reduced intention to revisit the web site" (2019, secondary). Captions: Ofcom's 2006 review found "about 7.5 million people had used them to watch television, of whom about 6 million did not have a hearing impairment" — "had used", from a sample of 4,365 adults; a 2021 survey found "80% of 18- to 24-year-olds 'use subtitles some or all of the time'", a tenth of them deaf or hard of hearing (Stagetext); a review claims "More than 100 empirical studies document that captioning a video improves comprehension of, attention to, and memory for the video" (Gernsbacher 2015 †). Typefaces: Rello's "also beneficial for those without dyslexia". And the public sector's own standard says it as a stance: "Inclusive, accessible services are better for everyone. For example, using simple words helps people who are in a hurry as well as people who have a learning disability" (Service Standard, point 5); "Accessible websites usually work better for everyone" (GOV.UK). *(Evidence; two experiments; reviews; a stance.)*

The reading, at its measured size: conformance to the guidelines helped non-disabled users in the two experiments that asked, both read in abstract; typefaces and captions converge; easy-to-read language had a measured cost for non-disabled readers in one study and plain English a preference among many; and nothing measures convergence for charts, dashboards or maps. "Accessible as the good version" is therefore a stance the house takes with evidence for pages and a named gap for charts, and the prototype's observed sessions are where the first measurement of that gap can be made `[Prototype flag]`.

## 6 · What official bodies do, and admit

### The two rules for a chart's description

The ONS and the Analysis Function, the United Kingdom's two sources of official guidance on statistical publishing, disagree about where a chart's description lives. The ONS: "Use chart alt text to provide a specific description of the type of chart and the main trend that it is showing"; "A chart alt text sentence should be between 100 and 200 characters and always end with a full stop"; "Use the format 'A [chart type] showing that [chart title].'"; and for tools, "For some interactive tools, it may not be possible or useful to provide a title that describes a trend". The Analysis Function: a chart may meet 1.1.1 by a table or "a text description of the message the chart is presenting", and "Which you choose should depend on what you would expect a non-disabled user to get from the chart"; "We advise that it should live in the body text rather than in the alt attribute"; "The text description should never be in both the alt attribute and the body text" "as this causes auditory clutter for screen reader users"; "It should also never be in a caption as screen readers do not always pick up on these"; "There is no specific character limit for the text description of a chart as charts can be complex"; and "It is best practice to provide the data displayed in each chart as an accessible data download." Its chart guidance: "all text related to the chart should be in the body text of the webpage unless it is essential for it to be in the image"; a text description, if used, "should live in the body of the page, directly underneath the chart", and in that case "the image of the chart should be marked as decorative and therefore hidden from screen reader software"; legends that match by colour alone fail 1.4.1; "when writing the chart's alternative text you should incorporate any messages given by annotation". Its format advice: build charts in HTML where the site can and "this is known to meet accessibility standards"; otherwise "we consider it the most accessible option for images of charts" — SVG. *(Standard (official statistics); Standard (design system).)* The two can be read together: the W3C's two-part rule is the ONS's formula as the short part (which identifies the chart and names its trend) and the Analysis Function's body text as the long part (which gives the figures and the relationships), and the Analysis Function's "never both" is about not giving the long description twice. The short part naming the trend that the long part then gives is the W3C's own pattern ("The short text alternative for the chart indicates that a description follows").

### The dashboard sentences

The Analysis Function's dashboards guidance (6 February 2026), under what it means by a dashboard: "Dashboards rarely meet full accessibility requirements." "This means you should: not publish a dashboard without the data being available in another form; consider a statistical bulletin to accompany your dashboard." Its software page: "External dashboards must meet WCAG 2.2 guidelines"; "Open-source languages (such as R, and Python) will give greater control over accessibility"; "Out-of-the-box options (such as Power BI) come with some accessibility features built in, but they can be difficult to customise"; "accessibility features are available but are not always used by analysts in a way that meets the WCAG 2.2 requirements"; "users who are reliant on keyboards or assistive software can find it difficult to interact with these dashboards"; Power BI "has a limited Accessibility Checker which does not cover all possible issues". An answer in its 2022 Q&A: "It is hard to make dynamic charts and dashboards fully accessible." "I have been looking into it but I am yet to find a fully accessible dashboard." The ONS's own page: dashboards "are difficult to make accessible and responsive for different devices"; "always make sure any dashboard design meets accessibility standards". *(Standard (official statistics); the orchestrator re-fetched the "rarely" sentence.)* Chapter 11 attributed the "rarely" sentence to the ONS; it is the Analysis Function's, and the correction is logged `[Counterbalance → Ch 11]`.

### What the statements declare

Each accessibility statement of an official dashboard that could be read and states a status says "partially compliant", and where a fallback is named it is the data in another form. The UKHSA data dashboard: "partially compliant with the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines version 2.2 AA standard"; "some charts present on the dashboards use colour alone to differentiate between lines on the chart … This fails WCAG 1.4.1"; "we have made no claims of disproportionate burden"; prepared and last reviewed 19 December 2023. The ONS: "partially compliant" with 2.2 AA; "Some of our data visualisations may not load or function correctly for users who have JavaScript disabled", "particularly the case for our more advanced interactive tools such as Explore local statistics, Build a custom area profile and Census maps"; "In all instances, the underlying data are accessible through other formats, such as the accompanying dataset files and the surrounding commentary on the HTML web page"; a disproportionate-burden claim for the 2018–2020 back catalogue of visualisations; a new platform "in 2026". Fingertips: "partially compliant" with 2.1; "Interactive elements within data tables are not keyboard operable"; "Relationship between information in data table cells is not always clear to those using a screen reader"; "Colour is the only method used for conveying information within some tables". Public Health Scotland, prepared 18 May 2026: "The R dashboards currently published on this website have a range of accessibility issues and fail the following WCAG 2.2 success criteria" — "Title of the graph is not communicated to screen reader users as it is an image of text."; "The graph is not announced by screen reader."; "Slider widget is inoperable with screen reader activated."; "Dynamic content is not announced."; "Content overlapping at 400% browser zoom." — some twenty items for its R dashboards and fifteen for its Tableau ones, by the tool's count; and of the Tableau dashboards, "Screen reader users do not have access to tooltip information." Two councils' Power BI — Scottish Borders: "Visitors using screen reader software or a keyboard will not be able to interact with them in the same way"; mapping and zoom "do not have an equivalent for keyboard only or screen reader users"; "Some graphs and charts within PowerBI dashboards rely solely on colour"; and Kingston, "partially compliant": "longer strings of alternative text may be cut off when using a screen reader"; "The frame does not reflow when zoomed in at high levels." *(Practice; official statements read for what they declare.)* `[Rich reference]` The reading: the public record of what official dashboards cannot do is a list of the criteria this book's accessibility rules rest on — 1.4.1, 1.4.5, 1.1.1, 2.1.1, 4.1.3, 1.4.10, 1.4.12 — and the list is the house's test plan.

### Design systems, platforms and the two bad answers

The GOV.UK Design System declares itself "fully compliant" with 2.2 AA and says in its strategy that "Using the GOV.UK Design System in a service does not immediately make that service accessible" and "WCAG does not cover all aspects of accessibility"; "To ensure services are accessible, user research must include disabled people"; its own statement says a visitor can "listen to most of the website using a screen reader (including the most recent versions of JAWS, NVDA, TalkBack and VoiceOver)" and that "The details component does not work well with Dragon and older versions of VoiceOver". The NHS manual: "Do not include text in graphical (raster) formats like PNG, JPEG or GIF"; alt text "ideally under 125 characters", with the caveat that for "a complex chart" the information may go in the alt text or a link. The BBC's infographics guide: alt text "Describes what is in the infographic; a literal interpretation of its form"; the caption "Explains what the infographic says; an editorial summary of its salient points"; "Multi-dimensional data that can be represented as a chart or graph should also be provided as a data table"; "Design it monochrome to begin with, and only add colour where desirable"; and "In most cases, you should avoid creating interactive visualisations". Platforms: Datawrapper, "The elements of all your Datawrapper charts, maps, and tables can be navigated using only the keyboard", an alt-text field and a "Get the data" link; Flourish, "instead of a blanket WCAG AA certification, we focus on enabling and guiding users"; Highcharts, keyboard navigation, "Screen reader users can explore the chart and its data point by point", and a sonification module with Georgia Tech's Sonification Lab; Vega-Lite, an aria flag and "A text description of the mark item for ARIA accessibility (SVG output only)"; Tableau, "You can use Tableau to create dashboards that conform to the Web Content Accessibility guidelines (WCAG 2.2 AA)" — what an author can build; Power BI, "All Power BI visuals support keyboard navigation", "The Alt Text textbox has a limit of 250 characters", "The accessibility features have a few known problems and limitations." Eurostat: "The interactive tools and visualisations on this site do not always have text alternatives"; "Interactive maps are not always accessible to blind users and users who rely on keyboard navigation". *(Standard (design system); Practice; vendor documentation.)*

The two bad answers have a record. Overlays: "full compliance cannot be achieved with an overlay"; "We will always advocate for the remediation of accessibility issues at the source of the original error" (Overlay Fact Sheet, about a thousand signatories); among 758 practitioners, "A strong majority (67%) of respondents rate these tools as not at all or not very effective. Respondents with disabilities were even less favorable with 72% rating them not at all or not very effective, and only 2.4% rating them as very effective" (WebAIM 2021); and the United States Federal Trade Commission's order of 2025 requiring one vendor "to pay the FTC $1 million" over claims that its widget made sites compliant — a consent order, not a finding; no UK regulator has spoken. Text-only versions: "Text-only versions do not accomplish the goal of accessibility"; "They accommodate users with some disabilities but neglect other types of disabilities"; "Updates may not be reflected on the text-only version, and the information can quickly become outdated and inaccurate" (WebAIM); the staleness claim is practitioner consensus with no audit behind it; a GDS staff comment of 2013, "I'd rather we didn't build a separate 'accessible' site". *(Practice; Evidence (survey); the regulator's order reported as fact.)*

## 7 · Chapter 12's departure, tested

Chapter 12 ruled that every chart carries, inside its frame, its title, unit, source, base, address and snapshot date, and for a comparison its gate and its wording — eight items — so that a screenshot says what it is — against the Analysis Function's rule that "titles should be in the body of the page or document, as opposed to within an image", given for accessibility, with the cost admitted: "This does mean an image of a chart can be taken out of context if it is copied and pasted elsewhere." This chapter owes it the test against the criteria.

The criterion is 1.4.5 Images of Text (AA): "If the technologies being used can achieve the visual presentation, text is used to convey information rather than images of text except for the following: Customizable: The image of text can be visually customized to the user's requirements; Essential: A particular presentation of text is essential to the information being conveyed." Its definition of an image of text excludes "text that is part of a picture that contains significant other visual content", and its Understanding page names the case: "Examples of such pictures include graphs, screenshots, and diagrams which visually convey important information through more than just text." Then the sentence that decides it: "Where images of text are used in addition to text to convey the same information, and where both are presented to the user, this success criterion is met." The page's example is a poster whose text is also given beside it. 1.4.11 adds that "Text within a graphic must meet 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum)", and the Analysis Function itself says "Chart labels and annotations can be considered essential images of text". 1.4.12 treats canvas text as images of text and does not mention SVG; the Analysis Function considers SVG "the most accessible option for images of charts". The one official failure on record is Public Health Scotland's: "Title of the graph is not communicated to screen reader users as it is an image of text" — a title that existed *only* in the image. *(Standard (W3C); Standard (official statistics); Practice.)*

The reading, and it is a reading: on the live page, the chart is SVG or HTML with its title, unit, source and date as real text in the page, which is what the Analysis Function asks and what 1.4.4, 1.4.5 and 1.4.12 are for; the frame that carries them *as part of the graphic* is text inside an SVG, which is text, or — in the exported image — text inside a picture "that contains significant other visual content", given "in addition to text", with "both … presented to the user". Chapter 12's rule, built that way, meets 1.4.5 by its own Understanding page. What it must not become is Public Health Scotland's failure: a title that lives in the picture and nowhere else. So the rule is restated with its condition: the frame's text — the identifying items, not the long description — is drawn as text in SVG on the page, repeated as HTML text beside the chart (the heading, the source line), and baked into the downloaded image; never the image alone. That repetition is of the title and source, which the Analysis Function itself places in the body; it is not the "never both" case, which concerns the long description. The remaining cost is 1.4.4's — text in a raster export "does not scale as well" — and that is the export's cost, not the page's; the design system's own preference for text outside images stands for the page and is kept. Whether eight items fit a phone-sized frame legibly remains the open question Chapter 12 left `[Prototype flag]`.

## 8 · The routes, and what each carries

Pulling sections 3 and 5 together: a dashboard view reaches a reader who cannot see it, or cannot see it well, or cannot read numbers easily, or cannot use a mouse, by several routes, and the evidence says what each carries and what it drops.

*The written account.* Chapter 11's sentence on every view and its generated account per place are, in this chapter's terms, the long description the W3C asks for, at the levels blind readers rank highest — the statistics and the trend — in the plain words COGA asks for. It is the one route every assistive technology handles, and it is the route the ONS, the Analysis Function and every official statement fall back on ("the surrounding commentary on the HTML web page"). It carries the meaning, for every reader, which is where interpretation lives; it does not carry the data.

*The table twin.* It carries the data, is the route screen-reader auditors rated best, is the route the Regulations' map exemption and the Analysis Function's "data being available in another form" point at, and is a native HTML table, which the Authoring Practices Guide prefers to any ARIA grid. It does not carry shape, and for a map it carries the figures and not the geography (0.23 against 0.44). It is paginated and sortable by keyboard; it has real header cells (1.3.1).

*The navigable structure.* A chart exposed as a tree — overview, groupings, marks — with keyboard movement and the focused node's name and value spoken at each step, built from the chart's specification by tooling that exists. It carries shape and lets a reader explore; the one study found the plain table easier to use than its richest prototype; it is the route to build second and test first. The tree is built on graphics-document and native roles, never on ARIA invented for the purpose: "No ARIA is better than Bad ARIA".

*Audio.* Overview and trend for a trained listener; unfamiliar to most; not maps; the platform's audio graph where the platform offers one. A labelled extra beside the routes above.

*Tactile.* The route the most surveyed users named as preferred (50 of 127), and the one a web page cannot give except as a file to emboss. Out of scope for the display; in scope for the data download, which is the file.

*The eye, helped.* Zoom to 400 per cent without two-dimensional scrolling (1.4.10), with the map and the data table excepted and the furniture around them not; text that reflows, resizes and respaces (1.4.4, 1.4.12), which rules out canvas text; colour never alone and 3:1 on each line and slice that carries meaning unless labels do (1.4.1, 1.4.11); the reduced-motion preference honoured and auto-updates pausable (2.2.2, 2.3.3); focus visible and never under the filter bar (2.4.7, 2.4.11); type at a size the eye-tracking evidence supports, in a face the dyslexia evidence supports, which is an ordinary sans serif and not a special one.

*The hand.* Everything by keyboard with no trap (2.1.1, 2.1.2); every control by single pointer without a drag (2.5.1, 2.5.7); targets at 24 pixels or the table twin (2.5.8); nothing fires on mouse-down (2.5.2).

*The mind.* One brief status message per change, never the view read aloud (4.1.3); interpretation in the authored account, on the page for everyone, and not in the chart's description (section 5); a filter that changes content and not context (3.2.2); numbers reinforced with words, each step carrying what the last one knew, uncommon terms explained within a click, a summary at lower-secondary level (COGA).

What no route carries alone is the whole, and that is the point of section 3's first finding: the routes sit on one page, as supplementary content, and the page conforms or it does not.

## 9 · The failures, read for the disabled reader

*People do not use it.* Because they cannot: 61.48 per cent less accurate, 210.96 per cent longer; "Many visualizations are entirely indetectable to screen readers"; 73 per cent of screen-reader users did not agree that the data they met was typically accessible. Test: a screen-reader user, using the written route and the table together, says the view's sentence, the figure it rests on and a next step (Chapter 11's three); the pass is all three; the time is recorded against a sighted reader's, and the ratio is the measure the prototype tracks, because the literature's ratio is three to one.

*People do not trust it.* Because it says nothing about itself: 94 per cent of screen-reader users had concerns about getting accurate pandemic information in time; the government found "many accessibility statements are now out-of-date". Test: the house's statement is dated within the last twelve months, lists each view's routes and failures, and names what it was tested with and by whom; a statement is a necessary condition for trust and not evidence of it, and the chapter claims no more.

*It breaks.* Because the keyboard route was never walked: focus visibility and keyboard the first and third commonest public failures, found by hand and missed by scanners. Test: the mouse unplugged, every view reached and left.

*It looks the same every month.* Because the accessible route is a frozen alt text on a changing view. Test: the description says the current figure, and a person has read it against the data since the last change.

*The parallel version.* Because a second build is a second thing to maintain, which the standard calls a fallback and practice says goes stale. Test: there is one page per layout, and the accessible routes are on it.

## 10 · The symmetry test

*The floor inherited whole.* Version 1: Fixed, from the Decision Book's Chapter 15 and the Style Guide's §13. **Holds**, and is now mapped to the criteria that each item meets, with 2.2.2 (auto-update) and 4.1.3 (status messages) added, which the floor did not name.

*Tooling chosen against the floor in week one.* **Holds, strengthened**: canvas text is images of text under 1.4.12; official statements name the tools whose dashboards fail; the Analysis Function says open languages "give greater control".

*Charts as navigable structure, not alt-sentence pictures.* **Holds as a route, re-weighted**: the one study's significant result favoured the table on ease; the structure is built second and tested; the description and the table come first.

*The accessible tree as a build step.* **Holds**; the tooling exists for Vega-Lite and Observable Plot specifications.

*State announcements reuse version 1's plain wording for data states.* **Holds, sharpened**: one brief status message per change, polite, never the view or the sentence read unasked; "chatty" is the standard's own warning.

*Audio designed once, serving access and product.* **Holds as a stance** with the evidence attached: overview and trend with training; half find it somewhat beneficial; not maps. The claim that the sound of a place serves a blind reader and a curious reader identically is unmeasured and stays a design intention `[Validate with users]`.

*Data sonification a labelled probe.* **Holds.**

*The generated written story as the guaranteed non-visual route.* **Holds, strengthened**: it is the W3C's long description at Lundgard's levels 2 and 3; it is official practice's fallback.

*The community's phone-read test as the reading-level standard.* **Holds as a house standard**, with the observation that no official reading-age figure has a source and COGA's "lower secondary" is the W3C's; the house's test is a procedure with people, which is better than a number.

*Chartability per release.* **Holds as a procedure**, priced: 50 heuristics, 14 critical, two to eight hours for a novice; evaluated on auditors' confidence, not readers' outcomes; the keyboard walk and the screen-reader read are done as well, not instead.

*Observed testing with disabled users of the real audience.* **Holds**; the Design System's "user research must include disabled people"; the standard's "User testing should be carried out".

*The distribution argument recorded once.* **Re-based**: the Regulations exempt unfunded, uncontrolled third-party content; the argument stands as funding and reputation, not inheritance; recorded once, in the new form.

*Accessibility outranks brand in the hierarchy.* **Holds**; the W3C's "essential" exception is narrow and the house claims it for nothing but the map's pins.

*No parallel accessible version.* **Holds as the house's rule, on the standard's stated preference**: "fallback option"; "preferred method of conformance is to make all content directly accessible"; the standard permits what the house forbids itself.

*The blind-fan test.* **Holds, re-written by the evidence**: the question is not what the chart looks like but what the trend and the figures are, and for a map where — because that is what blind readers rank most useful in a description and what a chart carries for a sighted reader; the answers are compared on those things, and the two groups' different ranking of interpretation is the reason the account, not the description, carries it.

*"The good version and the accessible version keep turning out to be the same build."* **Half-confirmed**: measured for whole pages in two experiments, for typefaces, for captions; counter-measured for easy-to-read language; unmeasured for charts. Kept as a stance with its gap named.

*The house's.* The Style Guide's §13 floor is the criteria list with the standard's words beside each; §02's hierarchy resolves the brand-against-access cases as before; §04's register meets COGA's clear-words pattern and gains the 1,500-word check; the Decision Book's Chapter 15 rules ("nothing load-bearing in a tooltip"; the non-interactive route) are 1.4.13 and the written account. The Vibe Setting Framework's phone-read test is cited as the community's standard and not reproduced.

*The studio's own statement.* The chapter makes publishing an accessibility statement a house procedure, in the public sector's form, as the record of this chapter. **Fixed as a procedure**; whether the Regulations' own statement duty reaches the studio is not the chapter's to say.

## 11 · What holds true regardless

Seven truths, each with its grounds, then the record. Stated for any public dashboard; the nightlife case in section 13 is one application.

**1 · The accessible version is the version: a page conforms whole or not at all, the standard names a separate accessible version a fallback and prefers content "directly accessible", and the written account, the table and the structure are supplementary content on the one page — so the house, going one step past the standard's preference, builds one page per layout and no parallel one.** "cannot be achieved if part of a web page is excluded"; "a fallback option for conformance to WCAG"; "the preferred method of conformance is to make all content directly accessible"; "should be deprecated"; "Text-only versions do not accomplish the goal of accessibility". *(Section 3; W3C Understanding Conformance; WebAIM; US Department of Justice.)*

**2 · No rule names a chart, so a chart meets the standard by readings the W3C has written down: a short description that identifies it and a long description that gives its values, relationships and trends with the data in a table where practical; contrast on each line and slice that carries meaning unless labels do; one brief status message when a filter changes a view and never the view itself; auto-updating that can be paused; motion that can be disabled; text that is text — and ARIA offers graphics-document and its generic roles and no chart role, so none is invented.** "scales, values, relationships and trends"; "Where possible and practical, the actual data is provided in a table"; "each line in a graph"; "'18 results returned'"; "too 'chatty'"; "a mechanism for the user to pause, stop, or hide it"; "The impact of animation on people with vestibular disorders can be quite severe"; "canvas implementations of text are considered to be images of text"; "Future work will expand on this framework"; "No ARIA is better than Bad ARIA". *(Section 3; WCAG 2.2 Understanding pages; Graphics Module; APG.)*

**3 · The description that stands in for a chart carries the chart — its type, its figures and its trend — in plain words, two to eight sentences, generated from the data by a published rule and read by a person against the figures; interpretation lives in the authored account on the page, for every reader — because blind readers rank the statistics and the trend most useful and were emphatic against an author's interpretation in the description, sighted readers with the chart rank interpretation highly, half of alt texts in the one measured sample omit the extrema and seven in ten the trend, and a reviewer anchored on a machine's draft reviews less.** "blind readers rank content and Levels 2 and 3 as most useful"; "emphatic that descriptions should not contain an author's subjective interpretations"; "reference the underlying data instead of visual elements"; "only 50% … extrema or outliers"; "only 31% … major trends or comparisons"; "significantly lower quality alt text when starting from the automatic alt text". *(Section 5; Lundgard & Satyanarayan; Jung et al.; Chintalapati, Bragg & Wang; Mack et al.)*

**4 · A table carries the data and is the type most often rated accessible in the one audit; it does not carry shape or geography, so a map's table twin is joined by a sentence for the shape and, where the house builds one, a text route that answers "where"; the navigable structure carries shape and is built second and tested; audio carries point estimates to a trained listener and an overview by its vendors' account, and is a labelled extra.** "tables … the only type … (54%, 7/13)"; "7% (1/15) of maps"; "multi-view was more difficult to use than table"; 0.44 against 0.23 on geographic questions, with lower workload on the table; "reduce performance error by 50%" after twenty minutes; "63% … had never encountered sonification"; "task accuracy did not differ significantly across systems". *(Section 5; Fan et al.; Zong et al.; Biggs et al. preprint; Walker & Nees; Sharif et al. 2025; Chundury et al. †.)*

**5 · The texts that bear on a private provider are the Equality Act's: a duty owed by a service "to the public or a section of the public (for payment or not)" to "disabled persons generally", which the explanatory notes call anticipatory, whose reasonable steps include "the information is provided in an accessible format"; the Regulations bind public bodies, presume conformity at WCAG Level A and AA "as amended from time to time" (WCAG 2.2 by the government's statement, monitored since October 2024), and do not apply to "third-party content that is neither funded nor developed by, nor under the control of, the public sector body" — content a body did not fund it must declare exempt in its statement, and content it funded is, on the government's reading, its responsibility; no regulator's text read names WCAG and no reported website judgment was found. Reported, not advised.** *(Section 4; S.I. 2018/952 regs 3, 4, 6–9, 12; Equality Act ss. 6, 20, 21, 29, Sch 2; the 2026 Code; GOV.UK guidance.)*

**6 · Official bodies admit that dashboards "rarely meet full accessibility requirements", each official dashboard statement read is "partially compliant" with the data in another form as its fallback, and the failures they declare are the criteria above — so the house publishes an accessibility statement in the public sector's model form, as its own procedure, declaring per view what each route carries and what fails, dated, with the technology it was tested with.** "I am yet to find a fully accessible dashboard"; "In all instances, the underlying data are accessible through other formats"; "Title of the graph is not communicated to screen reader users as it is an image of text"; "Screen reader users do not have access to tooltip information"; "an explanation of those parts of the content that are not accessible and the reasons why". *(Sections 4, 6; Analysis Function; ONS; PHS; Fingertips; UKHSA; reg 8.)*

**7 · Convergence is measured narrowly and holds where measured — conformance helped non-disabled users in the two experiments that asked (both read in abstract), readable typefaces for dyslexia read better for everyone, most subtitle users hear — and is unmeasured for charts, so "accessible as the good version" is a stance with evidence for pages and a named gap, which observed testing with disabled readers of the real audience is the way to begin measuring.** "better performance … than low or very low accessibility" (†); "for both user groups" (†); "also beneficial for those without dyslexia"; "about 6 million did not have a hearing impairment"; "increased reading time, decreased text liking"; "user research must include disabled people". *(Section 5; Schmutz, Sonderegger & Sauer †; Rello & Baeza-Yates; Ofcom; GOV.UK Design System.)*

## 13 · A nightlife illustration — method only

*Illustrative; fixes nothing about the design; invents no findings.*

A place-page from Chapter 12, read four ways.

**By keyboard.** Tab lands on the page's sentence, then the two columns as landmarks; the status region that will speak when a filter changes is never in the tab order. Into the missed column's profile as a tree: the chart's name, "four groupings", the first grouping, its marks by arrow key, each mark's label and value spoken, and a way out by Tab. The filter bar is below the focused mark, never over it. The map is a landmark too; where its pins must be small the table twin is the next landmark.

**By screen reader, without entering a chart.** The sentence; the generated account at levels 2 and 3 — the figures and the trend, no interpretation; the table for each view, headers announced; for the map, the sentence for the darkest shape and a short list of which areas sit beside which. When the reader changes the district, one polite message of the kind "District changed; 3 of 20 shown", and the reader goes to the sentence.

**By ear, if the house builds it.** An audio graph of the time view, labelled as an extra, after the description and the table; the sound of the place, if that is ever on the page, as content and not as the route to a figure.

**At 400 per cent on a phone, with motion reduced.** One column at a time, poster above door; the table paginates; the map keeps its two dimensions and its heading does not; nothing slides; the live panel says when it will next refresh and can be stopped.

**The statement.** Linked from the footer; twelve lines per the record; the non-compliances in the public sector's form; the test date and the screen readers named; the errors channel.

**The tests.** A scanner first, for what it catches. The mouse unplugged, every view reached and left. The phone layout at 400 per cent. A screen-reader user from the user groups completes the three — sentence, figure, next step — by the written route and the table together, and the time is recorded against a sighted reader's. A blind reader is asked what the district's trend is, what the figures are and, on the map, which places sit beside it, and a sighted reader is asked the same from the chart; the answers are compared on those things. Chartability's critical fourteen are walked before release. And the statement is read back against the page by someone who did not write it.

## What proves a break works

A scan, three walks and a question; each has a pass. The *scan*: an automated checker on every layout, for contrast and the rest of what it catches; a pass is zero detected failures. The *keyboard walk*: mouse unplugged, every view reached, every control used, every view left, with focus visible throughout, on the desktop layout and on the phone layout with an external keyboard; a pass is no failure. The *zoom walk*: the phone layout at 400 per cent, which is the 320-pixel width 1.4.10 names, every view read, no two-dimensional scrolling except inside the map and the table and never for their headings or controls; text respaced to 1.4.12's values with nothing lost; a pass is no failure. The *screen-reader read*: a screen-reader user who has not seen the page, with one of the two commonest desktop readers or VoiceOver on a phone, says the view's sentence, the figure it rests on and a next step, using the written route and the table together; a pass is all three; the time against a sighted reader's is recorded. The *question*: a blind reader is asked what the trend is, what the figures are and, on a map, which places sit beside the one named; a sighted reader is asked the same from the chart; a pass is agreement on the trend, the figures and the neighbours — the chapter's evidence says the groups differ on interpretation, which is why interpretation is not asked. The structure, where built, is tested by the screen-reader read with the table covered. A departure from any Fixed rule above is logged with the five results attached, and the statement's line 9 carries it until it is fixed.
