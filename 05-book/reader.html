01The default view

THE LANDING VIEW IS THE CLAIMSteered · revised on Carly's commentsPart 0 · The glance

Source file: 04-chapters/ch01-the-default-view.md

The decision

What does the instrument say before anyone touches it — and to whom?

The 60-second version

The default view is not a starting point for interaction. For most visitors it is the entire product: the New York Times' own analytics found only 10–15% of readers touch the interaction layer at all. So the first screen is not where the instrument begins — it is where, for five in six people, it ends.

Three measured facts govern it. Judgment precedes reading: visual appeal stabilises within 50 milliseconds, and it rewards low complexity and looking like what the thing claims to be. Text carries the message: eye-tracking and recall studies show titles and supporting words — not the graphics — dominate what people take away. And defaults are read as advice: across 58 datasets and 73,675 participants, pre-selected options shift choices by d = 0.68, partly because people treat the default as the designer's recommendation.

Meanwhile the visualisation field's own rule — "overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand" — turns out to be doctrine, not evidence. Its author called it a reminder he kept rediscovering; a systematic review found nobody had ever validated it; the best available evidence says overviews reliably please and unreliably help.

So the decision is editorial, and it cannot be delegated to convention: the default view is a sentence you are endorsing, addressed to a named person. Write the sentence first.

How the decision is made

Step one — accept that the default is the product

Gregor Aisch, then of the New York Times graphics desk, published the desk's own finding: readers interact with roughly 10–15% of graphics, and his correction of the lore matters as much as the number — "the 85% of readers didn't ignore the graphic, they just didn't click the button." His operative rule follows directly: "if some information is crucial, don't make the user click or hover to see it." [Measured — internal analytics, reported by its own staff, not peer-reviewed.]

Cho and colleagues then showed the default does more than reach the most people — it steers the few who go further. In a controlled study (81 participants), the initially shown view of an interactive tool significantly shaped which views users spent time in afterwards, and anchors in the first view biased final estimates despite twenty-five minutes of free exploration. [Measured.] The default is not neutral ground the user departs from. It is the frame they carry with them.

Step two — write the first sentence before designing the first screen

Borkin and colleagues' eye-tracking and recall work across 393 visualisations is unambiguous: "titles and supporting text should convey the message of a visualization" — text dominates both where people look and what they can later report. [Measured.] The Office for National Statistics rebuilt its house style on the same finding, requiring chart titles to "sum up the main message shown in the chart in 10 words or less", on the strength of internal telemetry showing its bulletins take 9.5 times longer to read than anyone actually spends on the page. [Practice guidance backed by unpublished telemetry.] The house already runs tighter: Carly's club-poster mantra is five words or less, and a poster is the harder test — read at walking pace, at night, in competition. Where the two rules disagree, the poster wins.

The audit of live instruments sharpened this into a split worth stating as a rule. The strongest first screens put a question in the visitor's voice at the top: the BBC's "are hospital waiting times improving near you?", the Thriving Places Index's "Does your place support people and planet to thrive?" The weakest lead with the institution describing itself: "London's first Cultural Infrastructure Map brings together new research…", "The Creative Footprint is a non-profit initiative that measures and indexes live music space." The visitor's question and the institution's self-description compete for the same line, and only one of them is about the reader.

One cross-cutting observation from the audit: none of the ten instruments pushes configuration at arrival. The slider-first era — the early OECD flower asking you to weight eleven dimensions before showing you anything — appears to be over, and the OECD's own current landing page has retreated all the way to prose and PDF downloads. The failure at both extremes is the same failure: a first screen that asks for work before giving meaning.

Step four — treat the default as an endorsement, because the reader does

The behavioural evidence is the strongest in this chapter. Jachimowicz and colleagues' meta-analysis — 58 datasets, 73,675 participants — puts the default effect at d = 0.68 (95% confidence interval 0.53–0.83), a 27.24 percentage-point average shift toward the pre-selected option in binary choices. And the mechanism matters more than the size: "individuals commonly perceive defaults as conveying an endorsement by the choice architect." [Measured, verified at source.]

Hullman and Diakopoulos named the same thing from the design side a decade earlier: "default views provide an initial point of interpretation anchored to the default visual configuration" — their example being the New York Times opening a national map "zoomed-out… of all of New York City (the largest US city and presumed home of the default New York Times user)." [Evaluated — a named concept with worked examples, no measurement.]

Read together: whatever place, measure, comparison and time period the instrument shows first will be received as what its maker thinks matters. There is no configuration of the first screen that avoids this. The only choice is whether the endorsement is deliberate.

And the absence is a finding. No study appears to exist measuring whether end users of an interactive visualisation ever change its defaults — default filters, default extents, default sorts — at what rate, or with what consequence. Behavioural economics has a precise effect size for defaults in decisions; visualisation has one anchoring experiment and a newsroom's internal telemetry. Nobody has run the defaults meta-analysis for dashboards. Logged below.

Step five — design for the glance you will be judged in

Visual appeal stabilises within 50 milliseconds of exposure (Lindgaard and colleagues), and Tuch and colleagues isolated what drives it: low visual complexity and high prototypicality — the degree to which the thing looks like what it claims to be — with both effects detectable at 17 milliseconds. [Measured. Scope caveat: what stabilises is appeal, not comprehension — the popular "users judge your site in 50ms" is an extrapolation.]

Glance cost also varies by chart form by roughly an order of magnitude: in Blascheck and colleagues' study, a simple comparison took ~200–285 milliseconds from a bar or donut chart and ~1,500–1,800 from a radial bar. [Measured, at smartwatch scale.] A default view built from slow-glance forms is spending its 50 milliseconds losing.

For this instrument the prototypicality finding cuts interestingly: the brief demands it not look like "a government boring thing" — but the measured aesthetic reward is for looking like what you claim to be. The resolution is that the claim is nightlife's, so the prototype to match is the culture's own visual language, not the dashboard genre's. That is Chapter 12's territory; the rule this chapter contributes is only that the register must be legible in the first glance, because there is no second one.

Step six — hold "overview first" as a preference, not a law

Shneiderman's mantra — "Overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand" — is the most cited rule in the field, and its evidential status is now clear. Shneiderman himself: "Each line represents one project in which I found myself rediscovering this principle and therefore wrote it down as a reminder." Craft and Cairns audited the citing literature and found, verbatim, "although numerous authors cite the Mantra, there are no reasonably obvious studies that have validated Shneiderman's recommendations" — 52 citations, zero validations — concluding it "appears to act as an inspiration and guideline for practitioners rather than a scientifically studied methodological approach." [Evaluated, verified at source.]

Hornbæk and Hertzum's review of 23 empirical comparisons found the only near-unanimous benefit of overviews is that people like them; task time improved in about half the comparisons, and on errors only 2 of 17 favoured the overview. [Measured — counts across studies.] And van Ham and Perer's counter-mantra — "search, show context, expand on demand" — argues a large class of users "are not interested in global patterns… they are trying to learn something more about a particular datapoint." [Evaluated — case study only, by its authors' own admission.]

So neither overview-first nor detail-first is settled. What the evidence does support is narrower and more useful: satisfaction favours an overview; the task decides the rest. A visitor who arrives with a place in their pocket is van Ham and Perer's user, not Shneiderman's.

Worked example

Illustrative. This applies the chapter to the vibe-map brief to show the decision being made; it fixes nothing about the eventual design.

The brief. "Measure your area at a click." The deck's promise: what systems count and what culture holds, on one screen, for someone who currently waits months for a team to assemble it.

Step one — the sentence. Before any layout: what does the default view say, and to whom? The brief's own phrase contains the answer — your area — which means the honest default sentence is something like: "Here is what [place]'s nightlife culture holds — and here is how little of it the official picture counts." That sentence cannot be rendered until the instrument knows the place.

Step two — so the pattern question becomes real. Pure input-first (the ONS pattern) shows a search box and proves nothing — the empty state asks for work before giving meaning. Pure exemplar-first (the Pudding pattern) opens on one place with full authorial voice — but the anchoring evidence says the exemplar place will frame every place viewed after it, and a Bristol-first default quietly tells every other city this is Bristol's instrument. Map-first says "this culture exists, everywhere" — the abundance message, which is genuinely the advocacy point — but the audit's finding stands: an all-pins map answers "how much, where" and never "so what," and staleness is invisible on a pin.

Step three — the decision this chapter's evidence supports. A message-then-input opening (the BBC's hybrid), where the message is carried by an exemplar rendered honestly: one screen that shows a real place's two columns — counted and missed — as the proof of what typing your own postcode will get you, followed immediately by the input. The exemplar place should rotate or be chosen deliberately as an endorsement, because it will be read as one either way. Freshness sits on the first screen, per rule — this is also the anti-observatory move: the instrument's first screen shows the age of its own data, so the container can never look more alive than its contents.

What was rejected, and why it's recorded. Select-first was rejected on the audit's evidence — a dropdown-first Power BI panel serves the professional who already knows their authority and loses everyone else, and this instrument's brief names advocates and the scene, not only officers. The rejection is logged so it does not get re-argued at 11pm.

Pressure points

Where there is real latitude.

The exemplar's voice. Exemplar-first is the most authored pattern and the most alive — "we'll call him Alex" is the strongest opening in the audit. A vibe-map equivalent ("It's 11pm on Stokes Croft…") is available, powerful, and entirely a craft decision. The constraint is only that the anchor is chosen knowingly.

The question-headline. "What does your city sound like?" is already the project's user-facing question, in the visitor's voice, and it is better than anything in the audit. The evidence says put it first.

Everything decorative. Register, imagery, motion at the threshold of the first glance — free, within the style guide, and this is precisely where "make it look like art" lives without touching a Fixed rule.

Where it looks sophisticated and fails.

The instrument panel. Controls before meaning reads as rigour and is the select-first failure: the score exists only after labour, and the citizen leaves before performing any.

The links-hub. A taxonomy of elsewhere. The audit's example communicates nothing about the night itself on arrival — no number, no picture, a promissory note for a product.

The brochure. Message-first without an exemplar decays into the institution describing itself above a download button. The audit caught a famous instrument mid-decay.

The all-pins map with no sentence. Abundance without argument. Every pin equal means no pin argued for.

What proves a break works. Show the default view, untouched, to someone in the intended audience for five seconds, then take it away and ask what it said and who it was for. If they answer with the sentence you wrote in step two, the default is working. If they answer with a description of the interface, it is not. Then check the other 85%: is everything crucial visible without a single click?

The decision

How do measured-harm data and cultural-value data share a display without the harm frame winning?

The 60-second version

The deck's slides 7 and 8 are a display problem nobody has solved on screen. One column is what systems count: risk, incidents, licensing, nuisance, footfall, spend. The other is what culture holds: belonging, trust, scene-building, creative risk, care, talent incubation, collective joy. The first column arrives with years of administrative data behind it. The second is the reason the instrument exists.

The framing evidence says the obvious layout is the wrong one. Audiences are "invulnerable to facts that do not fit within a narrative that they can understand and embrace", and repeating the opposing frame — even to refute it — strengthens it. A screen that opens on the harm column and then answers it has already conceded the frame. Lead with the value column, which is also the house's own operating principle: lead with abundance.

The harder problem is evidential asymmetry. The harm data will look more real, because robustness reads as truth. The resolution is not to dress the value column up — "strong design must never make weak evidence look stronger than it is" — nor to visually diminish it. It is equal visual dignity with honest provenance: both columns rendered with the same craft, each carrying its evidence tier in text, and every gap in the value column rendered as a visible gap. Because in this instrument, the gaps are the argument.

How the decision is made

Step one — decide which column speaks first, and know what the order asserts

The Decision Book's Chapter 16 carries the framing evidence, from the Othering & Belonging Institute: many audiences are "invulnerable to facts that do not fit within a narrative that they can understand and embrace", the same fact resolves differently depending on which story is active, and — the operative rule — never repeat the opposing frame in order to deny it, because repetition strengthens it. [Evaluated — framing research synthesis, read directly.]

Translated to a screen: a display that opens on incidents-licensing-nuisance and then presents cultural value as the rebuttal has structured itself as a reply. The reader's active story remains "the night is a risk to be managed," and the value column becomes special pleading against it.

The house already has the answer as an operating principle — lead with abundance, begin with the value already being created — and Chapter 1's rules give it teeth: the default view's sentence is written first, and here that sentence belongs to the value column. The harm column is context the reader already holds; the deck says so — it is what systems already count. Nobody arrives at this instrument ignorant of the harm frame. They arrive knowing nothing else.

This does not mean hiding the harm column. Omitting it reads as advocacy and fails the hostile-reader test in one glance. It means the value column owns the headline and the harm column is present, honest and second — the shared ground, not the story.

Step two — give unequal evidence equal dignity

The two columns are not evidential equals and will not be for years. The counted column rests on administrative series; the held column rests on emerging measures, qualitative work and, in places, nothing yet. Two symmetric traps follow.

The first is inflating the value column — the trap the style guide already bars: "Strong design must never make weak evidence look stronger than it is." [Axiom.]

The second is subtler and undocumented in the guide: robustness reads as reality. A column of crisp administrative numbers next to a column of ranges, estimates and gaps will be read as the real one, whatever the layout intends — not because anyone decided it, but because precision is received as a property of the thing rather than of the measurement. The Decision Book's Chapter 6 names the mechanism from the other side: precision styling on imprecise data "is a claim the data cannot support" — and its converse operates here: honest imprecision on the value column, next to false-precision neighbours, punishes honesty.

The resolution is a two-part rule. Equal visual dignity: both columns are set with the same craft, the same type scale, the same chart quality — the value column is never the sketchy one. Honest provenance, carried in text: every figure in both columns wears its provenance chip — the style guide's existing furniture, tier stated as a text code, colour never the sole carrier — so the evidential difference is stated rather than performed. The difference between the columns becomes something the instrument says, not something it looks like.

And the harm column's own weaknesses are stated with the same candour. Administrative robustness is partly costume — a point the instrument can make only if its own standard is visibly even-handed, which is the entire credibility architecture of this project.

Step three — check the direction of every encoding

The Urban Institute's Do No Harm guidance — practitioner consensus, derived from interviews rather than experiment [evaluated] — supplies the checks that bite here: which group or frame is treated as the reference category; how things are ordered (deliberately, not by inherited default); and whether a deficit framing has been designed into the encoding itself.

The Decision Book's Chapter 7 worked example shows the move in practice: an outcome colour ramp running "neutral-to-positive rather than negative-to-positive, because a deficit framing would encode 'these young people were problems that were fixed.'" [Read directly.]

For the two columns, the checks become specific:

  • The harm column is not the reference category. The value column is not displayed as deviation from a norm of risk.
  • No red-to-green moral gradient across the columns. The style guide already encodes ordinal strength as filled steps in ink precisely to avoid a judgement ramp; the same discipline holds here. The harm column is not "the red side."
  • Order within each column is chosen — by magnitude, by evidential strength, by the argument — and stated, never alphabetical by accident.

Step four — render the missed as measured absence, not blank space

The value column will have holes for years. How those holes are displayed is the chapter's most consequential craft decision, because the empirical evidence says readers do not notice missing data on their own: in the CALVI study of misleading-chart literacy, missing data and missing normalisation were the hardest defects for readers to detect — baseline correctness 0.39 on trick items. [Measured.] A silent gap in the value column will simply read as less value.

The Decision Book's rule is the foundation — a gap in the data is a finding, shown as hatched fill or a visible break, never silently omitted [axiom, carried into Style Guide §12] — and its Chapter 7 supplies the escalation this instrument needs: deliberate incompleteness as a named creative technique — "showing the gaps, the unmeasured, the refused" — which "requires confidence from the commissioner."

Here the commissioner is the instrument's own thesis. Culturally powerful but institutionally under-evidenced is the claim; the value column's rendered gaps are that claim made visible.

And the value column's contents are not a designer's invention — the scene has already named them. The Seeing the System workshop (Vibe Setting Framework, unpublished — confidential) was explicit that a useful map "cannot only show buildings, venues, jobs and economic outputs" and must make the case for what "usually goes unnoticed, undocumented or undervalued: the 'aunty networks', the chats, the favours, the care, the genre knowledge, the joy, the wellbeing, the feeling of being in a music space." That list is the value column, authored by the people it describes — which means its rendered gaps are not a display convention. They are a commitment the community has already asked for. A row reading belonging — not yet measured anywhere is not a weakness of the display. On this screen, it is the finding. The design rule: every gap in the value column is rendered at full size, labelled with why it is empty (never measured · suppressed · not yet collected), so the column's shape shows both what culture holds and how little of it anyone has counted.

Step five — never let the columns share a scale

The two columns count different things in different units — incidents per year against, say, a belonging measure that does not yet exist. Any shared axis, common index or single blended score on screen asserts a commensurability nobody has established, and quietly re-runs the composite- index problem at the level of pixels: merging the columns into one number is aggregation, with every exchange-rate commitment that implies, made silently by a chart.

So: juxtaposition, never superimposition. The columns sit side by side, each on its own scale, each internally honest, with the relationship between them carried where the evidence says meaning is actually carried — in the words. Titles and text dominate what readers take away [measured — Borkin et al., Chapter 1], so the bridging sentence does the work the shared axis must not: "[Place] recorded n incidents last year. It also holds m venues, k crews, and a scene nobody has counted." Ten words or less if it can; five if the poster rule can be met.

The decision

What kind of map is this — and where geography is the projection, what does it do well, what does it do badly, and when is it the wrong surface?

The 60-second version

The instrument's working name is the vibe map, so this chapter earns its place early: the map is the most seductive surface in place data and the least examined. The reflex is named exactly in the practice literature — "the impulse is since the data CAN be mapped, the best way to present the data MUST be a map." It usually isn't.

The measured misreadings are severe. Small areas with extreme values are missed by 30–40% of readers while large areas are detected over 90% of the time — the map weights attention by acreage, not importance. Around 90% of readers assume darker means more, with or without a legend. And an unnormalised map of anything human is a population map wearing a costume.

The audit of live music-venue maps found one pattern everywhere: the directory pin map — meaning carried by pin density alone, no time, no freshness, asserting what one auditor called "a permanent daytime-neutral landscape of venues." For an instrument whose subject runs 6pm to 6am, a surface with no time axis is not a neutral simplification. It deletes the subject.

And the house critique is already an axiom: the Liverpool map was "created by academics for local authorities… which is why they've got buses on it." Buildings and infrastructure, when the scene is people. The chapter's job is to turn that critique into display rules.

How the decision is made

Step zero — a map is not necessarily geography

The word "map" belongs to design thinking before it belongs to cartography, and the house uses it that way [axiom, stated on steer]: stakeholder maps, problem-space maps, systems maps, the Doughnut. The Decision Book's Chapter 10 already holds the vocabulary — six jobs a diagram can do (bound, relate, decompose, sequence, diverge, converge), with the Systems Map defined as nested containment, not flow, and the ecosystem framing is itself one: tallest trees, grassroots, mycelium — a vertical map of a living system with no geography in it. Doughnut Economics is the proof that a non-geographic map can carry an entire economic argument in one figure: a normative boundary drawn as a shape, readable in a glance, non-compensatory by construction — nothing on one side of the ring buys back an overshoot on the other.

And the live precedent is the house's own. In the Seeing the System workshop (A Ticket to the Future, Vibe Setting Framework — unpublished, confidential until release), participants built a co-created map of Bristol's music ecosystem and placed themselves on it — the insert-yourself-into-the-map move, already prototyped on paper, in a room. (Chapter 4 takes this further: the placed self is a collection instrument, not only a mark.) What the mapping process showed, in the framework's own words: "Bristol cannot be mapped only through buildings, venues or organisational structures" and "the feelings, relationships and people who make the scene matter also need to be seen."

So the step-zero question is: which kind of map is this view, and what is its job? Geographic projection is one answer among several, correct only when where is the finding. A scene's influence structure wants a systems map. Value flows want a flow map. The ecosystem's health against its boundaries may want a doughnut. The rest of this chapter governs the geographic case — but choosing geography is now a decision with named alternatives, not a reflex triggered by the word "map" in the project's name.

Step one — where geography is chosen, establish that it earns its place

Matthew Ericson, then deputy graphics director of the New York Times, wrote the canonical warning: campaigns target swing states "not because of their geographic location, which is what a map is most effective at showing, but rather because of their voter demographics" — and a map of a non-geographic argument forces readers "to try and visually sum up all the colors in their head." [Practice-lore, from newsroom leadership; no controlled study exists — see gaps.] Datawrapper's version: to show numeric differences between regions, "consider another chart type, a table or text instead." [Practice-lore.]

The test transfers directly: a map is the right surface when location itself is the finding — where things are, how they cluster, what is near what. When the finding is how much, how good, or compared to what, the map is the wrong surface wearing the right subject. "What does Bristol sound like?" is not intrinsically a map question; parts of its answer are.

Step two — match the form to the data, and venues are points

The cartographic rule is stated flatly by Axis Maps: "never use choropleth with raw data/counts" — choropleth requires rates or ratios — and, the sentence that matters most here: "The less the thing you are mapping is tied to enumeration units, the less sense a choropleth map makes." [Practice-lore, but canonical across the field.]

Venues, events, crews and scenes are not properties of wards. They are points, and point phenomena painted onto administrative areas inherit the modifiable areal unit problem: the patterns you see change — and can reverse — depending on where the arbitrary boundaries fall and at what scale (Openshaw 1984). [Evaluated, foundational.] A ward-choropleth of "cultural vibrancy" is therefore twice wrong before it renders: counts in a choropleth, and a point phenomenon forced into boxes someone else drew for a different purpose. This lands on material already in hand — the comparative methodology's boundary-vintage discipline exists precisely because areas are unstable containers.

Step three — design against the three measured misreadings

Schiewe's review of empirical choropleth perception supplies numbers [measured]:

  • Acreage is attention. Small regions with extreme values were missed by 30–40% of readers; large regions detected at over 90%. A city-centre ward holding half the night economy is, on a map, a speck beside suburbs holding none of it.
  • Dark is more. ~90% of readers assume darker means more, legend or no legend. For a night-time instrument the trap is doubled: dark basemaps are the genre's aesthetic, and on them, unlit reads as empty. No empirical study exists on empty map areas being read as "nothing there" rather than "no data" — logged as a gap — but the dark-is-more bias points one way, and for this subject unlit is not dead; it may be exactly where the unlicensed, informal and DIY scene lives, or where data was never collected. The two-columns rule arrives here: absence on the map must be legible as unmeasured, not as nothing.
  • The legend is ignored. Over 90% read colour lightness alone and skip the classification. Whatever the bins say, the lightness ramp is the message.

And the oldest trap of all, best stated by xkcd 1138: three near-identical heatmaps of "our site's users," a magazine's subscribers and something else entirely — all just population maps. An unnormalised map of nightlife activity is a map of where people live and go out, which is to say: a population map with a lens flare. Normalisation — per resident, per venue, per licensed premises, per something — is the fix, and Chapter 1's evidence already established that missing normalisation is the defect readers are least able to catch on their own.

The house holds the decisive worked case. A Ticket to the Future's research found that Bristol's venues under 1,000 capacity put on 78% of the city's paid events but take only 32% of ticketing revenue [ATTTF; the finding is in the published foreword and was given on steer]. In every aggregate economic view — revenue, GVA, jobs — the arenas are the biggest player and swamp the story, exactly as the biggest ward swamps the choropleth: acreage bias has an economic twin, and it is aggregate bias. Normalise — per event, per venue, per pound of revenue retained locally — and the story inverts: the small rooms are doing most of the work, programming the local artists, generating the originality and the community wealth, and struggling hardest with the economics. The display rule this yields is general: whichever unit the aggregate flatters, offer the normalised view that lets the rest of the ecosystem be seen — because the biggest player will otherwise swamp every surface it appears on, map or not.

Step four — a pin cloud is a directory, not an argument

The audit found the same pattern on both live music-venue maps examined: the directory pin map. Liverpool's (audited second-hand — the app itself is robots-blocked) shows "precise locations, capacity, and the primary functions" of venues, framed explicitly as serving an economic study; Toronto's mixes venues, studios and stores across one city surface. In both, meaning is carried almost entirely by pin presence and density; at a glance, "music" equals wherever pins are densest, which is downtown, which is the population map again.

Chapter 1 classified map-first landing states and found their failure: abundance without argument — "this exists, everywhere," but never so what; every pin equal means no pin argued for. Clustering, the standard fix for the pin cloud [vendor practice-lore], makes it worse in one respect: a cluster count hides category mix and reads as pure quantity, so the map's only remaining message is more.

The display rule that follows: a map earns its place in this instrument only when its pins carry an argument — differentiated by something that matters (a value dimension, a trend, a gap, a tier), not merely present. A directory has real uses; Chapter 1 filed it as a pattern with an assumed visitor. But the deck's promise is measure, and a pin cloud measures nothing.

Step five — a night map without time deletes its subject

Neither audited map surfaces time in any form: no hours, no night-versus-day, no closures, no data age. The audit's summary line deserves quoting because it is the finding: both maps assert "a permanent daytime-neutral landscape of venues."

The house definition makes night time operational — 6pm to 6am [axiom] — which means the instrument's subject is a time-slice of place, and a static map is a display that has silently integrated over the one dimension that defines the subject. The same venue is a café at 4pm and a scene at 1am; a map that cannot tell the difference is not simplifying the night, it is removing it. Time is therefore a first-class display dimension for any map in this instrument — which specific form that takes (a time scrubber, small multiples by hour, a night-only default) is a craft decision downstream; the Fixed rule is only that the map states when it is true, exactly as Chapter 1 requires it to state how fresh it is.

Step six — decide what a pin is, because the Liverpool critique is a display rule

The house critique — "less about buildings and infrastructure and more about the people who make the vibe happen" [axiom] — is usually read as a data question. It is also a display question: what does a mark on this map represent? A building outlasts the scenes inside it; a scene can move buildings and remain itself. A venue-pin map is a map of containers. The audit shows both incumbents chose containers, because containers have addresses and self-registration forms.

This chapter cannot resolve the unit — that is entangled with questions the research reaches later — but it can set the display rule: the map's marks must represent the thing the instrument values, and where that thing is not addressable (a crew, a scene, a recurring night that moves), the map either finds a form for it or hands that content to a different surface. The one thing the map may not do is quietly substitute buildings for culture because buildings have coordinates.

Worked example

Illustrative — the vibe map's map decision, not its design.

The question the surface must answer: "what does this city sound like, at night, now?"

Step one says: parts of that are map questions — where scenes cluster, what is near what, where the gaps are — and parts are not: how much, how strong, compared to last year belong to other surfaces. So the map is a view within the instrument, not the instrument.

Steps two and three say: no ward-choropleth of "vibrancy" — points, symbols or a normalised surface, with the denominator visible; city-centre wards protected from acreage bias by labels and callouts; the lightness ramp designed as the message because it will be read as one.

Step five says: the default map is a night map — its stated slice is, say, 6pm–6am this quarter — and freshness sits on the surface. The anti-observatory move again: this map's own age is part of its display.

Step six says: if the pin set is venues-only, the map must say so — "showing licensed and listed spaces; recurring nights, crews and scenes not yet mappable" — rendering its own missing layer per Chapter 2, rather than letting containers stand in for culture.

The five-second test: shown cold, a reader should say "the night is alive here, here and here — and the dark bits mean uncounted, not empty." If they say "there are lots of venues downtown," the surface has produced the population map with a lens flare.

Pressure points

Where there is real latitude.

The night register. Dark basemaps, neon accents, the whole aesthetic of the genre — free within contrast rules, and this is the surface where "looks like nightlife's" is most available. The constraint it inherits is only the unmeasured-versus-empty rule.

Non-standard geography. Cartograms, gridmaps, distorted or schematic geographies that trade shape fidelity for value fidelity — legitimate, arguable, and well-suited to killing acreage bias. Adaptable craft.

Refusing the map for a view. Some of the instrument's best answers may be lists, profiles or small multiples. Declining the map where location is not the finding is a decision this chapter explicitly licenses.

Where it looks sophisticated and fails.

The vibrancy choropleth. Wards coloured by a blended score. Three failures at once: counts in a choropleth, a point phenomenon in boxes, and Chapter 2's forbidden blended score — now with acreage bias on top.

The beautiful pin cloud. A thousand glowing dots on a dark basemap. Gorgeous, and it says "population density" while reading as "vibrancy."

The timeless night map. Any map in this instrument with no stated time slice.

Heat-mapping the informal. Interpolated "vibe heat" smeared from sparse points manufactures data where there is none — the exact opposite of rendering the gap.

What proves a break works. State what the map's marks represent and what its denominator is, out loud. Show it for five seconds; collect the sentence. Then show it to someone who knows one small, intense scene the map covers: if their scene is invisible and they cannot tell whether that means uncounted or absent, the map fails the dancefloor test regardless of its beauty.

04The felt layer

YOU CAN'T SCRAPE A VIBESteered · revised on Carly's commentsPart 1 · The instrument

Source file: 04-chapters/ch04-the-felt-layer.md

The decision

How is felt experience displayed as evidence rather than decoration?

The 60-second version

The felt layer — vibe, belonging, care, collective joy — is the instrument's reason to exist and its most dangerous surface, because the two ways of failing sit either side of a narrow path: flatten it into a wellbeing score and it stops being true; render it as mood imagery and quotes-as-wallpaper and it stops being evidence.

The path exists because vibe is three things, not one, and each displays differently. As produced condition it is fully researchable — atmospheres are made, by skilled labour, and that labour can be shown. As perceived quality it is measurable to an international standard: the International Organization for Standardization's (ISO's) soundscape circumplex includes vibrant as a standardised perceptual attribute of a place, rated 1–5. As singular excess — the thing that "emanates from but exceeds the assembling of bodies" — it is representable but not measurable, and must be displayed as what it is.

And the house holds the rule the literature lacks. "You can't consume a vibe. You can only respond, reflect, absorb, feel, sense. You are active. It needs you there, to be part of it, for it to exist at all." Vibe is co-produced — which means vibe data is co-produced, and a display that shows the crowd as a subject rather than an author has reproduced the arena on a screen: "you are chattel." The scene said the same thing in the workshops, as extraction. The felt layer displays with its authorship and its return visible, or it is the thing it set out to replace.

How the decision is made

Step one — split the vibe, because each part displays differently

The research behind this book's raw notes established a three-way split, and it holds up against the house's own material.

Vibe as produced condition. Böhme's founding move: atmospheres are made — by design, sound, light, staging, welcome — and that making is observable, skilled work. Michels turned it into an empirical programme (trace the material modulation; explore how bodies resonate; account for stability). [Evaluated.] The display consequence is the one the house has been circling for years: this layer is where the resident DJ, the lighting tech, the door person and the promoter become visible as skilled workers producing a measurable condition — the "lifetime value of a resident DJ" question, given a surface. The essay's line is the caption: "Creating vibes is a science."

Vibe as perceived quality. ISO 12913-1 defines soundscape as the acoustic environment "as perceived or experienced and/or understood by people, in context" — perception constitutive, not incidental — and ISO/TS 12913-3 supplies an eight-attribute circumplex — pleasant, vibrant, eventful, chaotic, annoying, monotonous, uneventful, calm — rated on 1–5 scales, with Pleasantness and Eventfulness computed from them, reported as medians and interquartile ranges. [Standard; measured within its domain.] This is the committee-room anchor: "vibrant" is an internationally standardised perceptual attribute of a place. One honest caveat travels with it: the standard's home is urban soundscape assessment; applying it inside venues and to night-time scenes is an extension, and the chapter marks it as one.

Vibe as singular excess. Anderson's affective atmospheres — "singular affective qualities that emanate from but exceed the assembling of bodies" — with three deliberate ambiguities and no method offered. Garcia-Mispireta's dancefloor ethnography shows what a careful account looks like and warns that the felt sense of togetherness is "vague, fluid, and may hide exclusions and injustices." [Evaluated.] This part is displayed as testimony, narrative and expressive encoding — never as a number — and it carries the Decision Book's Chapter 7 licence: the expressive version is permitted because the precise version ships alongside.

The Fixed rule: the three parts are displayed as three registers and never collapsed. A "vibe score" that blends a labour observation, a circumplex median and a feeling is a category error wearing a KPI's clothes — the flattening your own metric guardrail already names: culture reduced to a delivery mechanism.

Step two — use the displays the scene has already invented

The Vibe Setting Framework's workshops (unpublished — confidential until release) were not just consultation. They were display prototypes, and better ones than most of the literature.

Participants stood on a line across a room — agree, undecided, disagree — against three statements ("I feel welcome and able to be myself in Bristol music spaces" among them). That is a human dot plot: a distribution displayed in bodies, readable at a glance, self-placed. It translates directly to screen: the felt layer's natural chart form is the distribution of placed selves — marks people put down themselves — not a bar of averages. Self-placement on the co-created map is the same form spatially. One hundred and twenty-five post-its distilled to ten principles is qualitative aggregation with its working shown.

Two display rules fall straight out:

  • Show the distribution, never just the centre. The workshop finding was "largely towards agree, with consensus that more work is needed" — a shape, not a score. Belonging data displayed as a mean deletes the people at the disagree end, who are the finding.
  • A self-placed mark is a different object from an assigned one, and the display says which it is. This is the verified-versus-inferred discipline from the grants work, applied to feelings.

And the form scales up: the digital human dot plot is a collection instrument, not just a chart. "Insert yourself into the map" becomes measurable the moment the display invites the mark — "what does your city sound like?" asked as a playable survey, each answer a placed self that feeds the city's felt distribution in return. [House steer, 16 August 2026.] Three things make this more than a feature idea:

  • It is the studio model in miniature. A city-based study plus audience participation means the fans themselves build the evidence of how nightlife culture feels in their place — advocacy hand in hand with the community and the people who make things happen, and repeatable per place. The policy channel already exists: the Commons culture committee's Fan-Led Review of Live and Electronic Music (April 2026) gave fan voice its own chapter and named Bristol Nights in it — fan-contributed evidence is now a recognised input, not an anecdote. [Published.]
  • Belonging becomes a definitive metric — and it reorders the hierarchy. Self-placed marks measure attachment, not volume, so they do not scale with population. A town with a strong scene and no "music city" reputation — Hull is the house's example — can genuinely outscore London on belonging, because the metric is intensity per participant, not activity per square mile. That is Chapter 3's normalisation rule arriving at city scale: the aggregate flatters the biggest player; the placed-self distribution lets the strongest scene be seen.
  • Two honesty conditions travel with it, or it breaks. First, the yield is a distribution in register two — perceived quality, self-selected, provenance stated on the face — and any headline drawn from it says so; it never blends with the other registers into a vibe-o-meter. Second, a participatory surface invites manipulation: Queering the Map was brigaded offline before it had moderation. Moderation and consent are designed in before launch, not after the first incident.

Step three — display belonging as cues, because that is where it lives

The workshops located belonging precisely: "more than presence… feeling recognised, safe enough, useful, welcome and able to contribute without having to become someone else" — and, critically, in small cues: "who is visible, who is promoted, who is expected to know the language, who can access the space, whose genre is taken seriously, whose behaviour is judged, and whose contribution is quietly missed."

The essay holds the same truth at street level: the bar staff remembering an order, "let me charge that for you", "you ok to get home?" — "I came alone, I left with friends."

The display consequence: felt evidence is concrete or it is nothing. A belonging indicator displays as the cue itself — named, counted where countable, quoted where not — never as an abstraction ("community cohesion: 7.2"). The cue list above is, in effect, a community-authored indicator set; the instrument's job is to give each cue a surface, not to launder them into a composite. This is also what makes the felt layer auditable: a hostile reader can argue with "whose genre is taken seriously" as a claim about programming data; they can only dismiss a cohesion score.

Step four — the co-production rule, which is the chapter's spine

The essay's theory of vibe is participatory all the way down: "Music is born from place because it's a conversation. It needs response, feedback." The MC redirects the set because of one dancer. The crowd is not the audience of the vibe; it is the medium. "It needs you there, to be part of it, for it to exist at all." [House axiom.]

Now the display consequence, and it is the rule this chapter exists to state. A felt layer displayed about people converts participants into product — the arena on a screen, the Costa-Coffee-ification of the very thing being defended. And the scene named the institutional version in the workshops, unprompted: people described "relationships with institutions as feeling extractive: where knowledge, culture, credibility or community insight is taken from the scene, but the value does not always flow back to the people and places who created it."

So the felt layer carries three visible properties or it does not publish:

  1. Authorship — who said it, who placed it, in their register (design principle 9: "speak the language of the scene" — testimony is not translated into policy voice).
  2. Consent and control — the CARE-shaped question the workshop asked itself: "what consent processes are needed, especially for informal or underground networks that may not want visibility?" Some of the felt layer is community-held, not published — and per Chapter 2, that non-publication is rendered as a labelled gap, not silence.
  3. Return — design principle 3, visible, evidenced listening: "People need to see that their voice changed something… what was heard, what changed, what could not change, and why." The felt layer's provenance chip has a fourth field the quantitative layers don't need: what this changed. That is how a display proves it is a conversation and not a harvest — "the CBS needs to show its workings", as a design requirement.

Step five — when the felt and the counted disagree, that is a finding

The mixed-methods literature names three outcomes of integrating strands: confirmation, expansion, and discordance — and treats discordance as a result to report, not a failure to resolve. [Evaluated — Fetters, Curry & Creswell.] Pre-commit to it.

For this instrument the discordant case is the important one, and the 78/32 finding shows its shape: the room that is economically marginal and culturally load-bearing. A small venue failing on every counted metric while the felt layer shows it holding a scene together is not a data quality problem. It is the instrument's central story, and the display must let the two layers disagree on the same screen — Chapter 2's juxtaposition rule, now with the felt layer as one of the columns.

05Comparison without a league table

IDENTITY IS NEVER RANKEDSteered · revised on Carly's commentsPart 1 · The instrument

Source file: 04-chapters/ch05-comparison-without-a-league-table.md

The decision

How are places and scenes compared so that every place gains leverage — and none gains a ranking to lose?

The 60-second version

Comparison is the instrument's political payload. The purpose, in the house's words: a transferable metric by which any place — Margate, Stroud, Swansea, Croydon — can "pressure local policymakers into better supporting their area's unique sonic personality, through universally agreed data." That is comparison as leverage for everyone. A league table is the opposite: leverage for the top ten and a demotion notice for the rest.

The evidence on rankings is unusually one-sided. Ranked fields change to fit the rank: the mechanisms are documented — resources follow position, unmeasured qualities become invisible, institutions drift toward what scores. And ranks are largely noise at small differences: the standard audit method puts confidence intervals on rank, and in one flagship index a fifth of the ranked places' positions were artefacts of the modelling choices. The World Bank's Doing Business died of these diseases, and its post-mortem recommendation is this chapter's foundation: keep the data, drop the ranking.

Two more things make the non-ranking comparison not just safer but truer. Scenes are defined by differentiation — what makes Croydon sound like Croydon — so half of any honest comparison is identity, which can be different but never better. And the felt layer's intensity metrics don't scale with population, so the hyperlocal view genuinely reorders things: a borough with a scene beats a borough with a superclub. The display's job is to make that reordering legible as the finding, not mistakable for an error.

How the decision is made

Step one — know what a ranking does to the ranked

Espeland and Sauder's study of law-school rankings is the standard account, and its mechanisms transfer directly. [Evaluated — documented field study; agent-fetched, superseded-phase sweep.] Rankings are reactive: resources and applicants follow position; a one-tier drop was described inside a school as "devastating" though nothing real had changed; schools reverse- engineered the formula and drifted toward what scored — mission drift, where the unmeasured parts of the mission quietly stop being done. Campbell's law is the compressed version: the more a quantitative indicator is used for decision-making, the more it distorts the process it monitors.

Translate to this domain and the stakes are explicit in the house's own documents: the ambition is "not about smoothing out the scene, homogenising music experiences" [axiom — Vibe Setting Framework]. A national nightlife league table would be a homogenisation engine: every place optimising toward whatever the index counts, which is precisely the monoculture the instrument exists to resist. This is not a hypothetical — it is what rankings have done to every field that adopted them.

And the terminal case is recent. Doing Business was discontinued in 2021; among its structural defects, the CEPR post-mortem notes that under relative ranking a country that improves can still fall — genuine improvement registering as failure — and recommends keeping the underlying data while abandoning the rank. [Evaluated; agent-fetched.] That recommendation is available to this instrument at design time rather than after the scandal.

Step two — treat ranks as noise until proven otherwise

Where the field audits composite indices, it puts confidence intervals on rank: the Joint Research Centre's audit protocol runs thousands of Monte Carlo perturbations and treats a rank interval narrower than ten positions as reliable — and in its audit of a flagship global index, dozens of economies carried intervals twenty to thirty positions wide. The EU's own cultural cities monitor concedes that for roughly one city in five, the rank reflects modelling choices rather than data. [Measured; agent-fetched.]

The display rule follows immediately: a rank is never shown without its uncertainty, and at neighbourhood scale it is usually not shown at all — because at hyperlocal n, with emerging data, the intervals will overlap almost everywhere, which means the honest display of rank is no display of rank. What survives is position within a distribution: "where your place sits in the spread" is defensible when "you are 47th of 331" is fiction.

Step three — set the comparison unit where the scene lives

The house steer states the finding this chapter must make displayable: thinking of UK music, the mind goes to Manchester, Bristol, Liverpool, Leeds — but even within London's 33 boroughs, vibe measured hyperlocally "picks up Croydon, Hackney, or Romford, over Farringdon (where Fabric is) or Enfield (where Drumsheds is)." [House steer, 17 August 2026.]

Unpack that and it contains three of this book's rules at once. A venue is not a scene (Chapter 3): Fabric is world infrastructure hosted in Farringdon, not evidence of a Farringdon scene — big infrastructure inflates its host geography exactly as the arena inflates the aggregate. The intensity metric reorders (Chapter 4): belonging and participation per head don't scale with population or with venue capacity, so the hyperlocal cut is where small, strong scenes become visible. And the unit is chosen, not inherited (Chapter 3's boundary rule): borough, ward, neighbourhood or scene-defined area are different instruments — the comparison states its unit and what that unit hides.

The general rule: comparison happens at the scale where the scene lives, and the display says which scale that is. City-level comparison flatters infrastructure; hyperlocal comparison finds culture. Both are legitimate; silently conflating them is how Enfield "beats" Romford.

Step four — split the comparison: identity is never ranked, conditions always gated

Scenes are defined by differentiation — what a place sounds like that nowhere else does. That means an honest comparison has two different objects in it, and the display must never let them merge:

Identity axes — different, never better. Genre mix, sonic personality, the shape of a scene's week, who its audiences are. These are nominal: Croydon is not more or less than Hackney on them, it is else. Their display forms are profiles, fingerprints, small multiples — side-by-side portraits inviting recognition, not ordering. This is where "what does your city sound like?" lives, and ranking it would be a category error the instrument's whole philosophy forbids: all vibes are valid [axiom].

Condition axes — better or worse, and policy-actionable. Space, support, funding, licensing friction, affordability, security of tenure. These are ordinal and comparing them is the point, because they are what policymakers control. Here the style guide's rule is already absolute and this chapter inherits it as the gate: "Every chart comparing two or more places must state whether the indicator comparability gate has been cleared… A comparison without this note is not publishable." [Axiom — §12, backed by Comparative Methodology v1.0's eight-criterion gate.]

The advocacy sentence is the two halves joined. "Croydon's scene runs at this intensity with this support; scenes of similar intensity elsewhere get more." Identity establishes that there is something real; conditions establish that it is under-supported; the comparison delivers the ask. That sentence — not a rank — is what pressures a policymaker, because it cannot be answered with "so climb the table."

Step five — default to the place-centred view

Chapter 1's rule applied to comparison: the default view is the product, and the default comparison is one place versus itself over time and versus peers it chose — never the all-places table.

The precedents are already in the audit and the evidence. The Office for National Statistics' Explore Local Statistics service compares an authority to a cluster of statistically similar authorities and shows values within the national spread — comparison as context, not as position. The Better Life Index, published by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (the OECD — the Paris-based club of 38 mostly-rich countries whose statistics underpin much international comparison), lets the reader set the weights, and the finding from a decade of it is that the reweighting became a collection channel — tens of thousands of shared weightings telling the organisation what people value. [Both agent-fetched.] For this instrument, peer choice does the same double duty: a place choosing its comparators (similar size, similar scene-stage, aspirational twin) makes the comparison fair and reveals who each place believes its peers are — which is itself scene knowledge nobody currently collects.

Partial-order thinking supplies the honest structure underneath: with multiple dimensions and no imposed weights, some places simply are not comparable — A is ahead on some conditions, behind on others, and the truthful display says so rather than forcing a tiebreak. [Evaluated — the poset literature; agent-fetched.] "Genuinely incomparable" rendered honestly is not a weakness of the instrument; it is the instrument refusing to invent a tiebreak the data cannot support.

Step six — design against the screenshot

A display that can be cropped into a league table will be — by a journalist, a press office, or a hostile council. The Decision Book's breakout-statistic rule (the most-screenshotted element travels furthest, so everything it needs must be attached to it) applies here with a comparison-specific edge: no view of this instrument should survive cropping as a ranking. If a top-to-bottom ordered list of places exists on any screen, the crop exists; therefore ordered all-places lists do not exist on any screen. Distributions with places marked, peer-groups, profiles and dominance statements resist the crop structurally — there is no table to screenshot.

The decision

When a measure reaches the screen, what does its form commit you to — and what does the screen do back to the measure?

The 60-second version

Whether the Nightlife Cultural Value Index should be an index at all is a decision the house has deliberately kept open: "the result may be an index, a diagnostic, a framework, a value ledger, a modular evidence toolkit or a hybrid — or a decision that a scored index is not useful." This chapter does not close that question. It establishes what each form does on a screen, because the screen is not a neutral carrier: a composite displayed becomes a rank; a dial implies a target; a colour scale implies a judgement; a ledger implies two sides. The form decision should be made knowing what each display will do with it.

The evidence is blunt about composites. Every aggregation rule embeds exchange rates — under simple addition, a profile of (21,1,1,1) equals (6,6,6,6) — and published weights are routinely cosmetic: in one famous ranking, an indicator nominally worth 20% drove 21% of the variance while another worth 40% drove 81%. The incumbent instrument in this sector publishes three part-scores and a headline that is not the mean of its own parts, with the weights undisclosed. So the one Fixed rule that holds whatever form is chosen: the recipe on the face. A measure whose construction cannot be shown simply enough to survive the phone test is not simplified by hiding the recipe — it is laundered.

And two house steers become measure-design questions here: the attribution flip ("x% of overnight stays began with an event we put on") and the dynamic census (economic impact as a living series, not a clipboard night). Both are displays of a claim as much as of a number, and both are survivable by a hostile reader only if the claim's boundary is drawn on screen.

How the decision is made

Step one — know what each form commits you to on screen

What the research does license: one canonical data layer can drive several expressions. Identity wants a profile, conditions want a diagnostic, value flows want a ledger. The screen does not force a premature marriage; it forces each expression to obey its own commitments.

Step two — the recipe on the face

The aggregation literature's findings, graduated from the raw research and load-bearing here:

  • Every aggregation rule embeds exchange rates. Under linear aggregation a deficit in one dimension is offset by a surplus in another at a constant rate — the worked illustration is that (21,1,1,1) and (6,6,6,6) are identical. Geometric aggregation punishes imbalance; non-compensatory methods refuse offsetting entirely. [Evaluated — the standard handbook literature; agent-fetched.] Translated: under addition, a corporate late-night drinking strip with a collapsed grassroots scene can equal a modest broad ecology. Choosing the rule is the claim that those are substitutable.
  • Published weights are routinely not the real weights. Sensitivity analysis on flagship indices found nominal weights "little more than cosmetic": one ranking's indicator at 20% nominal weight explained 21% of variance while a 40% indicator explained 81%. The real weights come from correlation structure nobody negotiated. [Measured; agent-fetched.]
  • The incumbent fails exactly here. Creative Footprint's Rotterdam report publishes part scores of 8.58, 5.90 and 5.41 and a headline of 6.10 — when the mean of its own parts is 6.63 — with no disclosed weighting anywhere in its methodology. [Agent-fetched; the arithmetic is checkable from its own published numbers.] A published composite that cannot be reproduced from its published components is the display failure this chapter exists to prevent.

So the Fixed rule, whatever form wins: the recipe on the face. If the measure is composed, the composition is displayed — what went in, at what weight, with the implicit exchange rate stated in real units. The refusal test from the raw research is the right one and survives verbatim: how many new venues does the measure say compensate for the loss of one forty-year-old institution? If the display cannot answer that question, the measure is not ready for a screen. And the house guardrail sits underneath as the boundary condition: "Where would scoring create false precision or harmful incentives?" [Axiom.]

Step three — pay for "scene intensity"

Chapter 5 used the term as a placeholder; here is what the display requires of it, leaving its indicator construction to the methodology work, which governs.

Scene intensity is an intensity, not a volume: participation, belonging and activity per head or per participant, built from Chapter 4's registers (the placed-mark distributions, the counted cues, event frequency normalised by community size — not by borough population, which would re-import the density trap). Its display commitments:

  • Denominator on the face — the defect readers are least able to catch unaided (Chapter 1's literacy evidence), and the entire content of the Hull-outscores-London claim. If the denominator is hidden, the reordering reads as an error; shown, it reads as the finding.
  • Registers stay visible. Where a component is self-selected (the playable survey), the provenance says so on the same screen — intensity built partly from self-placed marks is legitimate and labelled, never laundered into an objective-looking number.
  • It never merges with conditions. Intensity is the identity side's summary statistic; support, space and tenure are the conditions side. One number across both is the forbidden composite arriving through the back door.

Step four — the attribution flip, displayed so it survives

The measure: origination — visits whose observable first cause was a ticket. The politics: "we attracted them here in the first place — you got that metric because of us"; the levy share; the destination-management case. [House steer.] The display problem: "because of us" is precisely where a hostile reader — an economist, a rival sector, a sceptical council — will strike, and the counter is always the same word: correlation.

And the mechanics are not speculative — they are already in the sector's hands. Every ticket carries a postcode and a purchase timestamp. [House steer, 17 August 2026.] A buyer living over a travel-time threshold implies an overnight stay; an event ending after the last train implies a taxi or a bed; destination management organisations hold hotel capacity data, and hotel price spikes already tell this story for football and for Pride — nobody disputes that Manchester hotels surge when United play at home. Almost every promoter can already tell you where their people came from and when they bought. The city-side data exists; the work is linking, anonymising and governing it, not inventing it.

The display answer is a claim ladder drawn on screen. The strong claim — the ticket was bought before the trip; the event was the visit's anchor — is displayed with its base, its anonymisation method, and its boundary: what it does not assert (that the event caused the hotel choice, the restaurant spend, the second night). The weaker associational claims sit visibly below it. The flipped sentence — "x% of overnight stays began with an event" — is a breakout statistic, and Chapter 6 of the Decision Book already legislates for it: it travels further than any chart, so it carries its source line, its base and its method in the statistic, because it will be screenshotted alone. The form that fits the fuller story is the flow display (Chapter 3's step zero family): source → visit → stay → spend, with the ledger's honesty — showing also what the night didn't originate. An attribution display that only ever flows one way is advocacy; one that shows its counter-flows is evidence.

Step five — the measure as a living series

The dynamic census reframes the measure's relationship with time, and time is a display dimension with its own commitments:

  • A snapshot presented as a state is the clipboard census's ghost. The one-night count's deepest flaw was not labour cost but stillness. The living measure displays as a series — with its seasonality honest, because nightlife breathes annually and a January reading presented bare is a lie about the year.
  • The leading-indicator claim is a two-track display. "Nightlife creates dynamism before it registers in the data" [house steer] is a claim about lag: the scene moves first, the administrative statistics move later. The display that makes this arguable rather than asserted is two time-tracks on one screen — the ticketing-led series running ahead, the official series (employment, business counts) trailing — with the lag visible and, over time, testable. This is also the university story's display: the vibe series leading, the enrolment and rent series following.
  • Freshness is part of the measure. A living series that silently stops living is the observatory again. The series displays its own last heartbeat — Chapter 8 inherits this.

Step six — the joy metric runs alongside, not instead

The standard economic development metrics are made by administrations and map what they care about; the night barely features. [House steer.] The scholarly version of the same conclusion comes from the sector's own foundational review: cultural value resists a single metric and is better held as "a framework within which sits a variety of components… each of them to be evaluated by appropriate and often different methodologies." [Evaluated — the Arts and Humanities Research Council's Cultural Value Project; agent-fetched.]

The display consequence closes the loop with Chapter 2: the measure — whatever form it takes — is designed to sit beside deprivation indices, job density and travel-to-work times, in the rooms where those are already on screen. Which means: it states its units and vintages to their standards (the comparability gate), it survives their visual conventions without adopting their judgement ramps, and it says plainly what it adds that they structurally omit. Not a rival statistics office. The missing column, installed.

07Discovery — finding your tribe

The decision

How does the instrument let a person find a scene that sounds like them — and let money find under-backed scenes — without ranking places, narrowing tastes, or exposing anyone who didn't ask to be found?

The 60-second version

"You cannot be what you cannot see." The discovery use case has two faces: the kid who likes jungle choosing Bristol over Birmingham because they can hear where their tribe is, and the funder, journalist or Business Improvement District finding the underground scene that's creating dynamism before it registers anywhere. Both are legitimate; both are dangerous; and the display evidence for both is better than expected.

Matching is not ranking. The best place-matcher ever built produced a different list for every user, from their own weights, with the score decomposed — no global "best city" existed anywhere in it. Explanations work: in the classic study, showing why something was recommended measurably improved acceptance, the winning display was a simple histogram, and 86% of users wanted explanations kept. And absence made visible is already how the grants world displays underinvestment: query a place, get almost nothing back, and the nothing is the signal.

Two warnings structure everything else. The finest scene-discovery display ever made — a map of six thousand genres where position meant sound and every scene was equally clickable — died the day its maker was laid off, because it ran at a platform's pleasure. And discoverability is a consent question before it is a feature: the live precedent for safe discovery of marginal communities is strict opt-in self-declaration. Being findable is something a scene does, never something done to it.

How the decision is made

Step one — matching, not ranking: every ordering is yours

Chapter 5 banned the league table; discovery is where the ban gets tested, because "which place suits me" genuinely wants an ordering. The resolution is in the precedents: Teleport, the city-matching tool, had users pick which aspects of life mattered to them, then produced "a list of cities that match your preferences, including a breakdown of your match score" — adjustable, regenerating, personal. [Practice-lore; the tool is now defunct.] No global ranking existed anywhere in the system: every ordering was the user's own, and the decomposition was the explanation.

That is the discovery display's constitutional rule, and it is Chapter 5's crop test surviving contact with recommendation: an ordering derived from your stated taste, displayed to you, decomposed so you can see why — that is not a league table, because it does not exist until you ask and it is nobody's ordering but yours. The moment the instrument publishes "best cities for jungle," Chapter 5 has been breached and every reactivity mechanism fires.

The state already operates this pattern one door down: Discover Uni, the Office for Students' course-comparison service, presents standardised statistics as comparison cards with no league table — the official answer to "inform choice without ranking." [Policy precedent.] And it contains nothing about scene or culture, which is precisely the hole the house steer names: the kid choosing a university chooses a city's sound, and no instrument shows it to them.

Step two — show the why, and know the persuasion trap

The recommendation-explanation evidence is directly usable. Herlocker, Konstan and Riedl tested 21 explanation interfaces with 78 users: the winning display — a simplified histogram of similar users' ratings — measurably beat unexplained recommendations, and in the follow-up, 86% of users wanted explanations added permanently. [Measured.] The design lesson from the winner: it reduced the judgement to "a single binary visual comparison."

For this instrument the explanation writes itself from Chapter 4's materials: "Why this matches you" rendered as concrete cues, not vibes-talk — you listen to jungle; Bristol runs n jungle nights a month; these x crews; free-entry tiers exist; the scene's own belonging distribution. Concrete cues are auditable (Chapter 4's rule) and they are what a life decision deserves.

And the trap is measured too. Tintarev and Masthoff's evaluation work found personalised explanations can be liked more but less effective — persuasiveness and effectiveness are different aims, and a display tuned to please converts worse decisions at higher satisfaction. [Measured.] For an instrument advising someone where to live or study, the rule follows: explanations are built for effectiveness — helping the person decide well — and tested against that, not against how much they are liked. A discovery surface that flatters its user into a bad move fails the dancefloor test at the scale of a life.

Step three — the similarity surface, and the platform-dependency rule

Every Noise at Once is the exemplar this chapter is lucky to have: roughly 6,300 genres as a spatial word-map where position encodes sound — organic to mechanical on one axis, dense to spiky on the other — every genre equally clickable, each playable in a thirty-second sample, with drill-down to artists and to "The Sound of [City]" playlists. [Practice-lore — a working artefact, never formally evaluated.] It demonstrates three things at once: similarity can be displayed without any rank; a place can be heard, which is the literal answer to "what does your city sound like?"; and wandering — adjacency browsing — is a discovery mode that no list affords.

And then it died. Its maker was laid off in Spotify's December 2023 cuts; he "lost the ability to fix any errors caused by changes on Spotify's end" and the site froze — the best scene-mapping instrument in the world, gone because it ran on one platform's data at that platform's pleasure. [Measured fact of record.] The rule this yields is structural, and it echoes both the observatory and the Centre for Thriving Places' funding logic: the discovery layer runs on data the instrument owns or is durably licensed — never at a platform's pleasure. For a studio whose data spine is its own ticketing relationships, this is an argument the business model already makes.

Step four — scene-curated pools, machine-matched delivery

Spotify's own published account of algotorial playlists gives the hybrid pattern: human editors define the pool — capturing qualities "difficult to describe in algorithms" — and the machine personalises selection and order from within it. One editorial object, millions of personal instances. [Practice-lore, first-party.] Bandcamp's discovery runs the fully human version: editorial, visible fan trails, curation by named people. [Practice-lore.]

Translated to this instrument, the pattern solves two problems at once. The scene defines the pool — which nights, crews and spaces are in the discovery layer is a curatorial and consent act by the scene (the community register's data-ethics questions bind here), keeping cultural truth in human hands. The matching personalises within it — the kid's taste finds its subset. And Bandcamp's visible-people principle serves the shine-a-light ambition directly: discovery through named promoters, resident DJs and crews — people as guides, not algorithms as oracles — which is also Chapter 4's authorship rule arriving in the discovery layer.

Step five — design against narrowing, without the folk theory

The filter-bubble claim should not be load-bearing: the strongest review evidence "finds no support for the filter bubble hypothesis" in news consumption, echo chambers measure far smaller than public debate assumes, and almost nothing tests music or place discovery. [Measured/synthesised — and the literature is news-focused, which limits transfer.] So the chapter does not justify its rules by bubble-fear. It designs against narrowing on first principles: a discovery instrument for scenes exists to widen what people can see, so adjacency is a feature — "close to your taste, new to you" — and the wandering surface (step three) affords it structurally. Engineered serendipity is a live research area rather than settled practice [evaluated but immature]; it is probe territory for the prototype, not a rule.

Step six — the money view: absence made visible

For funders, philanthropists, brand partners and Business Improvement Districts, the display precedent is GrantNav, the open UK grants navigator: funders use it to "see how your grants align or overlap with others" and to "find gaps, overlaps or chances to work with others." [Practice-lore, verbatim.] Its deep pattern: underinvestment displays as visible absence — query the place, get almost nothing back, and the nothing is the finding. The map of money, never a league table of the deserving.

The instrument's move is the overlay this sector has never had: the map of money laid over the map of scenes. High intensity (Chapter 6's measure, denominator visible) where the grants layer is empty is the opportunity display — "underinvested but alive" as a picture rather than a plea. The sweep found no platform anywhere that displays high-potential underground culture to investment — genuine white space, and this book's second candidate original contribution after Chapter 4's "what this changed" field.

Two guards, both already in the book's spine. The exposure risk: an opportunity display is "vocabulary and coordinates" for speculation as well as for support, so the community register's governing question — redistribute opportunity, rather than simply display what already exists — decides what the money view shows, and consent (step seven) decides who is in it. And the return mechanism: Fairbnb's cooperative precedent — half of platform fees funding community projects chosen by residents — shows discovery revenue flowing back to the discovered. [Practice-lore.] The house already built that logic: a 1% ticket contribution into a community-owned fund is the return mechanism, constituted. The discovery layer and the Community Benefit Society are the same argument at two scales.

Step seven — consent to be found

The live precedent for safe discovery of marginal communities is Everywhere Is Queer: a world map of queer-owned businesses that is strictly opt-in — businesses submit themselves — where being on the map is an act of self-declaration shaped by safety. [Practice-lore.] That, plus Chapter 4's consent architecture, plus the sector's own history — a scene findable to audiences is also findable to enforcement, and Form 696 is the case study nobody in this house needs explained — yields the closing rules:

  • Discoverability is opt-in at scene level, with granularity the scene controls: findable to audiences but not to the money view; findable this season but not archived forever; findable as a genre presence but not as named nights.
  • A scene can see how it is findable, and change it. The sweep found no platform with community-level control over being recommended — another white space this instrument would pioneer, and one the Community Benefit Society's governance is built to hold.
  • Default invisibility for the informal. The unlicensed and the underground appear in aggregate character (the city sounds like this) without coordinates, unless they opt upward. Chapter 3's unlit-is-not-dead rule, now with a consent mechanism behind it.

Pressure points

Where there is real latitude.

Sound as the display medium. A discovery surface that plays is native to this domain and almost unoccupied. The sound-of-place is the one artefact in this book nobody else can fake.

The wandering map. Adjacency browsing across scene-space — the Every Noise affordance, rebuilt on owned data with the night's aesthetics — is probe-ready and gallery-worthy.

Named guides. Human curation as interface — the promoter as the door into the scene — extends the studio's whole philosophy into the product surface.

The repeatable task. [House steer, 17 Aug.] An advocacy instrument is opened twice a year; a discovery instrument is opened twice a week — and the twice-a-week use is what keeps the data fresh, the scenes visible and the instrument fundable. Discovery is therefore not a feature of the dashboard; it is the retention engine — the repeatable task (finding events and scenes, beyond a basic listing site) that makes the whole instrument a habit rather than a report. The platform horizon this could grow into — partnership data spines, an affiliate return-path that benefits musicians — is recorded in the use-case register as hypothetical and post-prototype; the retention requirement binds now.

Where it looks sophisticated and fails.

The listicle output. Any export, share-card or press view that flattens matches into "top cities." The crop test applies to what the user's screen can generate too.

Taste-lock. Matching so tight the kid only ever sees jungle. The adjacency row is not optional furniture; it is the anti-narrowing mechanism.

The unconsented spotlight. Surfacing a scene to the money view, or to anyone, because its data was harvestable. Opt-in or absent.

Platform-borrowed discovery. Building the layer on a streaming service's similarity data — the exact dependency that killed the exemplar.

Vibes-talk explanations. "This city has amazing energy" is a persuasion display. Cues or nothing.

And the Form 696 test, standing for the whole chapter: if an enforcement body opened the discovery layer tomorrow, what could they do that they cannot do today? The answer must be nothing the scene didn't choose to show.

The decision

How does the surface stay honest about what it doesn't know — what is missing, how sure, and how old — in every state a reader can reach?

The 60-second version

Three honesties, and the evidence says each fails differently. Gaps: readers cannot detect missing data unaided — it was the joint-hardest defect in the misleading-chart literacy study (0.39 baseline) — so a silent gap simply reads as less. Uncertainty: the best-evidenced territory in the whole visualisation field — discrete, frequency-framed forms measurably beat intervals and text, and hard visual boundaries make readers sort the world into inside/outside even after extensive instruction. Freshness: the failure that killed the closest precedent — a live container over contents up to seven years stale, one dataset in twenty-four current, and the one alive being the one somebody else automates.

Interaction multiplies all three. A static chart makes one claim you can check; an instrument makes a claim for every combination of filters, and honesty that holds only on the default view is a demo, not a design. Gaps render as gaps in every state; intervals widen as the base shrinks and are carried or the value is withheld; suppression is enforced at query time and explained — because "no data for this combination" and "suppressed to protect confidentiality" and "not yet measured anywhere" are three different messages, and only the instrument that distinguishes them keeps a reader's trust.

This chapter is where the instrument's thesis and its craft coincide: for the value column, the gaps are the argument — so the honest surface is not a compliance layer here. It is the display of the case itself.

How the decision is made

Step one — gaps are rendered, at full size, with their reason

The Decision Book settles the baseline — a gap in the data is a finding: dashed segments, hatched fills, visible breaks, never a silent omission, never interpolation across a collection break [axiom, carried into Style Guide §12] — and the literacy evidence explains why it is Fixed rather than nice: in the misleading-chart study, missing data was among the defects readers were least able to detect unaided (baseline correctness 0.39 against 0.80 on clean items). [Measured.] A reader will not infer the hole. The display must draw it.

This book has already extended the rule twice, and both extensions consolidate here. Every gap carries its reason, from a fixed vocabulary: never measured · not yet collected · suppressed for confidentiality · community-held (consented non-publication, per Chapter 4) · collection break. And gaps render in every filter state — the Decision Book's interactive rule [axiom, Ch 15] — because a gap that appears on the default view and vanishes under filtering has become a silent zero somewhere a reader is standing.

For this instrument the rule is also the rhetoric: the value column's labelled gaps — belonging: not yet measured anywhere — are the two-columns thesis made visible. Honest absence is not this instrument's weakness to manage. It is its central exhibit.

Step two — uncertainty in forms the evidence supports

This is the most settled evidence in the field, verified in the raw research and graduating here:

  • Discrete, frequency-framed forms win. Quantile dotplots — the value as fifty countable dots — achieved 97% of optimal decision performance (n = 408, incentive-compatible payoffs, confidence interval [95%, 98%]), beating a no-uncertainty control and halving decision variance; cumulative-probability curves performed nearly as well; textual interval statements were unstable and improved little with practice. [Measured.]
  • Hard boundaries mislead durably. Error bars and cone-style bounds induce deterministic construal — readers sort outcomes into inside/outside — and the effect "persists even with extensive instructions." [Measured.] An interval drawn as a hard-edged bracket is a claim about a cliff that does not exist.
  • There is no one-size-fits-all. Animated outcome draws beat static forms for multi-variable judgements and lose for single-value estimates on volatile data; the value-suppressing palettes shifted behaviour in a 24-person study without improving accuracy. [Measured, with the small-n caveat carried.] The form is chosen per task, and the choice is recorded.

The house rules already say the rest and are inherited as axioms: a range is a legitimate headline; uncertainty can itself be the finding — "±1.4" belongs in the headline, not the footnote — and never round to imply precision the evidence does not support [axioms, §12 and Decision Book Ch 6].

Under interaction, one more rule becomes load-bearing. Intervals widen as the reader filters and the base shrinks; most instruments show the point estimate regardless. The Decision Book's rule is inherited whole: carry the uncertainty through every filter state, or below a stated base threshold replace the estimate with a range, and below the disclosure threshold withhold it entirely [axiom, Ch 15]. The felt layer already obeys this by construction — Chapter 4's distributions of placed marks are the uncertainty display — which is one reason the placed-self form was the right choice.

Step three — freshness is a display element with a heartbeat

The observatory gave this book its founding case: a maintained container over unmaintained contents, update labels reading "almost 7 years ago," one dataset in roughly twenty-four current — the one an external body publishes monthly. The community named the same requirement independently: a map not "actively maintained and ethically governed… risks becoming outdated or extractive as soon as it is published." [House, confidential.] And the scene's own steer made freshness economic: the dynamic census only means anything if the series visibly lives.

The consolidated rules:

  • The heartbeat on the face: every view states when its data last moved — data to Thursday — as first-screen furniture (Chapter 1's rule), not dataset-page metadata.
  • Per-dataset refresh mechanism named (Chapter 1's observatory rule): not a maintenance owner for the whole, but the mechanism for each layer — because the container and its contents age independently, and the front page shows only the container.
  • Seasonality honesty (Chapter 6): nightlife breathes annually; a series shown without its rhythm makes January a lie about July.
  • Staleness degrades visibly. When a layer stops refreshing, the display says so and shows its age — it does not keep rendering confidently from a stopped clock. The instrument that cannot afford to maintain a layer retires it visibly, which is the anti-observatory move: better an honest amputation than a phantom limb.

Step four — suppression is enforced at query time, and explained

The thresholds are axioms — no published figure rests on fewer than three contributing units; secondary suppression so totals cannot give the cell away; categories fixed across editions so tables cannot be differenced [axioms, §13]. What this chapter adds is where and how they display in an interactive instrument:

  • At query time, on every combination — because aggregate-safe does not mean filter-safe; a drill-down that reaches a cell of two is "a disclosure breach with a user interface" [axiom, Decision Book Ch 15].
  • With the reason stated. The empty state distinguishes its three species: no data for this combination · suppressed to protect confidentiality — fewer than three contributors · not yet measured. The second message is the one that builds trust rather than spending it, and the style guide's [c] marker already exists for it — the row stays, the gap is visible.
  • On the national statistical office's precedent, held in the raw research: the census flexible-table architecture enforces fixed classifications (users cannot request category subsets — which is what prevents differencing), applies perturbation keyed to the cell so the same cell answers identically every time, and accepts, openly, that it does not track cumulative risk across a user's query history. That accepted trade-off is available to this instrument too — if declared. Undeclared, the same choice reads as ignorance.

Step five — small scenes are where all four honesties collide

At city scale these rules are hygiene; at scene scale they are safety. A scene of forty people makes one placed mark with a genre and a night attached a name (Chapter 4); an under-threshold cell in a hyperlocal cut is a person; a freshness stamp on a tiny layer can reveal when someone contributed. The standing test is inherited and sharpened: filter deliberately, as an adversary, toward a small scene — through genre × ward × month — and verify that what appears is the explained suppression message, the widened range, the labelled gap; never the confident small number. If that walk has not been taken, the instrument has been demoed, not tested.

Worked example

Illustrative — one reader's filter walk; fixes nothing about the design.

A reader lands on a ward view — heartbeat top right: ticketing to Thursday · felt layer to March · grants layer: retired June 2026, shown at last state. Three ages, three honesties, no pretence of one.

They filter to a genre. The intensity figure becomes a range — base below 200; showing the spread, not the point — and the felt-layer panel switches from median-plus-distribution to distribution only, because at this base the middle is noise.

They filter to genre × month. Two venues' rows now read [c] — suppressed: fewer than three contributors — and the monthly series shows a hatched band across February: collection break — platform outage, not absence of events.

They push to genre × ward × month — the adversary's walk, taken by an ordinary user. The panel yields entirely: below the disclosure threshold at this combination. Zoom out one step to see this scene safely. The message names the threshold, offers the way back, and never shows the confident small number.

Nothing on any screen in the walk displayed a bare point estimate on a shrunken base, a silent zero, or a stale layer wearing a live face. That, rather than any single chart, is what "honest surface" means.

Pressure points

Where there is real latitude.

The visual language of absence. Hatching, ghosted vessels, the unlit-but-not-dead treatment on dark ground — Chapter 2 opened this and it remains the most distinctive craft surface the instrument owns. Honest absence, beautifully drawn, is the house style nobody can copy.

Uncertainty as the headline. "Somewhere between 90 and 140 nights a month — and that width is itself the finding about how little the city counts" is a stronger sentence than any point estimate. The house already believes this; the evidence licenses it.

The retirement notice. A layer retired with a dated, dignified marker — this count ended when the funding did — turns a maintenance failure into evidence about the sector. Even the instrument's own decay can tell the truth.

Where it looks rigorous and fails.

The silent zero. The empty cell rendered as nothing — indistinguishable from measured absence. The single most common honest-surface failure in the wild.

The confident tail. Point estimates surviving filtering down to bases where the interval is wider than the value.

The hard bracket. Crisp error bars on soft numbers — inside/outside sorting installed by the display itself.

Freshness in the footer. The observatory's disease: the label exists, where nobody looks.

The undeclared trade-off. Adopting the no-cumulative-tracking compromise silently, or perturbing inconsistently so the same query wobbles — either discovered later costs more trust than both cost to declare.

What proves a break works. Take the adversary's walk to a small scene and read every message aloud. Then unplug the pipeline for a week in staging and look at the instrument: does it visibly age — heartbeats lagging, layers greying — or does it keep smiling? An instrument that cannot look stale cannot be trusted to look fresh.

The decision

What must an interaction buy to justify existing — and what does the evidence say each one costs?

The 60-second version

The default position is measured, and it is against. Across roughly 4,300 real web sessions, adding narrative introductions to exploratory visualisations was null to negative — the no-story group explored roughly twice as long (108.8 seconds against 54) and performed more operations, and only 35.4% of readers followed the intended sequence at all. The newsroom telemetry points the same way: 10–15% of readers touch the interaction layer, and the author of that number's most famous correction drew the operative rule himself — "if some information is crucial, don't make the user click or hover to see it."

The evidenced exceptions are narrow, and the narrowness is the useful part: prediction-before-reveal improves recall — in the visual modality only, for unfamiliar data only; staged animated transitions improve tracking and estimation — until over-staged. And the few readers who do interact are not free agents: the first-shown view measurably anchors what they explore and what they conclude, through twenty-five minutes of free use.

So every interaction in this instrument starts owing. It pays its way with a named purchase — and this book has already accumulated the purchases that are genuinely worth paying for: personalisation (your place), collection (the placed mark), chosen comparison (your peers), adjacency (new to you), and contribution that returns. An interaction that cannot name its purchase from that list is decoration with a maintenance bill.

How the decision is made

Step one — start from the null, not from enthusiasm

The evidence base, all previously verified and in the ledger:

  • The null. Boy, Détienne and Fekete's three field experiments: narrative introductions did not increase engagement with exploration; the no-story condition explored about twice as long; only about a third followed the authored sequence. [Measured.]
  • The reach. The New York Times' internal analytics: 10–15% of readers interact — with Aisch's published correction that the famous 85% "didn't ignore the graphic, they just didn't click the button." The graphic worked; the button was optional. [Measured, internal; the correction is the point.]
  • The steering. Cho and colleagues: the initially-shown view of an interactive tool significantly biased which views users then used and their final estimates, despite twenty-five minutes of free exploration. [Measured.] Interaction does not free the reader from the author; it hides the authorship.

None of this says "don't build interactions." It says: the static reading of every view must already work (Chapters 1–8 built exactly that), and interaction is a paid extension, never a prerequisite.

The decision

How are authored narrative and reader-driven exploration sequenced in one instrument — and where does the story actually live?

The 60-second version

The field has a beautiful vocabulary and no evidence. The canonical taxonomy of narrative visualisation — seven genres, and the three named patterns: the martini glass (authored stem, then open exploration), the interactive slideshow, the drill-down story — comes from a study of 58 professional pieces that contains no user research at all, and its authors said so. Scrollytelling, the era's default form, is essentially unevaluated as a comprehension technique. These are menus, not findings; a genre is chosen as a bet and named as one.

What is known reshapes the question. Story built as a gate suppresses exploration (the Chapter 9 null). Authored defaults are already rhetoric — "default views provide an initial point of interpretation" — so the instrument is telling a story before any narrative layer is added. The strongest institutional precedent chose writing: the Office for National Statistics, with every interactive tool available, told its census results as 331 semi-automated written local stories, because "we need to pull out the narrative and the story to help guide our users through the numbers." And the best interactive-story finding in the sector's own family is that a decade of reader reweighting became a collection channel — the story readers told back.

So the resolution: the story is the authored spine this book already built — the default sentence, the portrait, the bridging line — generated per place, in writing, with exploration standing beside it, skippable in both directions. Not story then explore. Story beside explore, and the story is five words where it can be.

How the decision is made

Step one — treat the genre taxonomy as a menu, and choose bets knowingly

Segel and Heer's design space — seven genres, the author-driven/reader-driven axis, the three sequencing patterns — is the field's shared vocabulary and carries no evaluation; the authors flagged the absence of reader-centric research themselves. [Evaluated as taxonomy; unmeasured as prescription.] The martini glass is the structurally obvious bet for an evidence instrument (authored claim, then exploration), and this book takes it as a bet, recorded as one — with one amendment the evidence forces (step two).

Scrollytelling's status is worse than unproven: the raw research found it essentially unevaluated for comprehension — one adjacent study in another domain found engagement gains with comprehension merely equal. [Absence, verified in the sweep.] It is not banned; it is priced: a scrollytold piece is an editorial artefact the studio might publish from the instrument, not the instrument's own spine.

Step two — never build the story as a gate

The Chapter 9 null bites sequencing directly: narrative introductions ahead of exploration halved exploration time, and only a third of readers followed the authored order. Bret Victor's design principle — the oldest in the explorable-explanations tradition — said it first: interactivity, and narrative, remain optional. The reader who wants the answer gets it at once (Chapter 1's default sentence is the story's first line); the reader who wants to wander wanders without toll.

So the martini glass is amended to the house shape: not a stem you must drink through, but a poster beside an open door. The authored layer is short, front-loaded and complete in itself; the exploratory layer is reachable from the first screen; each links into the other at every step (a finding in exploration links to its written line; every written line links to the view that shows it — which Chapter 9's stable state addresses make possible).

Step three — the defaults are already the narrative; author them as one

Hullman and Diakopoulos named it: sequence, selection and default views are procedural rhetoric — the story is being told by what is shown first, compared by default and omitted. [Evaluated.] This book has already authored those choices deliberately (Chapters 1, 2, 5, 6). This chapter adds only the honesty rule: the instrument's narrative positions are stated, not embedded. The two-columns frame, the value-first ordering, the intensity denominators — a "how this instrument argues" page says what the design asserts and why, in the same register as the methodology. The alternative — letting the rhetoric sit unacknowledged in the defaults — fails the hostile-reader test the moment a sceptical analyst notices it, and they will.

The strongest precedent is the one from the Chapter 1 evidence, now given its full weight: the Office for National Statistics faced the same problem at national scale — every place wanting its own story — and solved it with semi-automated written narrative: 331 local stories, generated from data through authored templates, because prose "pull[s] out the narrative… to help guide our users through the numbers." [Practice, institutional.]

This is the house pattern waiting to be claimed. The Croydon sentence of Chapter 5 — "scene at Hackney-level intensity on Enfield-level support" — is a template with authored voice and per-place values: written story, generated for every place, in the studio's register, governed by §03/§04 and the five-word discipline where headlines allow. The felt layer's testimony (Chapter 4) supplies the human line no template can, with its consent architecture already built. Story, at scale, without a scrolly in sight — and every generated sentence carries Chapter 6's discipline, because a generated claim is still a claim.

The Better Life Index's decade-long lesson: letting readers reweight became "an important back channel" — tens of thousands of shared configurations telling the publisher what people value. [Practice, sustained.] Chapters 4 and 7 built this instrument's versions: the placed mark, the guess, the chosen peers, the taste profile. The story is not only told to the city; it accumulates from it. That closes the loop with the Vibe Setting Framework's own principle — visible, evidenced listening — at product scale.

The decision

Can the accessible version be the primary version — and what does that require deciding at the start, when it is still cheap?

The 60-second version

The floor is settled and inherited: full keyboard operation including chart elements, visible focus, 200% resize and 400% zoom without spill, semantic structure, alt text generated from the current state, user override of colour, contrast and font — and the tooling chosen against those requirements at project start, because they are unfixable later. That is the Decision Book's dashboard chapter and the style guide's §13, and none of it is re-derived here.

What this chapter adds is the frontier position: for this instrument, accessibility is not a parallel version to maintain — it converges with the product. The evidence: charts served as navigable hierarchical structures reduced cognitive load for blind screen-reader users against flat tables (a real study, 13 participants); newer work authors visual, textual and sonified representations as peers from one data abstraction (5 participants — promising, not proven). And this is a music instrument: the sound of a place is simultaneously its most distinctive display (Chapter 7) and a native non-visual channel. The written story layer (Chapter 10) is simultaneously narrative craft and the screen-reader spine. The plain-language register (§04) is simultaneously house voice and cognitive access. The good version and the accessible version keep turning out to be the same build.

The strategic frame seals it: the regulations likely don't bind this instrument, but a government user cannot embed or cite a non-compliant product without inheriting the problem. Meeting the standard is a distribution decision. The audit exists and is cheap: fifty heuristics, ten critical, two to eight hours.

How the decision is made

Step one — inherit the floor, and choose tooling against it now

The Fixed floor, consolidated from the Decision Book's Chapter 15 and §13 [axioms]: entire instrument keyboard-operable including chart elements, data points and legends; visible focus, logical tab order; 200% resize and 400% zoom without spill or horizontal scroll; large tables paginated; semantic structure with no skipped levels; alt text generated from the current filter state, stating the finding; users able to override colour, contrast and font — which rules out canvas-only charting; observed task-based testing with real users. And the rule that makes all of them affordable: the tool choice is an accessibility decision made in week one — a charting stack that renders to inaccessible surfaces cannot be retrofitted at the audit.

The audit instrument is Chartability: fifty heuristics under seven principles, ten flagged critical, a two-to-eight-hour full pass, validated across audit environments and adopted by major toolmakers. [Evaluated, adopted.] It is scheduled per release, not per crisis.

Step two — serve structure, not pictures of structure

The strongest design model in the accessible-visualisation literature is structure / navigation / description: a chart exposed to a screen reader as a navigable hierarchy — overview, then axes and groupings, then marks — rather than one alt sentence or a flat data table. In the founding study, hierarchical navigation reduced cognitive load for blind and visually-impaired users versus flat tables (13 participants). [Measured, small-n.] It ships as open tooling that builds the navigable tree from standard chart specifications — turning the accessible chart from bespoke cost into build step.

For this instrument the model maps directly onto structures the book already built: the portrait navigates as identity → axes → marks; the filter walk announces Chapter 8's states ("suppressed: fewer than three contributors" is already screen-reader-ready wording); the two columns navigate as columns. The information architecture and the accessibility architecture are one drawing.

Step three — author multimodal from the abstraction, and notice what this instrument is

The frontier work authors visual, textual and sonified representations as peers from a single data abstraction — not sonification bolted onto a finished chart, but three renderings of one structure. Evaluated so far with five expert screen-reader users: promising, not proven — and this book prices it that way. [Evaluated, very small n.]

But notice what the instrument is. Its subject is sound; its Chapter 7 discovery surface already plays places; its most distinctive asset is audio nobody else holds. For every other dashboard in the world, sonification is an accessibility feature. For this one, the audio channel is the product converging with access: the sound of the scene serves the blind user and the curious kid identically. The rule: audio renderings are designed once, as first-class display, to serve both — never twice, as feature and afterthought. Step five — the distribution argument, made once

The public-sector accessibility regulations almost certainly do not bind an independent studio's instrument; the Equality Act's reasonable-adjustments duty does. The decision

The 60-second version

The founding instruction, from the Skiddle call: "with a really high design standard, we make it look like art… not look like a government fucking boring thing." The founding fear is that rigour forbids it. The evidence says the opposite — the rules don't forbid the art; they name where it lives.

And the institutional proof exists: an expressive rendering of noise data hangs in MoMA's permanent collection.

How the decision is made

Step one — know the three tiers here better than anywhere

The tier model does its hardest work in this chapter. Fixed holds everything Chapters 1–11 built: contrast, structure, honesty, disclosure, the recipe on the face. Step two — read the embellishment evidence as a licence with conditions

  • The "useful junk" claim — embellishment aiding memorability without harming accuracy — has been contested since it was made. [Contested; the original study stands but generalises poorly.]

[Measured.] Decoration doesn't carry the message; words do — so decoration is free because the words are doing their job (Chapter 1's sentence rule was the licence all along).

[Measured/evaluated.] Translation: the government template isn't neutral; it's invisible. Distinctiveness is not a risk to seriousness — sameness is a risk to being seen at all.
