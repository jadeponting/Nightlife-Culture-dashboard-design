# Chapter 1 — newcomer read

## 1. What it teaches

The chapter decides that a dashboard is not a screen but an argument: a display that turns data into a decision for a named reader, which changes the thing it measures and is trusted only as far as its makers are trusted and checkable. It is written for anyone about to build or commission a public-interest dashboard, with a nightlife evidence base as the running example, and it sets the ground rules every later chapter will lean on. After reading it I would write down the reader and the decision before opening any tool, release the data alongside the display, show uncertainty as a numeric range, show and justify the unit I aggregate to, and plan from day one how I will know whether anyone opens the thing.

## 2. One rule, and why it exists

"Rankings by rate are screening instruments, never verdicts; show intervals." The chapter earns this three times over. Wainer's de Moivre argument shows that small units land at both ends of any rate ranking purely because they are small (a county of 100 with one cancer death jumps from the lowest tenth to the highest). Gelman and Price show that the obvious fix, shrinking small-area estimates toward the mean, simply moves the extremes to the big, well-sampled places, so no setting of the dial makes the ranking honest. And Goldstein and Spiegelhalter's analysis found that about two-thirds of the pairwise comparisons in a school league table could not be statistically separated. A ranking therefore tells you where to look, not what you will find, and the only honest display carries the interval.

## 3. Where I stalled

- "**and the Vibe Map are all**" (§1, "The definition this book uses"). The Vibe Map arrives in a list of example dashboards with no introduction; it is explained only in §11. A newcomer reads it as a product they should already know.
- "**The eighth of the brief's failures**" (§6, opening). "The brief" has not been introduced as a thing; whose brief, what it is, and that it contains eight failures all arrive later (§8). The sentence also points forward to section 8 for a list the reader has not seen.
- "**Fixed (sequence)**", "**Fixed (statement of the record)**" (§9, truths 1 to 16). The tier vocabulary is used in §9 before the pointer "Kind and tier as in Chapter 0" appears above the rules table. Chapter 0 does define it, so the reference delivers, but a reader meeting the words in §9 has no signpost yet. Within §9 the usage also wobbles: truth 5 ("the countable proxy moves before the goal") is tiered "Fixed (sequence)" although it is a finding, while truth 10, also a finding, is "statement of the record". I could not tell what sequence truth 5 fixes.
- "**the 2020 panel's**" (§8 table, row 2; §10 "How much to frame"). No "panel" is ever named. I assume it means the Ivanković expert appraisal of COVID dashboards from §4, but the chapter does not say so.
- "**Dong (anomaly detection as the heart**" (§8 table, row 6) and "**Dong et al.**" (§9 truth 16, rules). The note above the table says "Author names refer to the studies introduced above", but the Johns Hopkins builders are never named as Dong in §4. The same applies to "**Lee et al.**" (truth 9 and the rules; §6 says only "a 2025 paper in political science"), "**Hawkins**" (truth 14; the textbook outlier definition is quoted unattributed in §6), "**Pascale & Sternin**" (rules; the positive-deviance practitioners are unnamed in §6) and "**Velcu-Laitinen & Yigitbasioglu**" (rules; the Finnish survey is unnamed in §1). A reader has to go to the Sources list to join them up.
- "**Few 2007**" (rules, row 2). The body quotes Few's 2004 definition; the rules table cites 2007. Probably a different Few piece from the sources list, but it reads as a mismatch.
- "**†**" (rules table, several rows). The dagger is used throughout the table and explained only in the italic note at the top of Sources, 130 lines later.
- "**The simplest solution to the MAUP**" (§6). The phrase "modifiable areal unit problem" is given, but the abbreviation MAUP is never spelled out as such before it appears inside Openshaw's quote. Similarly "**OSR 2021**" (rules, row 4): the Office for Statistics Regulation is named in §4 but the abbreviation OSR never is.
- "**and then classified around**" (§1, Bach paragraph). I had to read this twice to get "quoted, then classified in ways that ignore the definition". It is compressed to the point of opacity.
- "**Participation … showed medium to large effects**" (§3, Schools). The next sentence says this rests on nine studies, five medium or large against four small or negligible, mostly confounded. "Medium to large" and "five of nine, mostly confounded" are two different claims and the paragraph asserts the stronger one first.
- "**Bach's workshop participants named**" (§1, lineages). Bach was introduced as a review of 144 dashboards; the workshop is new information dropped in mid-sentence.
- "**The suburban hub that holds a music community together**" and "**the value column will stay empty**" (§11). Both read as references to a specific case and a specific data model the chapter assumes I know. Which hub? Which value column?
- "**85% of people trust**" (60-second version) against "**85% of people who expressed a view**" (§5). Not a stall exactly, but the headline drops the qualifier the body is careful to keep.
- "**Chapter 14 returns to what that teaches**", "**Chapter 12's insistence**", "**the deviation entry Chapter 16 describes**". Forward references I could not check from this chapter. They read plausibly; I note them only because I cannot confirm they deliver.

## 4. What reads like it was written for the people who made it, not for me

- "That passage is widely reproduced; the original article is paywalled and the wording has not been checked against it here." (§1, lineages) — a verification note.
- "the definition here is taken from Endsley's own later restatement, since the original is paywalled." (§2, Belief one) — a verification note.
- "(Porter is quoted here from a published excerpt collection; the book itself is on the reading list for Chapter 5.)" (§2, Belief four) — a to-do for the writers.
- "Only its abstract has been read for this chapter; the typology belongs in Chapter 7." (§3, Government) — a process note about what has and has not been read.
- "This chapter looked for the number and could not find one that was independent." (§3, Business) — borderline; the absence is itself a finding, but "this chapter looked" is the writers talking about their own search. The rules table then cites "**This chapter's search**" as a Source.
- "Any dashboard built only from official sources will be blind in exactly the places Carly's brief cares about" (§6, small cells) — the brief is cited as a source, which is allowed, but the reader has not been shown what it cares about.
- "The analytical move is the one Carly describes in words" (§6, outlier) — cites her without quoting or pointing to where she says it; the reader has to take it on trust.
- "A positive-deviance analysis on the Vibe Map's own data is one of the clearest early experiments available." (§6) — project planning, not teaching.
- "The candidate canonical truths this chapter contributes, each with its kind and firmness. Later chapters test them; the closing chapter confirms or demotes them." (§9 lead-in) — book-production machinery. "Candidate", "contributes", "confirms or demotes" describe how the book is being assembled.
- "Adaptable — and the order is a Nightlife Culture decision." (§10, Which failures) — asks someone to decide.
- "The project's months of convening are not a preamble to the dashboard" (§11) — assumes the reader knows the project's timeline.
- "the link was opened on 5 October 2026 and the quotation taken from it, 'spot-checked' and 'verified' that it was re-opened and re-traced on 6 October" (Sources, italic note) — a verification log. Likewise "full text read for a later chapter" (Ivanković; van der Bles) and "the three statistics quoted rest on the full text" (Purich).
- "not checked against HBR" in bold (Sources, Kaplan & Norton) and "quoted via a published excerpt compilation, not the book" in bold (Porter) — verification status shouted at the reader.

Not flagged: "Carly's eight failures" (§8) and "The brief, version 2 — Carly's failure register" (Sources), which cite her brief as a source; the Vibe Map funding deck quotations in §11, which are cited as published work.

## 5. Best passage

"In 1984 the geographer Stan Openshaw took one dataset…" — the whole of §6. Six statistical facts, each with a named case and a number, each ending in a rule. It is the part of the chapter I could act on immediately without knowing anything about the project.

## 6. Verdict

Yes, with friction: a research partner or a funder's analyst could use the argument, the evidence and the rules as they stand, while a creative technologist would get the checklist and the rules table but trip on the tier vocabulary arriving before its signpost, the register's author names that the body never introduces, and the Vibe Map and "the brief" appearing as things already known.
