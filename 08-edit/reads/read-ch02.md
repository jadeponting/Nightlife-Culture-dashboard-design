## B. What the chapter teaches

The chapter decides that a dashboard has no reader in general: eight kinds of reader (officer, councillor, regeneration team, developer, funder, investor, trade body, the scene) each make a different decision, accept different evidence and keep a different clock, and grade the same screen at once by rules that contradict each other. It establishes, from the Treasury's own books, that a dashboard is monitoring, not evaluation, and sits on the first or second rung of every evidence ladder in use. It then shows, from the evidence-use literature and the persuasion experiments, that what moves readers is source, timing and relationship rather than rigour, that charts persuade the undecided and may backfire on the opposed, and that the big number and the human story both do less than folklore says. From the placemaking field it takes the lesson that a place indicator is a description, not an attribution, and that every valuation headline sheds its caveats downstream. A newcomer would take away one rule: name each reader, their decision, their evidence rule and their clock; choose openly whose grammar the first screen speaks; keep the others reachable.
