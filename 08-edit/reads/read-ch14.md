## B. What the chapter teaches

The chapter draws one line: everything in a display that makes a claim about the world (data, encodings, titles, units, sources, dates, gates) is the evidence layer; everything a designer adds on purpose (register, palette beyond the encoding, type, illustration, sound, motion that means nothing) is the expressive layer. It gives the line a formal name (Mackinlay's expressiveness criterion: all the facts and only the facts), a procedure to check it (the removal test: mono, silent, still, undressed, then stripped beside dressed), and a record (the boundary log). It tells a designer what the evidence says expression buys (engagement, mood, recognition, memorability) and does not buy (accuracy, empathy, action), prices three choices (the dark ground, the drawn look, the invented form), shows that plainness is also a style that makes a trust claim, and says what a dashboard about sound owes a deaf reader. A newcomer leaves knowing which side of the line any mark falls on, how to test it, and that the whole expressive side is theirs.

Written for the creative technologist or visual designer who will propose the Vibe Map's look and needs to know what they may not touch, and for whoever audits them.
