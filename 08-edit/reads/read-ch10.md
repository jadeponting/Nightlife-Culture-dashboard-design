### B. What the chapter teaches (as a reader)

The chapter splits the brief's "without exposing or exploiting" into two problems with two literatures: exposure is statistical disclosure, old and measured; exploitation is consent, benefit and voice, where the law protects persons and has nothing for communities. It decides that a filter is a table builder and must be protected as a query system, stricter than a table; that a community cannot consent and is instead consulted, governs and agrees; that "known but not shown" is a declared status for a category, never a marker on a unit; and that the harm a dashboard does runs through representation and must be assessed for persons, groups and places. It is written for someone building or commissioning a public dashboard about people, in the UK, with no legal training, and it says repeatedly that it reports the law and does not advise. A newcomer takes away seven truths, a fourteen-line "safeguards record", and the knowledge that protecting the small by hiding them is a second kind of failure.

Best passages (must survive any edit):
- Lines 58–92, the eight beliefs. Each ends with why the belief persists ("because anonymisation feels like an operation performed on a file, and the file does not change", line 68). Concrete, rhythmic, a point of view.
- Lines 154–184, the methods and their costs, especially line 162: "A dashboard that protects the small by suppressing them has protected them from exposure and delivered them to erasure". And line 170: "The census protects by doubt, not absence".
- Lines 470–478, the form and the three nothings — the chapter's own finding, and the sentence "None of them hides the hiding; and none of them marks the hidden unit" (line 472).
- Lines 665–683, the nightlife illustration ("show us, don't expose us") and lines 739–743, the break test, which is two conversations rather than a checklist.

Passages a newcomer could not follow:
