### B. What the chapter teaches (as a reader, not the author)

The chapter decides that "learning from the best" means taking other sectors' *mechanisms* (a printed rule, a stated reason, evidence or a named cost) and never their content or their look; it gives a three-word vocabulary for what fails the test (a look, a habit, a bet) and a nine-line record for anything borrowed. For a newcomer (a creative technologist or analyst) the takeaway is a short list of practices public health, finance, place statistics, the culture and charity sector and the big open-data projects have written down: a page per metric, a labelled change log kept apart from the refresh, vintages, the revision/correction distinction, no arrow and no judgement colour without a printed significance rule, a "can and cannot say" text beside any ranking, a trust question asked the way the statistics office asks it, and a retirement plan written before launch. It also teaches a negative: trust in a *display* is almost never measured, reach is counted in units that do not convert, and public data projects end, so the plan for ending is part of the design.
