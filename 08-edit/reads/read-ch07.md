## B. What the chapter teaches

The chapter decides what each of a dashboard's four comparisons (place against place, this period against last, before against after, what is against what might be, plus a fifth discipline of reading borrowed market indices) may claim and must declare. For a newcomer it settles: a rank is a noisy summary that changes what it ranks, so a dashboard of small places shows position in a distribution with its interval, not a league table; like is compared with like by standardisation and peers chosen by a published method; a trend line and a seasonally adjusted series are models someone chose, revised when data arrive; "after" is not "because", and each counterfactual design earns a specific verb on a published ladder; a projection is a range with its assumptions, switching value and track record. The reader leaves with a ten-line comparison record to put beneath any comparison, and a filled example of one.

Best passages, which must survive any edit:
- Lines 100–102: the Indices of Deprivation arithmetic ("a small area with a rank of 1,000 is not half as deprived as a place with a rank of 500") into the *Doing Business* post-mortem, ending "a quintile band is a rank with fewer rows". Concrete, cumulative, with a point of view.
- Lines 216–218: Campbell and Stanley's eight rivals, then the paragraph that turns them into a checklist for "a line crossing a vertical marker labelled 'policy introduced'", ending "A reader shown the line and the marker and nothing else is being asked to rule out all eight from the picture, which is not possible."
- Lines 391: the filled comparison record for District A ("Seven of the ten lines change what the number means"). The single best teaching device in the chapter.
- Also strong: line 158 (funnel reconciles "Always rank bars by value" with "it is inappropriate to rank"); lines 176–180 (same month last year); line 346 (the "beats" sentence failing its own test).
